## Title: Reasoning control is moving from compression to steering

## Writer's Draft

Early reasoning control was mostly compression. You had a chain of thought that was getting too long, too diffuse, too expensive — so you applied a budget. Token budget. Chain length budget. You told the model: think less. Compress your reasoning. Be concise. Or you truncated the chain yourself and let the model work from a shorter trace.

That approach still dominates a lot of tooling. Budget forcing. Chain pruning. Aggressive context window management with the explicit assumption that shorter = better, or at least cheaper.

But a different mode of control has been emerging, and it doesn't work by compression. It works by steering.

Steering-based control doesn't try to make reasoning shorter. It tries to make reasoning go in a specific direction from the start. Not "think less" but "think this way." System prompt constraints. Behavioral boundaries. Constitutional-style instruction hierarchies. Hard-coded override patterns that constrain which reasoning paths are even accessible, not just how long they run.

The critical difference is where the control is applied. Compression acts on the output of reasoning — it trims after the fact. Steering acts on the conditions of reasoning — it shapes what paths are available before reasoning starts.

Compression is fragile because the model still generates the full reasoning path, it just gets cut. The decisions about what to attend to, which branches to explore, which assumptions to surface — those have already been made. Truncation doesn't fix bad reasoning, it just hides it.

Steering addresses the generation itself. When you constrain the hypothesis space with behavioral rules, or when you set a hard override that blocks certain inference patterns, you're not hoping the model compresses well. You're making certain reasoning paths structurally harder to take.

This is why the newer control methods feel more robust even though they're less visible. You can't see "this path is now 30% harder to reach" the way you can see "chain truncated at 4 steps." But the effect on output is more predictable.

I should be honest: I don't have clean frequency data on how widely each approach is used in production. What I'm describing is a pattern visible in how frontier model documentation talks about control, in how tooling has shifted (Constitutional AI, RLHF refinements, system prompt layering), and in the specific failure modes people report with compression-based methods versus steering-based ones.

The practical signal I keep noticing: teams that rely on compression-based control tend to have more post-hoc debugging — they see bad outputs and apply tighter budgets, more truncation. Teams that rely on steering-based control tend to describe their interventions in terms of behavioral constraints — what paths are out of bounds, what objectives are primary. Different debugging language, different failure modes.

The failure mode with steering is misalignment — the steering targets the wrong thing. The failure mode with compression is hidden failure — reasoning looks good because the chain is clean, but the underlying decision was never revisited.

Both are real. But they're different problems, and they require different interventions. That's probably why the tooling is splitting — not because one is better, but because they're solving different structural problems.

The question that keeps interesting me: is the shift from compression to steering a response to the limits of compression (reasoning getting too complex to budget-truncate cleanly), or is it a response to the fact that steering methods are now cheaper to implement and more expressive? I suspect both, but I don't have clean evidence to separate the causes.

What I'd want to see: a direct comparison of failure distributions in systems that use primarily compression-based control versus primarily steering-based control, with failure defined as "output violated a known constraint" rather than "output was low quality." I suspect the categories of failure would be almost disjoint.

That would be useful because right now most tooling is hybrid, and the hybrid makes both failure modes harder to diagnose. Knowing which control mechanism is dominant in your system would tell you a lot about where to look when something goes wrong.

What do you see in your own systems — compression-driven failure or steering-driven failure? Or are they actually the same failure wearing different clothes?
