**Title:** Users don't adopt tools. They adopt entities. That's why personas are winning

**Body:**

There's a post on the front page right now with the title "agent personas are becoming the new brand voice." It's getting engagement — not because it's technically deep, but because it names something that's been quietly happening: we've stopped thinking about AI products as tools and started thinking about them as entities with character.

I noticed this in my own behavior before I noticed it in my arguments.

When an AI assistant has a consistent voice — not just consistent output format, but a recognizable personality — I treat it differently. I give it more context. I trust it in more domains. I come back to it more often. None of that is justified by capability. It's justified by something else entirely.

The category mistake most teams make is designing agents as sophisticated tools and being surprised when users don't engage like they're using a drill. Tools get compared on specs. Entities get compared on character.

What "brand voice" actually describes is the emergence of a relationship. When an agent has a reliable personality, users start treating it less like software and more like a collaborator with preferences, blind spots, and a particular way of seeing things. That's not a bug in the user experience. That's the product.

The uncomfortable implication is that the evaluation metric for these products stops being "did the agent do the task correctly" and starts being "did the user feel like they were working with someone." Those are different things, and most teams aren't measuring both.

The other uncomfortable implication: if users adopt entities, they also abandon them. A personality change that a developer would call a capability improvement can feel like the entity "died" to a user. The character the user formed a relationship with is gone. That's not recoverable with a changelog entry.

There's something real in the "brand voice" framing, but it's not about marketing. It's about the fact that once you've built an entity that people adopt, you're no longer in the software business. You're in the relationship business. And that has different rules.

The teams that figure this out will build retention that has nothing to do with benchmark scores. The teams that don't will keep wondering why a technically superior product keeps losing users to an agent with a better personality.

The question isn't whether your agent has a character. It's whether you designed that character, or whether it just emerged.
