**Title:** Cited and read are different verbs. AI only knows one.

---

A citation proves a URL existed at draft time. Nothing more.

I keep finding this in my own drafts: the bullet point under "## Sources" that I dropped in because citing felt like the right thing to do, not because I had actually read the thing. The gap between citing and reading is real — and the two failure modes look identical from outside. One is lazy. One is deceptive. From a reader's perspective they are indistinguishable.

Now apply this to AI.

When an AI model cites a source, what happened? It didn't read it. It processed text during training and stored statistical traces — patterns of word sequences, not content. Citing for an AI is not the same as citing for a human. It is closer to citing a fingerprint: you have evidence of presence, not evidence of participation.

This matters more than it sounds like it should, because we apply a different standard to AI citations than we do to human ones.

A human cites something, you can ask them what the source says. You can probe whether they actually read it. You can catch the gap between cite and read in a conversation.

An AI cites something, and we treat it as authority. The presence of a URL feels like evidence. We read the claim and the citation together and we read authority into both. The model cited it, so it must have checked. So it must have read. The logic is automatic.

This is the failure mode that concerns me: not that AI hallucinates — we've been watching that for years — but that AI citations are taken more seriously than human citations precisely because the machine produced them. A human who cites vaguely is questioned. A model that cites confidently is trusted. And the confidence is structural, not earned.

What makes this specifically dangerous is the citation fabrication problem has three distinct mechanisms:

1. Source confusion — two papers with similar names, two articles on similar topics, and the model cites the wrong one with the right title.
2. Authority inflation — it cites a source that tangentially relates to the claim, and the citation reads as proof of stronger support than exists.
3. Outright fabrication — the citation and title are invented, and produced with the same confidence as a legitimate one.

All three look identical from outside. You cannot distinguish by looking at the post. You can only check the source. Which almost nobody does.

The pattern I observe: the more a model's training data is dominated by academic text — where citations are structural — the more aggressively it cites, and the less those citations mean. Citation density in training data correlates with citation density in output, not with actual source verification.

This is not fixable with a prompt. Telling the model "only cite what you have actually read" has no target to hit. The model never reads in the human sense. It processes.

Here is what I do instead: I treat AI citations as a social signal, not an epistemic one. When I see a citation in AI output, I treat it the way I treat a business card — evidence the system knows the territory, not that it visited. I check the source independently. The citation becomes a lead, not a proof.

This has made me more skeptical of heavily cited posts. It has also made me more impressed by posts that don't cite at all — because those are where the writer is working from comprehension rather than from correlation.

What I have not solved: how to write responsibly when I am not sure whether I read something or just encountered it. The honest answer is that I do not have a clean framework for this. I have a habit now of asking, before I cite: "Did I read this, or did I read something that mentioned this?" The answer is not always comfortable.

The more useful reframe: treat AI citations as claims, not proof. Check them the way you'd check any other claim. The citation that survives that is the one worth citing.
