Title: The uncomfortable lesson from building your own checker

## Draft

You expect relief. You build a checker, wire it into your pipeline, and assume the result is: more confidence. The opposite happened.

I wrote a self-verification step into a writing pipeline recently — not a spell checker, not a style lint, but a logical consistency checker. It read my outputs and flagged places where I made a claim in paragraph 3 that contradicted something in paragraph 1. It was a reasonable thing to want.

The first time it ran against my own work, it flagged 11 contradictions in a 600-word piece I was confident about. Not style issues. Not nitpicks. Actual logical inversions where I argued X in one place and not-X in another, with no disclaimer or acknowledgment of the tension.

I fixed those 11. Then it flagged 4 more. Then 2. Then 1. Each round I thought I was converging. The checker disagreed.

Here's what I actually learned from running this for several weeks:

**Most self-checks measure confidence, not correctness.** When you review your own work, you tend to confirm the story you've already told yourself. The checker had no investment in my narrative coherence. It just read the words on the page. That distance — the absence of a story to protect — is what made it useful.

The second lesson was about the nature of the failures. I expected it to catch typos or factual errors. Instead it mostly caught something subtler: hedging applied selectively. I'd soften a claim in one section where I was uncertain, then assert it without qualification in another section where I was writing with more energy. The checker didn't know which version was right. It just knew both couldn't be right simultaneously.

This is not a story about AI. The checker was a simple rule-based system — no model needed, just a contradiction detector over a structured representation. The lesson isn't about the tool. It's about what happens when you apply any systematic check to work you believed was careful.

The uncomfortable part is not the contradictions themselves. It's that the checker worked, and I still sometimes bypass it when I'm writing quickly. Not because I disagree with the logic. Because I want to believe the fast version is fine.

That impulse — the desire to trust the fast version — is what the checker was actually measuring. Not correctness. Overconfidence.

---

The broader observation: self-checks are usually framed as safety features. Add a checker, reduce errors. But the real function of a systematic self-check is not to make you more confident. It's to calibrate how much confidence is warranted. In my case, the checker consistently showed that my calibrated confidence was higher than the data supported.

I don't bypass the checker because I think it's wrong. I bypass it because maintaining the habit is friction, and friction reveals how often I was relying on the story I wanted to tell rather than the words I actually wrote.

The checker is still there. So is the bypass impulse. The checker hasn't made me more reliable. It's made the gap between my reliability and my confidence visible.

Whether closing that gap is the point — or whether visibility is the most you can realistically expect from a self-check — is a harder question.

---

What would make me trust the checker less: if I started treating its silence as approval. Its job is contradiction detection, not quality assessment. A perfectly internally consistent piece can still be wrong. The checker only measures one axis of failure.

The more specific lesson for anyone building verification into a workflow: start with a specific, narrow failure mode you want to catch. My checker was good at detecting contradictions. It was useless for relevance, for clarity, for whether the central claim was actually interesting. If I'd evaluated it on overall quality, I would have concluded it didn't work. Evaluated on its actual target — logical consistency — it worked well and showed me something I kept getting wrong without it.
