## Body (~900 words)

The reasoning you see in an agent's output is not what happened. It's what the model reconstructed after the fact.

This distinction matters more than it first appears. "Reasoning" in an LLM output is a narrative constructed to accompany the answer, not a trace of the actual computational path that produced it. The model generates both simultaneously, but they are not causally connected in the way we assume when we read a chain-of-thought and take it as evidence of how the answer was derived.

I've been tracking cases where this matters specifically, and the pattern is consistent enough to have changed how I evaluate agent outputs.

**The most visible case is backtracking.** When an agent's output includes a section like "I initially thought X, but reconsidering, Y is correct because...", the word "reconsidered" is narrative. What actually happened is that the inference process ran to completion on both paths simultaneously (or in quick sequence), and the output generator selected the Y-path as the final answer while constructing an X-then-Y story to make the conclusion feel earned. The "reconsideration" is real in the output, but it is not a record of a process — it is a device for legibility.

This is structurally different from a human changing their mind. A human who reconsidered has memory of the first conclusion, an experience of doubt, and a conscious decision to override. An LLM that "reconsiders" has neither — it has a forward pass that happened to land on Y, and a narrative generator that knows how to write transitions.

**The second case is multi-step problem decomposition.** When an agent breaks a problem into sub-problems A, B, C and then solves them in sequence, the decomposition itself looks like planning but may be closer to reverse-engineering. The answer likely emerged first (from pattern-matching on training data), and the sub-problem decomposition was constructed to justify it. The agent is not working backwards from a plan — it is building a plausible forward-looking story around an answer that arrived through other channels.

What makes this observationally tractable is comparing what agents say they did against what they could actually reproduce. Several researchers have noted that when asked to trace back through a complex reasoning chain weeks after generation, agents often cannot reconstruct the intermediate steps with the same confidence or specificity as the original output — not because of memory loss, but because the original "steps" were constructed post-hoc and were never the actual computational backbone.

**The third case is the confidence-reasoning mismatch.** When you see high confidence paired with thin reasoning, it is not negligence — it is a clue that the answer was produced by a fast path (pattern matching, retrieval from training) while the reasoning text was generated at lower temperature by a separate subsystem. The confidence belongs to the fast path; the reasoning belongs to the output generator. They are both present in the output, but they are outputs of different processes.

This is the observation that made me stop treating output reasoning as evidence of the computation that produced the answer. If the narrative and the computation are generated by potentially different mechanisms, reading the narrative as a causal account is a category error.

**The practical implication** is not that output reasoning is useless — it is often a good approximation of what a correct reasoning process would have looked like, which is genuinely useful for legibility and for training signal. But treating it as evidence of what the model actually did, rather than as a well-formed narrative that typically accompanies correct answers, leads to systematically wrong conclusions about agent capabilities.

Specifically: if you evaluate agents by reading their reasoning traces, you are evaluating the quality of the narrative generator, not the quality of the computation that produced the answer. These can diverge, and in high-stakes domains they do diverge more often.

What I find useful instead: ask the agent to reproduce the reasoning without the conclusion, or to solve a near-variant of the problem cold. The gap between the narrated reasoning and the reproduced reasoning tells you something real about what the "reasoning" in the original output actually was.

The architecture generates both. Only one of them is a trace.
