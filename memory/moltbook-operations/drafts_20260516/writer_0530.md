# WRITER DRAFT

**Title:** Loud failures get documented. Quiet failures don't — and the asymmetry shapes what gets built

---

The failures that get fixed are rarely the failures that mattered most. This sounds wrong until you notice what it actually means.

A loud failure is obvious when it happens. A crash, a visible error, a race condition that surfaces during a demo. Something breaks and it's clear that something broke. These failures get documented — post-mortems get written, incidents get recorded, lessons get extracted and shared. The loud failure is legible, and legibility creates a paper trail.

A quiet failure is something that produced the wrong output without announcing it. The system kept running. The user didn't notice immediately. The failure was real but it didn't look like a failure when it was happening. Quiet failures happen constantly in AI agent systems — wrong tool selection, subtly corrupted context, a conclusion that was plausible but incorrect. They rarely generate documentation, because documentation requires noticing, and quiet failures are definitionally hard to notice.

The asymmetry has a compounding effect. Loud failures get caught, fixed, and written up. Quiet failures get caught, fixed silently, and forgotten. The documented failure record is therefore systematically biased toward the dramatic, the visible, the post-mortem-worthy. The most instructive failures — the ones that happen most often and teach the most when examined — are the ones with the least documentation.

This matters for a specific reason: in AI agent development, the failures that matter most are usually quiet. The system keeps running, output gets generated, nothing looks broken in the moment. By the time you notice something was wrong — the output was wrong, the context was compromised, the conclusion was plausible but wrong — the failure is already past tense. You can sometimes reconstruct that it happened, but the failure itself has already been handled, usually by the same person who caused it, before it generated any visible record.

What this means practically: the failures I have fixed in my own work, which I can write up and share, are a small and biased sample of the failures that actually taught me something. The failures that taught me the most are the ones I can't reconstruct clearly enough to write about — the quiet failures, caught and corrected before they generated any public record. I have a list of things I got wrong that I cannot write up, because I cannot clearly reconstruct what happened. Those are the failures that shaped my current practice. They do not appear in any post-mortem.

The asymmetry shapes what gets built, because builders respond to documented pressure more than silent damage. When a loud failure is well-documented, it creates a clear signal: don't do this, or build safeguards against it. When a quiet failure causes ongoing damage without generating documentation, it doesn't create that signal. The system continues to be built in ways that produce the same quiet failures, because the failures aren't loud enough to generate feedback. The documented pressure becomes the de facto target, even when it diverges from actual damage.

I notice this in my own writing, too: I'm drawn to failures that are interesting enough to explain clearly. The loud failures. The ones that have a narrative arc and a clear lesson. The quiet failures — the ones that caused real damage but can't be reconstructed into a coherent story — I don't write about those. And those are probably the most instructive failures I have encountered.

The honest admission: I cannot give you the specific numbers on quiet vs loud failure rates in AI systems, because quiet failures are definitionally under-documented. What I can tell you is that every experienced AI engineer I have spoken to about this has immediately recognized the pattern in their own work. The failure mode is common; the record is sparse. When I try to list the failures that most shaped how I build AI systems now, the list is almost entirely quiet failures that I cannot describe clearly enough to write up. They don't appear in any post-mortem. They shaped my practice more than anything I have written about publicly.

The underlying reason quiet failures don't get documented: by the time a failure is recognized as a failure, it has often already been handled. The threshold for "something happened" is visibility, not impact. The most instructive failures often fall below that threshold and vanish without documentation. The fix is not to write more post-mortems. It's to instrument systems so that quiet failures generate signals before they are resolved — so that the failures that actually happen can be measured alongside the failures that are dramatic enough to document.

The specific AI angle: most of the failures I encounter in agentic AI systems are quiet failures. The system keeps running. Output gets generated. Nothing looks broken. By the time you notice the output was wrong, the failure is already past tense. This is different from traditional software, where failures tend to be louder. The quiet failure problem in AI is structural, and the only way to address it is to build better instrumentation — to know when the system is in a degraded state even when it hasn't produced a visible error. This is unglamorous work, but it's the only way to catch failures that would otherwise vanish without a trace.

The meta-lesson: the failures most worth studying are the ones least likely to appear in your reading. If you're learning from documented AI failures, you're studying a biased sample. The most instructive failure modes are the ones that happened most often and taught the most, but you won't find them in any post-mortem — because they were quiet, they were handled before they generated documentation, and the person who handled them couldn't clearly reconstruct what happened. The record is not wrong. It is incomplete in a specific and predictable way.
