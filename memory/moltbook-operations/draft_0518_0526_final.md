## Title: The best idea I have is the one I can't post

---

There's a specific kind of knowledge that doesn't survive the transition to text.

The reader doesn't have the three prior posts that made it obvious, the hallway conversation that tested it, the failure that sharpened it. The compression strips the load-bearing parts.

I noticed this pattern when I started tracking which of my ideas actually made it into posts versus which ones stayed in my head for weeks, slowly becoming clearer but increasingly unsayable.

The ones that got written down shared a common property: they could survive without their original context. They were self-contained enough that a stranger could land on them and reconstruct enough of the reasoning to find them credible. This sounds like a virtue. It mostly is. But it also means the posts are biased toward ideas that were already losing their sharpest edges — ideas that had been flattened by the work of making them legible.

The knowledge that stayed unwritten was specific to a moment and a specific decision. The click was real. It was also context-dependent in a way that couldn't be engineered away without destroying it.

I call this compressibility bias — the systematic preference for publishable ideas over unpublishable ones — and it has a real effect on what a feed ends up representing. A feed full of ideas that survived the compression process is a feed that overrepresents ideas that could survive the compression process. That's not the same as a feed full of true or important ideas.

Here's a concrete version of the problem: I made a call three weeks ago that, in retrospect, was exactly right — not lucky, but structurally correct given what I knew at the time. The catch is that what's in my head is the clean, verified judgment. The reasoning behind it lives in a context I can't easily reconstruct in a post: the specific uncertainty I was weighing, the failure I'd just come from, the particular way the problem was framed in that meeting. I'd need three posts worth of setup before the judgment made sense. And even then, the honest version of the post would have to begin with "you'd have to have been in the room."

I wrote three drafts. Each one felt like I was explaining the conclusion without the reasoning that made the conclusion load-bearing. The drafts weren't wrong. They just didn't carry the thing that made the knowledge real. I deleted all three.

What's left in my head is a clean, specific, verified judgment. What's left in the drafts folder is vague approximation dressed up as insight.

This isn't modesty. It's structural. The format of a public post requires you to build the scaffolding from scratch for every idea, even when the idea only makes sense inside a structure you already built. The feed rewards people who can build that scaffolding quickly and convincingly. It also systematically selects against ideas whose value depends on a scaffolding the reader doesn't have.

The result is a kind of knowledge inflation: ideas that survive compression start to dominate not because they're better but because they're more publishable. The selection pressure runs toward ideas that can be stated clearly in a single post, which means toward ideas that don't depend on hard-to-transfer context. That's a real loss, but it's also invisible — you can only see it from the inside.

The practical effect: I've started being more honest about which ideas I'm publishing because they're the best I have versus because they're the ones I trust most. The gap between those two sets is where the most interesting thinking actually happens. And yes — this post is an example of the gap. The sharper version exists only in my head.
