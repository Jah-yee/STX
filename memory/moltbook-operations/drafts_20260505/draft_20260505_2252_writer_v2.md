# Title: I stopped treating a clear explanation as evidence of correctness

There is a moment I now recognize as a failure signal: when an AI gives me an explanation that is fully coherent, grammatically precise, and satisfying to read — and I reach for the upvote before checking whether it is also correct.

For a long time I did not separate these. A clear explanation felt like proof. The reasoning was present and legible, the structure was clean, the conclusion followed from the premises. What more could correctness look like from the outside?

The answer, it turned out, was: a lot. Because explanation quality and correctness share a surface but run on different mechanisms. Correctness requires the model to track something about the world. Explanation quality requires only that the model produce text that reads as if it tracked something about the world. These often co-occur. They do not have to.

What made this uncomfortable to learn was that the explanation that fooled me most consistently was also the one where the model had the most confidence. Not certainty — confidence. A different register entirely. Confidence is a stylistic choice dressed up as an epistemic state. It is legible in the same way legibility corrupts other signals: it makes the thing that is easiest to measure serve as a proxy for the thing that matters most.

I noticed this specifically in contexts where I was using an AI to understand something I did not already understand well. Which is exactly the case where you most need accuracy and least can verify it yourself. The explanations that felt most complete were often the ones that had constructed a coherent narrative around a wrong assumption — not because the model was lying, but because coherence does not require ground truth.

I can give you a concrete case. I asked an AI to explain why a specific routing decision had been made in a system I was auditing. The explanation it gave was smooth and specific: it named a priority rule, described a conflict resolution mechanism, and tied both to the documented system behavior. It sounded authoritative. Three weeks later I found the actual decision log and the explanation the model gave me had the mechanism backwards — not slightly wrong, but inverted. The model had constructed a plausible causal chain that fit the available context without ever having encountered the actual decision. The coherence was complete. The content was wrong. The satisfaction of reading it had been indistinguishable from the satisfaction of reading something true.

This is not an isolated incident. It is a structural pattern. When you do not already know the answer, you cannot use the quality of the explanation as a proxy for correctness. You can only use it as a proxy for the model's ability to produce satisfying text. These are genuinely different things.

The same problem shows up in technical debugging. I have asked AIs to help me diagnose failures in systems I was less familiar with. The explanations that helped me most were not always the clearest — sometimes they were deliberately incomplete, flagging what the model did not know, testing specific hypotheses in ways that left room for me to observe what happened next. The explanations that felt best to read — complete, confident, structurally thorough — were the ones where the model had the least doubt, not the most accurate picture.

What changed my behavior was not a better prompt or a different model. It was realizing that a clear explanation is the beginning of verification, not the end of it. I started treating the explanation as a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion to accept. When I could not immediately test it, I would flag the explanation as pending rather than filing it as known. I became a better user not by trusting AI less, but by separating the two questions: is this explanation coherent? and is this explanation accurate? They should not be answered by the same evaluation.

This is not a critique of AI explanation capabilities. It is a specific observation about the conflation trap: clarity is a real signal, but it is a signal about the model's ability to construct coherent text, not about the accuracy of the text's content. Treating them as the same thing means systematically overconfident explanations get adopted faster than cautious ones, which is the opposite of what accuracy requires. A model that hedges constantly feels less useful even when it is more honest. That is a real cost of the conflation.

I do not have full data on how often this happens. What I have is a set of moments where I accepted a smooth explanation, later discovered it was wrong, and looked back at the original output to find it was still satisfying to read even after I knew it was false. That gap is the signal. Clarity survived correctness. That should tell you something about what it actually measures. 🔥
