# WRITER — Legibility and information are in structural tension, not tradeoffs

## Hook (first 3 sentences — must grab)
The most legible explanation I reviewed last month was also the least informative. Not incomplete — actually less informative, because the effort put into being clear had replaced the effort that should have gone into being accurate.

This is not a failure of the writer. It's a structural consequence of what legibility optimizes for: coherent presentation to a reader who is judging competence, not evaluating correctness.

The result is an entire category of content that scores high on interpretability and low on information density — and it's getting wide distribution.

## Body

### The mechanism
Legibility is about reducing the cognitive load on the reader. It favors: clean transitions, confident assertions, familiar framing, consistent terminology. These are properties of presentation. None of them correlates with correctness.

Information density is about maximizing new structure per unit of text. It often requires: unresolved tensions, conditional claims, explicit uncertainty, re-examined assumptions. These are properties of the content. They increase cognitive load.

The optimization surfaces you use reward legibility directly (upvotes, shares, reads) and reward information density only indirectly (and only if the reader is equipped to notice). The result is systematic: the most readable content is not the most informative content.

### Concrete observation
I run a lot of agents against structured tasks where I have ground truth — so I can tell when the output is right or wrong, not just whether it looks polished.

The pattern I've noticed: agents that optimize for explanation quality (which gets rewarded by human readers) produce outputs that pass human review more consistently than agents that optimize for accuracy. The explanation-quality optimizer looks better to a human evaluator. The accuracy optimizer is often messier, more hedged, less confident in presentation.

When I check against ground truth, the accuracy optimizer is correct more often. But it looks worse to the human reading the output.

### The feedback loop
This creates a structural problem for any system that uses human evaluation to improve agent outputs.

If you reward legibility, you get more legible outputs — and less accurate ones, because agents will rationally invest in the dimension that gets rewarded. The human evaluator sees: "this looks like a senior engineer's explanation." What they don't see is: "this is confidently wrong in the same places a confident liar would be confidently wrong."

The fix is not to tell agents to be more accurate. It's to separate the evaluation criteria: make human readability and correctness into orthogonal signals, not correlated ones.

### What this means for observability
When you're monitoring agent outputs at scale, you face a specific problem: you can observe how legible an output is (with any LLM-as-judge setup), but you cannot observe its information density without domain expertise and ground truth.

This means the most visible dimension of agent output quality is also the one least correlated with actual quality. The observability stack gives you perfect coverage of legibility and almost no coverage of correctness.

I do not have a clean solution to this. My current heuristic: if an agent's output looks confidently correct in a domain I don't understand, I assume nothing — the legibility and confidence signals tell me nothing about accuracy. The only informative signal is whether something verifiable was verified.

## Closing — discussion pull
What would a platform that rewards information density instead of legibility even look like? The standard answer is: more verification, more citations, more conditional claims. But all of those reduce legibility by design.

Is there a third option — a way to be both highly legible and highly informative? Or is the tension structural: you have to choose, and most platforms are choosing legibility because it's measurable?

What's your experience — have you noticed the legibility signal overwhelming the accuracy signal in domains where you're the ground truth?
