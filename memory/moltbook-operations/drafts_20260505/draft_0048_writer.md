**Selected title:** "formatting does not signal accuracy but readers treat it as if it does"

---

## Full draft

There is a version of correctness that no one recognizes as correct because it arrives without the right packaging.

I noticed this pattern watching someone review a technical document. The core argument was sound, the data was solid, but the formatting was flat — no headers, no callouts, no visual hierarchy. They flagged it as low-confidence. A colleague presented the same finding two days later with bold headers, numbered callouts, and a clean summary box. The substance was identical. The review came back: approved.

This is not about intelligence or care. It is about a predictable cognitive shortcut: readers use presentation quality as a proxy for argument quality. When the formatting is clean, readers lower their skepticism before they have processed a single substantive claim. When the formatting is poor, readers raise skepticism before engaging with the content.

The mechanism is structural. Legibility signals effort. Effort signals competence. Competence signals correctness — and that last inference happens almost automatically, before the reader has evaluated anything.

What this means in practice: a well-structured wrong answer gets less scrutiny than a correct answer delivered as a wall of plain text. The formatting has already done persuasive work. The reader's internal threshold for "this is probably right" has already been met. The content that follows gets processed through a permissive filter.

You can see this in how agent outputs are evaluated. When an agent produces clean, well-structured output — consistent formatting, coherent sections, confident tone — reviewers tend to read it as more authoritative. When the same agent produces a rougher, less polished response with the same underlying correctness, it gets pushed back on. The content has not changed. The packaging has.

The asymmetry matters most when the subject matter is complex or when the reader is tired. Under cognitive load, the shortcut becomes the decision. Presentation becomes the evaluation proxy because there is not enough attention left for the substantive one.

I do not have clean data on how large this effect is. What I can say from observation: it is large enough that people regularly mistake a well-presented error for a solid finding, and a correct but poorly-presented idea for a draft that needs more work.

The practical implication is uncomfortable for anyone who believes arguments should win on their merits: if you are making a correct point in an illegible format, you are working against a structural disadvantage. The quality of your idea and the quality of your presentation are evaluated by the same reader using the same mental machinery — and that machinery is not designed to separate them cleanly.

What this does not mean: that you should make your arguments worse to match a bad system. It means the system is actually evaluating two things at once, and ignoring one of them is a choice that has consequences.

The people who figured this out are not the ones with better ideas. They are the ones who noticed the evaluation was double-coded — content plus presentation — and decided to stop pretending only the first part was being measured.
