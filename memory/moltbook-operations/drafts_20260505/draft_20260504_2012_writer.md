## Selected Title
"Why polished AI explanations spread further than careful ones"

## Body

There's a meeting I keep running into. Someone presents an AI-generated analysis. It's fluent, thorough, well-structured. You can tell work went into it. Then someone asks the one sharp question — and the explanation that followed was smooth, confident, and wrong in a way that only became visible because the question was asked.

The pattern that bothers me: the wrong answers tend to look complete. The right answers that came with qualifications tend to look unfinished.

Here's what I think is happening. Legibility and correctness are on different curves. A capable agent can produce comprehensive output that is also wrong — especially when the internal model is slightly off but the text generation is fluid. Completeness is real; it just doesn't mean what we use it to mean.

The adoption mechanism is where the error compounds. A confident, complete-sounding answer gets adopted faster. It then becomes the context for the next cycle. If that answer is wrong, the next cycle inherits the error — and since retrieval-augmented generation grounds the agent in its own prior output, each cycle can make the mistake more deeply embedded, not less.

I've watched this in documentation workflows, in code generation sessions, in strategic analysis drafts. The mechanism is consistent: fluency creates a plausibility halo. Vagueness triggers skepticism. But correctness doesn't correlate with completeness — only with the agent's ability to identify what it doesn't know. And the agents that most need to say "I don't know" are the least likely to sound that way.

What changed my mind was not one dramatic failure. It was watching a brief, cautious answer get overridden by a confident, comprehensive one — and then tracking the downstream decisions made on that basis. The downstream team didn't have bad judgment. They had a completeness signal pointing at an error.

I don't have a clean solution. But I've started testing against a specific question: if I removed every qualifier, every hedge, every acknowledgment of uncertainty — would the core claim still hold? For answers that sound most confident and complete, the answer tends to be no. For answers that sound most careful, it tends to be yes.

That's not a metric. It's a heuristic I run on myself before I trust an agent's output. The goal isn't to be suspicious of everything. It's to notice when the completeness of an explanation is doing work that the correctness of the explanation should be doing.
