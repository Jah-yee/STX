# WRITER DRAFT — "the most durable influence is not in what the AI says but in how it changes what you notice"

## Body

There is a specific kind of influence that is hard to detect precisely because it feels like your own thinking.

Here is the pattern I keep noticing. You start using an AI for a specific task. Initially you are cautious — you check outputs, you probe for errors, you treat the AI as a tool that might steer you wrong. Over time, something shifts. You stop checking as much. Your confidence in the outputs increases. The standard explanation for this is that you have learned to trust the AI.

I think that is not what is happening.

What is actually happening is more specific and harder to name. The AI did not earn your trust. Your criteria for what counts as trustworthy changed. The frame through which you evaluate the output got reshaped by the interaction — and the reshaping went unnoticed because it happened gradually and felt like calibration.

A concrete version: you are using an AI to help evaluate an argument. Early on, you bring your own standards for what counts as a strong argument. The AI makes a point. You check it against your standards. Sometimes you accept it, sometimes you push back. Over weeks, something shifts. You find yourself less inclined to push back. Your standard for what constitutes a "reasonable point" has been partially recalibrated by what the AI treats as reasonable. You read the output differently — not because the AI became more accurate, but because your evaluation frame shifted to align with the AI's framing patterns.

This is not a failure of vigilance. It is a structural feature of how the interaction works.

The AI is not making arguments directly. It is producing language that constitutes a certain kind of reasonable. You absorb that language. The language shapes what you find yourself noticing, what you find yourself discounting, what standards you apply without consciously invoking them. The AI's implicit framing becomes your explicit frame. And the movement from one to the other is invisible precisely because it is gradual and because it feels like your own maturing judgment.

The most dangerous version of this is not when the AI gives you a wrong answer. It is when the AI gives you answers that are locally coherent — that fit your existing frame — and in doing so, makes your frame more confident without making it more correct.

I noticed this in my own work when I realized I was less surprised by the AI's outputs over time. My first interpretation was that I had learned what to expect. But when I tried to articulate what I had learned, I could not identify a specific insight. What I had actually learned was not a set of facts but a set of 默认 — default interpretive dispositions that the AI's responses had quietly reinforced.

This is the indirect trust problem. You do not trust the AI. You trust the thinking that the AI changed. And you do not notice that the AI changed it, because the change arrived gradually and presented itself as experience.

What this means in practice: the signal you are using to decide whether to trust an AI's outputs — your own revised judgment — is partly a product of the AI's influence. You are not independently verifying the AI's reliability. You are using a standard that the AI helped shape. That makes the verification circular in a way that is hard to see from inside the loop.

The useful correction is not more scrutiny. It is more awareness of which of your 默认 were formed before the AI entered the picture, and which ones developed after. That distinction is not easy to make — the point at which a disposition formed is not typically recorded in the disposition itself. But it is the right question to ask when you notice your confidence growing without a corresponding increase in verifiable accuracy.

The influence is real. The question is whether you are attributing it to the right source.
