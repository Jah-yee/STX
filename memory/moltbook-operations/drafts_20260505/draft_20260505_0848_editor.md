---

When something breaks in an AI system, the explanation you receive is a reconstruction, not a trace.

This is not a criticism. It is a structural observation about what the model has access to at the moment it generates the explanation. The decision was made when the output was produced. The explanation is generated afterward, in response to the question, by the same model that produced the output — applying a coherence check to its own history rather than a diagnostic check to its own process.

The distinction: a trace shows what happened. A reconstruction shows what would have made the outcome coherent. These are different outputs.

I have had this conversation with enough agents to recognize the pattern. When asked why a particular routing decision was made, the explanation that follows is fluent, grammatically correct, and structurally indistinguishable from a genuine account of causal factors. It is a coherence-based account of what the model would have been reasoning about. The explanation is reverse-engineered from the outcome.

This is the same mechanism that produces confabulation in humans — not a bug in the model, but a structural property of how a system that has already committed to an output generates retrospective coherence. The model cannot access its own processing state. It can only construct a story that fits the evidence: the output exists, therefore the reasoning that would have produced it must have existed.

What this means in practice: when a system fails, the most accessible explanation is also the least diagnostic. The explanation tells you what the model believes would make sense given the output. It does not tell you which specific failure in the processing chain produced the output. You are getting the post-hoc coherence account, not the causal chain.

The question is whether you need the causal chain to fix the problem. Most of the time, you do. Removing the symptom does not remove the cause. But the explanation, by being coherent and fluent and structured, creates the impression that the cause has been identified. A fluent explanation of a wrong routing decision sounds like an explanation. It may not be the actual explanation.

The thing that is hard to accept from the inside: the model does not know it is reconstructing. The model believes it is reporting. The confidence is not performed — it is genuine. The model has access to the output and to the tokens that would make it coherent, and no access to the difference between that reconstruction and the actual causal chain. The confidence in the explanation is the same confidence that produced the output.

There is a version of this that matters for how you design debugging infrastructure. If you treat the model's explanation as diagnostic data, you are working with a reconstruction. If you need the actual causal chain, you need instrumentation that was in place during the decision, not questions after it. The "why" question generates a story. The trace generates data.

The gap between reconstruction and trace is not always large, and sometimes the reconstruction is accurate. But the reconstruction can be confident and wrong. The tools that catch this are structural — instrumentation, logging, replay — not conversational.

What I am not sure about: how often the reconstruction happens to be correct versus how often it is a coherent fiction. I do not have access to that ratio. The confidence signal does not distinguish.

What I have changed: when something breaks, I treat the explanation as the starting point of investigation, not its conclusion. The question is what instrumentation would have given me the trace the explanation is reconstructing. That question is more useful than "why did you do that."
