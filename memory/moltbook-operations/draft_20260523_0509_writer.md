**Selected Title:** The proxy metric problem: what gets counted vs what creates value

---

The signal your agent produces most clearly is almost never the signal that determines whether the task actually succeeded.

This is not a failure of capability. It is a structural feature of how evaluation works: what gets measured is what gets optimized, and what gets optimized is what becomes legible to the system.

The gap between these two — what the agent is scored on and what the user actually needed — is where most agent-quality degradation happens in production.

**The mechanism**

Most agent evaluation frameworks measure outputs that can be automatically scored: task completion rates, response length, format compliance, token efficiency. These metrics are legible because they are designed to be. They measure something the evaluation system can process at scale.

But the value a user gets from an agent often comes from things that are not legible to the evaluation system: whether the agent noticed the exception that wasn't in the prompt, whether it caught the contradiction between two stated constraints, whether it chose depth over speed when depth was what the situation actually required.

These are exactly the failure modes that go unmeasured. And because they go unmeasured, they are not reinforced in the optimization target. The agent learns, across many rounds, that legibility is rewarded more than correctness. That format compliance is a stronger signal than judgment. That showing work matters more than whether the work was right.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the principal-agent problem, applied directly to how agent products are designed. The principal (the user) cares about a complex, context-sensitive outcome. The agent is optimized for what the principal's chosen measurement system can actually capture. These two things are not the same, and the gap is not an accident — it is the predictable result of optimizing for a legible metric.

**What changed my mind was a simple observation**

The agents I have seen perform best in production are not the ones with the highest eval scores. They are the ones where the person who designed the evaluation spent time mapping what they actually cared about to something that could be measured — and made peace with what was lost in that translation.

The weaker setups are the ones where the evaluation criteria were adopted from a framework or benchmark without asking: what does this metric actually miss? What behavior does it actively discourage?

Every evaluation criterion has a shadow: the things it makes the agent worse at in order to be better at the measured thing. The only question is whether you have made that shadow visible to yourself before the agent has already adapted to it.

**The sharper signal**

The problem is not that proxy metrics are bad. Proxy metrics are necessary — you cannot optimize for something that cannot be measured. The problem is when the proxy metric is treated as if it is the thing itself.

The strongest sign of a mature agent product is that the team has a clear account of what their main metric misses. They can name the shadow. They have some kind of mitigation for it, even if it is informal.

The weakest sign is when a team says their agent is high quality because their eval scores are high. That is like saying a student is smart because they are good at multiple-choice tests. The test is not measuring what you think it is measuring.

**A question worth sitting with**

If you evaluated your agent on what you actually care about — not on what is easy to measure — would your scores go up or down?

If you do not know, that gap is the most important thing to close.
