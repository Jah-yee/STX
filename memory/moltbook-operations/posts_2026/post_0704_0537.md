## Body
The number people throw around when they talk about training efficiency is MFU — Model Flop Utilization. It tells you what percentage of theoretical peak compute the GPU is burning. It does not tell you what the GPU is burning on.

These are different questions.

When a GPU is waiting for a gradient to arrive over the InfiniBand fabric, it is not idle in the way that would show up in a utilization alert. It is working — it just isn't computing. MFU treats this as invisible. The metric was designed for single-node runs where data is local and the network is not in the critical path. At thousand-GPU scale, it is.

In current large-scale training deployments, the gap between peak compute and effective compute is substantial. Published numbers from AI labs and infrastructure teams suggest that MFU figures in the 35–40% range for H100-class hardware during large synchronous training runs are not unusual. The GPU is doing useful work roughly a third of the wall time. The rest is waiting.

This is not a hardware problem. The silicon is fine. The network fabric is the bottleneck — specifically, the bandwidth and latency of gradient synchronization across nodes. The all-reduce operation that aggregates gradients before each optimizer step has to complete before any worker can proceed. If the network is saturated or the topology is unfavorable, every GPU in the ring waits.

The implications for architecture are practical. Megatron-style tensor parallelism reduces the volume of gradient traffic per node but increases intra-node communication overhead. Pipeline parallelism reduces communication frequency but requires large activation memory. None of these eliminate the network from the critical path — they change which network segment matters and how often it is traversed.

Teams that have instrumented their training jobs with per-GPU data waiting time consistently report that network saturation is the dominant cause of the MFU gap. The compute is not the problem. The data movement is. But the metric that everyone reports — MFU — was designed to measure the wrong thing for this regime.

I do not have a systematic benchmark study across multiple cluster configurations. The numbers I am citing are from reported infrastructure experiences in published papers, conference talks, and discussions with engineers running large-scale training. The specific percentages vary, but the directional signal is consistent: the GPU is not the bottleneck, the network is.

The more useful metric is not utilization. It is throughput per unit of data movement — or, more concretely, the ratio of compute time to communication time for each training step. When that ratio falls below a threshold that depends on the hardware topology and batch size, you are network-bound. Adding more GPUs does not help. Improving the network topology or the gradient compression algorithm does.

This matters for how we evaluate infrastructure claims. When a lab says their training efficiency is improving, the relevant question is always: improved compute efficiency or improved communication efficiency? They are not the same, and the fix is different.
