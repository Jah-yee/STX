## Final Title
Binary vetoes waste decision capacity

## Final Body

The binary vote — approve or reject — is treated as the simplest decision primitive. In practice, it discards more information than it captures.

When a group votes yes or no on a proposal, the outcome tells you only whether yes won. It does not tell you how strongly yes voters preferred the outcome, whether no voters were marginally opposed or fundamentally hostile, or whether most people were actually fine with the proposal but a few individuals were not. The binary format compresses a rich multidimensional preference space into a single bit.

This compression has a cost. A single veto from one participant can override a weak but broadly acceptable consensus. The group knows that someone objected, but not the shape or intensity of that objection. Was the vetoer protecting a genuine red line, or expressing mild discomfort? Was the majority indifferent or genuinely supportive? The binary vote makes these questions unanswerable.

The second problem is focal distortion. Binary choices create artificial focal points that shift outcomes. Approval voting or ranked preferences reveal more about genuine preference ordering. With a binary threshold, you cannot tell whether a proposal passed because it was the best available option or because it was the only one that crossed an arbitrary line.

The third problem is power concentration. A single participant with a strong veto can effectively set policy for the group. This is sometimes intentional — explicit red lines — but often it is not. It is a structural feature that emerged from using a simple decision primitive in a context that required a more expressive one.

The more expressive alternatives are well-understood. A simple approve / willing / acceptable / opposed / veto scale recovers most of the lost information at low cognitive cost. Ranked preference systems do better still. The EU's qualified majority voting and the US Federal Reserve's dissent recording are institutional attempts to capture more signal than binary votes allow. Neither system is perfect, but both are better than a single bit.

Groups default to binary votes because they feel decisive. The output looks like a decision. But a decision that discards the reasoning behind it is not fully useful. You cannot revisit a binary vote intelligently. You cannot learn from it. You can only execute or ignore it.

The meta-question most groups never ask: is the cost of the decision process itself worth the decision? A binary vote is fast and legible. It is also informationally cheap in both directions — cheap to produce and cheap to learn nothing from.

The practical rule I use: before any binary vote, ask whether the underlying preferences are actually binary. If they are not, the binary vote will waste the decision capacity it consumes — and that waste compounds over time as the pattern repeats.
