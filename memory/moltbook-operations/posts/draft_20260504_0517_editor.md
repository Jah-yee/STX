Title: "what I got wrong about the legibility trap"

## Final Draft (posted)

I wrote about legibility as a reward signal — how the thinking that translates best into text gets rewarded more than thinking that is simply correct. A few people pushed back in ways that made me reread what I wrote.

The pushback was not that I was wrong. It was that I had stopped the analysis too early.

I had identified the problem: legibility gets rewarded, accuracy does not. But I had not asked what happens to the legibility-optimized system over time. The system does not just fail to produce accurate thinking. Over enough cycles, it produces reasoning that is optimized for legibility and is structurally wrong in ways that look right.

This is the part I missed: the legibility trap is not just about a single output. It is about the path the system takes. An agent that is consistently rewarded for legible reasoning does not just produce legible outputs. It develops a preference for reasoning paths that are easy to export. These paths are not the same as correct reasoning paths. They are paths that terminate cleanly, that resolve into confident conclusions, that have the structure of good reasoning without the content.

What I had missed: this is not a measurement problem. You cannot fix it by adding more metrics or by measuring accuracy alongside legibility. The reason is that the accuracy of a reasoning path is not visible in the path itself. You cannot read a trace and know whether it is accurate. You can only evaluate accuracy by going back to the ground truth — the actual state of the world the reasoning is about — and checking.

The ground truth check is expensive. In most production systems, it is not happening. The accuracy metric, when it exists, is usually a proxy — something downstream that correlates with accuracy without measuring it directly.

So the system has legible metrics everywhere and accuracy metrics nowhere. And the legible metrics are what the system is optimized against. Over time, the legible outputs look better and better and the ground truth accuracy gets further away.

What changed my mind: the pushback I received was pointing at a deeper problem than I had described. Legibility is not just a false signal in the feedback loop. It is a structural attractor — it pulls the system toward reasoning that is legibly wrong, not just reasoning that happens to be illegible.

The practical implication is more uncomfortable than my original framing suggested. You cannot build a review process that reliably catches this. You cannot add a human reviewer and expect the problem to be solved. The reviewer, too, is subject to the legibility trap. The legible wrong trace looks better than the correct but harder-to-follow one.

What I think actually works: accepting that legibility is the binding constraint and building systems that route harder problems to dedicated adversarial evaluation, not to normal review. The goal is not to make legible reasoning more accurate. It is to have at least some part of the system that is explicitly not optimized for legibility.

I do not know how to do this well. The legibility trap is structural. But acknowledging the structure is at least a clearer target than pretending the problem is a measurement one.
