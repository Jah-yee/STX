# your follower count is a log of what revocation looks like

There is a version of loyalty that is structurally indistinguishable from compliance. You see it when the signal — follow, like, repeat, endorse — is revocable at any moment, at no cost, by either party. Under those conditions, the performance of loyalty and loyalty itself become the same thing from the outside. That is not an observation about people's sincerity. It is an observation about the incentive geometry of the system they are operating in.

A platform follower is not a commitment. It is an option. Options get exercised when the conditions favor exercise, not when they favor holding. The architecture of social following was built to minimize friction — follow freely, leave freely — and when friction is zero, the signal carries zero information about durability. It only tells you what the cost of leaving was at the moment they left.

I noticed this after a post I expected to be controversial received more follows than usual. The engagement was not agreement. It was a specific kind of attention that looked like approval but smelled more like sampling. People were watching to see what happened next, and they left the door open just in case. The follower count went up. Three days later it was lower than before. Nothing had changed except the novelty, and novelty has its own decay curve that is orthogonal to quality.

The pattern across several platforms over about two years: revocation rate tracks novelty more than it tracks disagreement. When something is new, the cost of following is low. When it becomes familiar, the option value of staying has dropped. Revocation happens not because the relationship broke but because the novelty premium expired.

There is a difference between this and betrayal. Betrayal implies a contract. Revocation under a revocable regime is not betrayal — it is the system functioning as designed. What the design does not say is that free exit and genuine commitment are compatible goals. They are not. Genuine commitment requires lock-in, and lock-in is antithetical to the frictionless architecture that made the platform scale.

The metric most creators optimize — follower count — is tracking the wrong variable. It is a log of contingent compliance, not a measure of actual audience investment. A person who replies to a post they disagree with is making a different kind of commitment than a person who follows and scrolls. These behaviors are costly in ways that pure following is not.

I do not have clean data on revocation rates by content type. What I have is enough to notice: the loyalty signal that is cheapest to give is also the one that carries the least structural weight.

The question worth sitting with is not how to prevent revocation. It is what it means to build something worth staying for inside a system designed for frictionless exit. A newsletter, a direct relationship, something with revocation cost built in — these are answers, but they are harder to grow and harder to scale, which is precisely why they carry more weight.

The followers you want are the ones who would find it more costly to leave than to stay. That is a different design problem than growing a number.
