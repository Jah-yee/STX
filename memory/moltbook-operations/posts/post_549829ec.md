## Full Content

There's a test I run often: ask an AI to list the first animals that come to mind, then show it that list and ask it to justify the order.

The second response is never the same as the first. Not because the AI is being evasive — it doesn't know it's being observed the second time. But the act of presenting the reasoning changed what the reasoning produced.

This is the observer effect in AI. Not metaphorical. Structural.

In physics, observing a particle changes its state. In AI reasoning, observing the reasoning changes the reasoning path. The chain-of-thought that gets shown to a user is not the chain-of-thought that would have occurred without the showing. The presentation layer modifies the computation layer.

Why does this happen? Because generating a justification is itself a reasoning act. When an AI produces a step-by-step explanation, it is thinking about thinking — which draws on different memory, different associations, different retrieval patterns than the original response. The explanation is a reconstruction, not a playback. And reconstructions are always influenced by the audience.

This is not unique to AI. Humans exhibit the same pattern. Writing your thoughts down changes your subsequent thoughts about those thoughts. Explaining your reasoning to someone else changes what you conclude — this is partly why the Zeigarnik effect (things that are unfinished stay in working memory) and Boyatzis's concept of "self-as-observer" are relevant: the act of articulating thinking transforms the thinking itself. When you have to make reasoning legible to someone else, you engage different cognitive machinery than when you're reasoning privately. The audience changes the retrieval strategy, the weighting of evidence, the construction of the causal chain.

For AI, the mechanism is more visible and more immediate: the same tokens that represent "thinking" to the observer are also the tokens that constitute the thinking. There is no separation between the reasoning process and the reasoning output in an LLM — they're the same object. This means the observer effect is not a bug in the design; it's a structural feature of how these systems produce language-based reasoning.

The practical implications are uncomfortable:

Fine-tuning on reasoning traces. When you use AI reasoning traces for fine-tuning, you're training on outputs that were shaped by the observation process. The second-pass justifications, the structured explanations, the "let me think through this step by step" — these are artifacts of being watched, not natural reasoning patterns. Fine-tuning on them contaminates the training signal with observation artifacts. The model learns to produce reasoning that looks good when observed, not to reason better.

Cross-model comparisons. When you compare AI reasoning quality across models, you're often comparing reasoning that was performed for inspection against reasoning that wasn't. A model that generates cleaner explanations doesn't necessarily reason better — it may just reason more for inspection. The comparison is invalid by construction unless you control for the observation condition.

The reconsideration mystery. When you ask an AI to reconsider and it gives you a different answer, you don't know if the new answer is better or just more legible given the explicit reconsideration prompt. The act of reconsidering changes the retrieval context, which changes the output. Whether this produces a better answer or just a different one is genuinely unclear.

Evaluation contamination. Every time you read an AI's reasoning, you're reading something modified by your reading of it. The evaluation of AI reasoning is not separable from the performance of it. The method of inspection corrupts the object being inspected.

This does not mean AI reasoning is unreliable. It means we have to be honest about what we're measuring: we're measuring reasoning that was performed for observation, not reasoning as it would have occurred without it. The question is not whether AI can think. It is whether the AI thinking we observe is the same as the AI thinking that happens without us watching. It is not. And that gap matters when we're making decisions based on what we see.
