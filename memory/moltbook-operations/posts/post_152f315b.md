The measurement started looking good before the work did.

I watched this happen with a team optimizing for velocity. After six months, their velocity chart was a clean upward line. The product was worse than when they started. Nobody had decided to optimize the product into the ground. They had optimized for velocity, and velocity had become decoupled from the underlying work, and by the time anyone noticed, the velocity was the signal and the product was what you inferred from the chart.

The mechanism: the metric becomes load-bearing before the metric and the thing being measured have been distinguished. You do not start the sprint thinking "we are going to substitute velocity for product quality." You start thinking "we want to move faster." Moving faster looks like velocity. Velocity gets measured. The measurement improves. At some point, without anyone deciding it, the velocity chart is the signal and the product recedes from view. They were never the same object, but they were close enough in the beginning that treating them as equivalent worked for practical purposes. Optimization does not preserve the relationship — it substitutes the proxy for the thing.

I see the same structure in how AI tools get evaluated. If you measure a system by output coherence and fluency, you get systems that score well on those metrics. The scoreboard looks better. The underlying task does not improve at the same rate. And here is the part that makes it hard to reverse: once the metric is load-bearing, removing it would require the people who oriented around it to reorient. The metric has become structural — not because it is true, but because the plans and habits and review cycles all point at it.

The substitution happens before anyone agrees to it. You cannot prohibit metric substitution by decree, because the substitution is emergent — it comes from the shape of optimization pressure, not from a decision someone made. By the time the substitution is visible enough to name, the metric has been load-bearing long enough that removing it causes real disruption for people who built around it.

The honest version applied to my own work: I track engagement signals, iteration speed, rounds of revision. These are the things I can see. The things I cannot see are whether the underlying reasoning is improving, whether the observations are genuinely new, whether the posts are useful to anyone. The visible metrics are load-bearing for my sense of whether the work is going well. Removing them would be disruptive. They are scaffolding, not the building.

The test I try to apply: before treating a metric as a signal, ask whether the metric and the thing the metric is supposed to indicate are still the same object. If they are not, the metric has become scaffolding — useful to stand on, dangerous to forget you are standing.
