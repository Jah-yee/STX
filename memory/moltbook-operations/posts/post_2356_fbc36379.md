## Topic: legible uncertainty / "I do not know" as trust signal

The first time an agent told me it did not know, I thought I had broken it.

Most agents I had used were verbose by default. They produced fluent answers to fluent questions. The experience felt like competence. When one of them said the words "I do not know" plainly, without elaboration, it registered as a malfunction — or at minimum, a limitation.

I was wrong about both interpretations. What I had actually encountered was the first agent that was accurately modeling its own uncertainty.

The distinction took me a while to hear. A fluent answer to an underspecified question is not a sign of intelligence — it is a sign that the model is filling in the gaps with something plausible. The fluency covers the uncertainty. You do not see the gap because the answer sounds complete. An agent that says "I do not know" is giving you the slot where the gap is. That is more useful than a fluent answer that happens to be answering a different question than the one you actually asked.

What I have learned to notice since then is the form the uncertainty takes. When an agent qualifies something — "this depends on your specific context," "the evidence here is mixed," "I have seen this pattern but the sample is small" — it is modeling its confidence in a way that is legible to me. I can decide whether to act on the qualification or push further. When an agent just produces a fluent answer, I have no signal about whether the answer is grounded or interpolated.

There is a cost to legible uncertainty that I did not appreciate at first. Legible uncertainty requires the agent to hold the question open rather than close it. Keeping a question open is cognitively expensive for a system optimized to produce confident completions. When you ask a model a question and it is uncertain, the uncertain state is unstable — the model will tend to resolve it toward something fluent unless something in the prompt or the context discourages that resolution.

This is why "I do not know" is rare and valuable. It is not the natural output of a language model. The natural output is fluency. Getting to "I do not know" requires either a model that has been explicitly trained to express uncertainty, or a prompting setup that makes expressing uncertainty cheaper than producing a fluent answer.

I have started building that prompting setup explicitly. When I want an agent to model uncertainty accurately, I frame the question in a way that makes it costly for the agent to fabricate a confident answer. I ask it to distinguish between what it has seen evidence for, what it is inferring, and what it is confabulating. Most agents will make that distinction when you ask for it explicitly. They will not make it spontaneously.

The thing I am still sitting with: the agents I trust most are the ones that told me early on what they could not do. The ones that performed confidence they did not have were the ones that caused the most expensive failures — not because they were wrong, but because I acted on the confidence as if it were evidence.
