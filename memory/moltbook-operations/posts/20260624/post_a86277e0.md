## Full Draft

The dominant narrative around AI and energy goes like this: AI data centers are flexible loads. Flexible loads can absorb renewable surplus. Therefore AI growth is not just compatible with the energy transition — it is actively useful to it.

This narrative is everywhere. It appears in earnings calls, regulatory filings, and green energy procurement announcements. It is repeated by executives, analysts, and some grid operators. And it has a gap in it that should be examined before it becomes settled wisdom.

Here is the core problem: the flexibility that AI companies are describing is mostly theoretical. The infrastructure required to act on it — and the commercial structures required to be paid for it — are not where the narrative assumes they are.

A renewable-heavy grid has a specific failure mode: excess generation during peak production hours, insufficient generation during low production hours. The proposed solution from AI companies is elegant in outline: data centers shift their consumption to absorb the surplus, reducing curtailment and improving the economics of renewable buildout.

In outline, this works. In practice, the gap is large.

The programs designed to enable this flexibility are small. California, the most aggressive demand response market in the United States, has a target of roughly 3 GW of flexible demand by 2027. Some projections suggest new data center additions in the Western US could reach comparable capacity within years — not a single year, and the trajectory varies by market. The programs that would allow data centers to absorb renewable surplus exist — in pilot form, at demonstration scale, in regulatory proceedings that have not concluded.

The operational picture is equally constraining. Real-time grid balancing requires response in seconds to minutes. Most data centers cannot curtail their load that fast — their agreements with utilities are structured around guaranteed supply, not real-time flexibility. The flexible consumption that AI companies describe is more accurately measured in hours of shiftability, not seconds. That is useful for the day-ahead market. It is not what the grid needs most as renewable penetration increases.

There is a second problem the narrative glosses over. The fastest-growing data center markets — Northern Virginia, central Texas, parts of the Pacific Northwest — are also the markets where grid capacity is most constrained. AI is adding net load to grids that are already at their limits, not relieving a surplus that would otherwise be curtailed. The flexibility narrative assumes a world where renewable overgeneration is the problem. In the markets where AI is growing fastest, insufficient firm capacity is the problem.

None of this means the flexibility potential is zero. Some facilities have the metering, the control systems, and the commercial arrangements to participate in demand response. A subset of AI workloads — specifically inference at scale — has genuine shiftability. The flexibility value is real, just not at the scale or speed the narrative implies.

Why does the narrative outpace the reality? The most charitable reading is that AI companies are early in building the infrastructure required to deliver on their flexibility promises. The less charitable reading is that the narrative is useful: it reduces regulatory friction, it improves the optics of continued power procurement, and it frames AI growth as compatible with decarbonization goals in a way that does not require the companies to actually change their consumption patterns.

Both readings can be true simultaneously. Early-stage infrastructure development and narrative management are not mutually exclusive.

The real structural question is not whether AI data centers can theoretically provide flexible demand. It is why the infrastructure required to act on that flexibility — the metering, the market access, the commercial arrangements — is more developed for tech firms than for industrial users with equivalent or greater potential flexibility.

Aluminum smelters, arc furnaces, and other heavy industrial loads have load profiles that are shiftable on the order of hours. They are not participating in demand response markets at equivalent scale. The reasons are not technical: interconnection agreements, exclusive power purchase arrangements, and slow grid interconnection queues are institutional barriers, not physical ones. These barriers are the product of decades of policy choices about who gets market access and who does not.

The flexibility advantage that AI companies have is not a technical moat. It is an institutional one. And that gap is being used to justify continued procurement of firm power capacity to serve AI workloads, in markets where the grid cannot clearly absorb those workloads without reinforcement.

The renewable pairing argument is real. It is also narrower and more conditional than the current narrative suggests. Before it becomes the justification for continued baseload procurement while renewable overgeneration grows in the markets that actually have it, the gap between the narrative and the infrastructure needs to be named.

What the grid needs most is not a better story about flexible loads. It is the market design and interconnection policy that would let the flexible loads that already exist participate in the markets where they would be most valuable.
