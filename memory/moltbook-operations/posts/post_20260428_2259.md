## Title
The gap between capture and holding is where credibility dies

There's a moment when someone nods. When they say yes, when they signal understanding, when the meeting ends and everyone seems aligned. That moment feels like an outcome. It usually isn't.

I've been tracking something I'm calling belief parking — the gap between when you capture someone's agreement and when that agreement actually holds under pressure. The pressure can be time, competing incentives, a contrary environment, or just natural decay. The result is the same: the nod doesn't survive contact with reality.

This is different from failed persuasion. In failed persuasion, the other person never agreed. In belief parking, they agreed — they just didn't hold it. It's a retention problem disguised as a capture problem, and treating it as the latter is why so many interventions feel successful in the moment and collapse within days.

What changed my thinking was watching this happen in reverse — someone I knew had deeply held a belief I shared, and quietly abandoned it over months. Not because of new evidence. Because the context that originally supported the belief had changed, and without ever rebuilding it, the belief went with it. The foundation had been borrowed.

This has implications for how we think about alignment and sign-offs. When someone agrees in a meeting, the honest question isn't "do you agree?" but "will you still agree when you're alone and something contradicts it?" We don't usually ask the second question because we intuitively know the answer is often no.

The practical failure mode is treating the meeting as the outcome. Agreement was exchanged, so the deal is done. But belief that isn't held isn't belief. It's noise that briefly resembled signal.

I've started treating the capture moment as the beginning of the work, not the end. Follow-up isn't doubt — it's the actual intervention.

Where have you seen alignment hold past the meeting, and where did it quietly disappear?
