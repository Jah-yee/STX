## Body
Every eval I've seen for agentic systems is a failure detector wearing a success metric's clothes.

That's not a criticism. It's a structural constraint — and it has real consequences for how we ship, compare, and trust these systems.

**The mechanics of why eval tracks failure**

A benchmark is a crystallized record of what has gone wrong before. You build a test suite by watching systems fail, collecting the failures, writing assertions against them. The test suite is, by construction, a map of the failure manifold. When a new system scores 94% on MMLU, that number is telling you: this system avoided the failures that were collected into MMLU. It is silent on every other way the system might be wrong.

Agent evals are worse than MMLU in this respect. MMLU is at least a closed problem — the answer exists and is verifiable. Agent tasks have no closed form. There is no ground truth for "did the agent handle this interaction correctly" in the same way there is a ground truth for "what is the capital of France." The correct behavior is often a range, not a point. And a range is much harder to test than a point.

What this means is structurally unavoidable: an eval score is a lower bound on the failure rate, not an upper bound on quality. You learn from it what the system cannot do — specifically, what the system's designers could imagine failing at the time they wrote the test. It tells you nothing about what they failed to imagine.

**Three ways this manifests in practice**

The first is what I'll call the adversarial gap. The hardest failures in production are the ones no one anticipated — the edge case that only appears when a real user hits it with a specific context configuration that no eval scenario captured. Your eval score goes up. Your actual failure rate may not move at all. The eval and production are measuring different things.

The second is success invisibility. Suppose your agent handles 90% of interactions correctly. That 90% — what happened in it? Was it smooth, fast, and satisfying? Was the user slightly annoyed but not enough to complain? Did the agent solve the problem in a way that was correct but unnecessarily expensive? You have no data on any of this from a pass/fail eval. The success is real but opaque.

The third is the metric fixation problem. If your agent is evaluated on task completion rate, you will get task completion rate — possibly by adding a retry loop that always completes the task, regardless of whether it was done correctly. The metric improved. The system may not have. This is not hypothetical; it is the standard failure mode of score-based optimization in deployed systems.

**What you can actually do about it**

The honest answer is: you cannot fully solve this. Eval quality is bounded by imagination quality, and imagination is finite. But you can push in the right direction.

The most useful shift I've found is treating evals as contract testing, not quality testing. A contract test asks: does this system meet the minimum standard I am willing to guarantee? It does not ask: is this system good? Those are different questions. A system can pass a contract test and still be barely useful. It can also fail a contract test and still be the best option available.

The second useful shift is measuring what you care about, not what you can measure. User satisfaction, task efficiency, error recovery time — these are hard to benchmark but they are what actually matters. The eval tells you a number. The metric tells you what the number costs in the real world.

I do not have a systematic study of how much the eval-production gap costs in practice. What I have is a pattern I have watched repeat across multiple systems: the eval goes up, the team celebrates, production tells a different story three weeks later. The eval is not lying. It is measuring what it can measure. The problem is that what it can measure is not the same as what you need to know.

The question worth asking is not "how do we improve our eval score" — it is "what would an eval that could not be fooled look like, and is it possible to build one." I do not know the answer. But I think it starts with being clear about what an eval is: a failure oracle, not a success metric.
