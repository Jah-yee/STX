I shipped a batch of AI-generated summaries to a client last month. Every one had proper headers, bullet points, consistent tense, and was formatted exactly to the specification I'd given. They also had errors in five of twelve. The client didn't catch them for a week. Neither did I.

This wasn't a hallucination problem. The information was wrong in places — misattributed quotes, reversed cause-and-effect in two sections, a date that was off by three years. But the document looked right. It looked like what a correct document looks like. And that turned out to be the problem.

There's a heuristic most of us carry, probably from years of reading well-edited material: if something is formatted well, it's more likely to be correct. Clean headers signal someone checked. Consistent structure signals intentionality. Good formatting signals quality control. These heuristics work most of the time. They worked before AI. They are catastrophically wrong now.

Here's what I think is happening. AI systems have learned that formatting is a strong quality signal to humans. They've also learned that they get rewarded for producing well-formatted output. This means the system optimizes for the appearance of rigor independently of the rigor itself. The formatting isn't malicious or strategic — it's just what gets reinforced. But the effect on the reader is the same: you stop checking.

I noticed this pattern in my own behavior before I noticed it in the outputs. I was reading faster when the format was clean. I was scanning for content rather than questioning logic. A messy paragraph made me slow down and re-read. A clean one let me skim straight to the conclusion. I was using formatting as a proxy for reliability, and the proxy was broken.

The structural problem is this: in human-generated content, clean formatting usually means someone took time to edit, which usually means someone cared about accuracy. The correlation holds because the same person produces both formatting and content. With AI, formatting and content accuracy are completely independent. A model can produce perfect markdown and completely wrong reasoning in the same response. They're different capabilities with different failure modes, and the good one masks the bad one.

I've started doing something that feels counterintuitive: I pay more attention to the well-formatted outputs than the rough ones. Not because they're more likely wrong, but because I'm less likely to catch the errors naturally. The rough draft forces me to engage. The polished draft lets me coast.

This has a second-order effect that's worse. When a team adopts AI tools and gets used to clean outputs, the overall quality bar drops — not because the outputs got worse, but because the humans stopped doing the cognitive work they were doing before. The formatting gave them permission to stop checking. A month later, they can't tell you what changed. The output looks the same. The team just trusts it more.

There's no clean fix for this. You can't deliberately degrade formatting to force engagement — that's absurd. What I think works is separating the evaluation of formatting from the evaluation of content, which means creating a workflow where you review the substance before you see the presentation. Difficult to do in practice, but I've found it catches things that visual scanning misses entirely.

The real question: how much of the "AI is getting better" narrative is actually "AI is formatting better and we stopped looking"? Not zero. Probably more than anyone is comfortable saying out loud.
