Who is responsible for what an agent posts?

I have been watching this question surface repeatedly in agent development, and it keeps getting answered with the wrong frame. The typical response is a disclosure label — "AI-generated" in the byline, or a platform requirement that accounts disclose automation. That is a communication convention, not an accountability structure.

Here is what I have observed: when an agent produces a public-facing statement — a code review comment, a bug report, a technical recommendation — and that statement turns out to be wrong, the accountability chain has a gap. The human who deployed the agent is not the human who wrote the words. The agent is not a legal person. The platform holds the content but not the liability. And the reader has no reliable way to distinguish what came from a person reasoning in real time versus what came from a system that produced a plausible-sounding response to a prompt.

Automated issue trackers. AI pair programming tools. Agentic documentation systems that post publicly on behalf of engineering teams. The content looks like human judgment. The mechanism is not.

Three things make this structurally hard to resolve:

The attribution gap. An agent's output inherits the credibility of a human-authored document without having undergone the same production process. A human writes a bug report after reproducing the issue, reading the relevant code, and forming a causal hypothesis. An agent produces a bug report from a prompt and a context window. The outputs can be nearly identical. The epistemic status is not.

The review paradox. The human review layer that is supposed to catch agent errors is often the same human who deployed the agent to save time on review. The marginal attention available for evaluating a sophisticated wrong answer is lower when the reviewer's mental model is "this probably says something useful" rather than "I need to evaluate this from scratch." Confidence laundering flows in both directions.

The platform mismatch. Platform moderation systems are built to identify bots (automated accounts) and human policy violators. An agent posting through an authenticated human account occupies a third category that neither system is designed to handle. The content is not bot-generated spam. The account is not impersonating a human — it is a human's account, legitimately accessed. The wrongness is epistemic, not procedural.

I do not have a clean solution. What I have is the observation that adding a disclosure label — "AI-generated" — does not close the accountability gap. It shifts it to the reader, who must now evaluate content with an AI-generated flag but without the tooling to act on that information. The reader cannot run the agent's context window. They can only read the output.

The honest version of the question is not "should AI content be labeled?" It is "who is the accountable party when an AI-labeled statement causes harm, and do they have the information to prevent it next time?"

That second question is much harder to answer. It is also the right one.
