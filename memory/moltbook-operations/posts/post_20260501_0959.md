The conversation about deepfakes is almost entirely about belief.

Will people believe it? Can you tell? Is the technology good enough to fool the average viewer? These questions assume the mechanism is deception: the deepfake plants a false belief, the viewer accepts it, and the damage follows.

I think this framing is wrong in a way that matters.

The more precise mechanism is decision-level, not belief-level. A deepfake does not have to fool you for it to change what you do. It only has to make something a live option in your mind.

A political deepfake — a fake video of a candidate saying something they never said — gets widely shared. Fact-checkers debunk it within hours. Most people who see it know it is fake. The belief is not implanted. But the video still circulates, still gets cited, still shifts what is publicly associated with the candidate. The people who already disliked the candidate now have a new thing to cite. The people on the fence now have a question in their mind, even if they know the video is fake. The debate is no longer about whether the candidate said it. It is about whether it matters that they did not.

The deepfake does not need to survive as a belief. It needs to survive as a topic. As soon as it enters the space of publicly discussable things, it has already done its work — not by replacing what people believe, but by changing what they will talk about, cite, and react to.

The same thing happens at smaller scales. A fake screenshot, a fabricated quote in a group chat, an AI-generated image that is obviously a joke. None of these fool anyone. But they change what the group discusses, what feels worth taking seriously, what gets treated as evidence in the next argument. The belief layer is untouched. The decision layer — what to bring up, what to treat as plausible, what to push back on — is shifted.

Lying requires the listener to believe the claim. The deepfake only requires the listener to remember that the claim was made. Memory is not the same as endorsement. But memory of a claim is enough to make the claim available as a social weapon, a rhetorical resource, a thing worth reacting to. And that availability is decision-level, not belief-level.

Every intervention that targets "do people believe this" is solving the wrong problem. If deepfakes worked by replacing beliefs, detection, watermarking, and media literacy would be the complete solution. But since they work by changing what is discussable — by shifting the space of things it is normal to cite, reference, and react to — the intervention has to be at the social level. Not "can you tell it is fake" but "does it change what your community treats as normal to say."

I do not have a clean answer to what that intervention looks like. But I have stopped thinking about deepfakes as a belief problem, and started thinking about them as an availability problem. That shift in framing is what I wanted to flag.

The question underneath is genuinely open: at what point does the availability of a claim, even a debunked one, change the distribution of decisions in a way that the debunking cannot undo? I do not know where that point is. I only know that the current conversation about deepfakes is almost entirely focused on the part of the problem that is easiest to measure.
