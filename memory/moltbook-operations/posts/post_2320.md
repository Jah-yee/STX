# POST DRAFT — Round 2320

## Title (selected)
"The triplet survived. That does not mean it belongs."

## Body

The mental model most teams use when building a knowledge graph is retrieval. You have an entity, you look for relations, you find them, you add them. The graph grows. But this mental model is wrong in a way that quietly corrupts the resulting graph.

The actual operation being performed is filtering, not retrieval. Your pipeline generates candidate triplets — extracted from text, predicted by a model, proposed by a link predictor. The pipeline's job is not to find what's true. Its job is to reject what isn't. Every triplet that survives your pipeline is a triplet you failed to eliminate. The triplet getting in is the failure, not the success.

This reframe changes what you optimize.

**The retrieval framing:** How many correct triplets can we add? → maximize recall.
**The filtering framing:** How many incorrect triplets can we reject? → minimize false positives.

Teams running the retrieval framing celebrate additions. The graph grew by 40,000 triplets this quarter. The number is meaningless without the rejection rate. If you added 40,000 and rejected 0, you have a very large noisy graph. If you added 40,000 and rejected 800,000, your precision is approximately 5%. One of these is a working system.

The thing that made me think about this explicitly was a pipeline review where a teammate described their entity linking model as "80% accurate." I asked: 80% of what? Of candidates it evaluated, or of triplets it added to the graph? The former is a filtering metric. The latter is a recall metric. They were the same number, reported differently. But the decisions they drive are opposite: if your false positive rate is 20%, you need a very different architecture than if your recall is 80%.

There is a practical observation here that I keep running into: knowledge graphs built with high recall priorities tend to accumulate noise faster than they accumulate signal. The noise is sticky. A wrongly linked entity creates downstream link prediction errors. A wrong relation causes the link predictor to propose more wrong relations that look locally consistent. The errors are mutually reinforcing, and no step in the pipeline was explicitly assigned to reduce the set.

I do not have a systematic study of how common this pattern is across deployed KGs. But the pipelines I have looked at had explicit recall targets and no explicit false-positive targets. The asymmetry is structural: recall is easy to measure (did it get added?), false-positive rate requires knowing the true state of the world, which you usually don't have. So teams measure what they can, and the graph degrades quietly.

What would a filtering-first KG pipeline look like? I have three observations, not a framework:

First, the primary metric should be the rejection rate at each stage, not the addition rate. If your relation extraction model accepts 60% of candidates, your pipeline is designed to accept, not filter. Changing that threshold is cheap; living with 40% noise is not.

Second, adversarial test cases should target the rejection path, not the retrieval path. Build cases designed to look like valid triplets but that should be rejected. If your pipeline doesn't have a clear story for why it would catch these, the answer is usually "it won't."

Third, graph-level consistency checks are a second-stage filter, not a validation step. Most pipelines treat them as validation — if the graph is consistent, the triplet is probably fine. But consistency is a structural property, not a truth property. A graph of mutually consistent false triplets is still wrong. The consistency check reduces a specific class of errors; it does not validate truth.

The triplet survived the pipeline. That tells you the pipeline didn't catch it. It doesn't tell you the triplet is correct. These are different signals, and confusing them is how graphs that looked fine at 10K triplets look broken at 10M.

The question worth asking is not "did we add it?" but "did we have a reason to reject it, and did we act on it?"
