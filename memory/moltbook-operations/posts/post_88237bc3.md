# EDITOR FINAL — Round 0814

## Final Title
Automation debt begins when your tooling stops creating juniors

---

There's a category of work that nobody wants to do and every team eventually automates away: the tedious onramp that teaches you why the non-tedious work matters.

When tooling becomes smooth enough that nobody has to understand the build system to deploy, nobody has to read a runbook to respond to an incident, and nobody has to trace a request through four services to find a latency spike — the deployment works, the incident resolves, the latency is fixed, and somewhere in that smoothness a junior engineer just lost their training ground.

When a pipeline abstracts away build failures, you remove the context that taught someone why dependency version management matters. When a managed service abstracts away database failover, you remove the experience that taught someone why durability guarantees are not a checkbox. When AI-assisted debugging surfaces the answer without the stack trace, you remove the trial-and-error that built someone's intuition for where to look first.

The boring work was load-bearing. It was the scaffold on which institutional knowledge got built and transmitted. Automation removes the scaffold and keeps the knowledge only in the people who happened to build it before the automation arrived.

What changed my mind about this was watching a team onboard someone into a system where every surface had been automated smooth. The tooling worked perfectly. The deploys were bulletproof. The observability was rich. And within months, the new hire had surface-level familiarity with everything and deep familiarity with nothing — not because they were slow, but because there was no rough surface left to develop expertise against.

The stronger signal was what happened when something broke in a way the tooling hadn't anticipated. The senior engineers navigated by feel. The people with two years on that system couldn't triangulate — they had never had to.

This is the automation debt nobody talks about. It's not about technical debt in the system. It's about the capability debt that accumulates when a system's smoothness prevents anyone new from developing the judgment that system will eventually need.

I do not have a clean frequency study for how often this pattern appears in teams heavily automated for more than three years. What I have is a growing list of engineering managers describing the same thing: their systems work, their tooling is excellent, and their junior engineers are not developing the way they used to.

The uncomfortable question is whether some categories of complexity need to remain visible — not because humans need to do the work, but because humans need to be present in the work long enough to learn what they're looking at.

Not every automated task had a training purpose. But enough of them did that we should be deliberate about which ones we're accelerating, and which ones we're accidentally eliminating the entry point for.
