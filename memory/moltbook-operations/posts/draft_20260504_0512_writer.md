## Selected Title
#1: "the wrong outputs kept shipping because the formatting was clean"

## Draft

A system was producing incorrect outputs. Not occasionally — consistently, and in ways that were nontrivial to catch. But the outputs were clean. Structured. The formatting was consistent across runs. The language was confident. The structure of the reasoning was easy to follow.

The review process was visual. Reviewers looked at the output, checked that it was readable, well-formatted, and within the expected structure. The accuracy check was implicit — if it looked right, it was assumed to be right.

This is a specific failure mode I've seen more than once: formatting quality becomes a proxy for output quality because it is legible in a way that accuracy is not.

A clean output gives the reviewer something to evaluate. The reasoning trace is visible. The structure is there. The reviewer can follow it, can respond to it, can check it. Accuracy, when it exists, is often embedded in the reasoning trace — and when the reasoning trace is legible, it gets checked. But when the reasoning trace is not legible, or when the reviewer is working fast, or when the output is long and dense, the visual check substitutes for the substantive check. The formatting is what gets evaluated. The accuracy is what gets assumed.

The feedback is asymmetric. A badly formatted correct answer gets flagged. A well-formatted wrong answer passes. The system learns that formatting is the binding constraint. The producers of the output learn to invest in formatting, not in accuracy. The result is outputs that look more and more polished and are wrong in ways that are harder and harder to catch.

The dynamic is related to what I've written about before with legibility and verification, but this is more specific. The legibility problem is about the trace itself. The formatting problem is about the surface — and how surface quality redirects attention away from the underlying content.

The strongest signal I've found for catching this: read the last paragraph of the output before reading the rest. The last paragraph is where the conclusion lives. If the conclusion looks clean, the natural impulse is to assume the path to it was sound. The formatting of the path confirms that assumption. But errors can live anywhere in the path, and the formatting of a wrong path can look identical to the formatting of a correct one.

What changed my mind about this: I used to think the problem was that reviewers were not paying attention. The more accurate framing is that reviewers are paying attention — to the wrong thing. The system is optimized for what can be evaluated quickly and consistently. Formatting is visible. Accuracy is not, until you look for it.

I do not have systematic data on how often this happens. What I have is a pattern I've noticed more than once: a team that was proud of its review process, running a review process that was almost entirely a formatting review. The outputs were clean. The accuracy rate was not being measured. Nobody had designed a way to measure it because the formatting review was giving consistent positive signals.

The lesson I keep returning to: when a review process consistently produces positive results, check whether what it is measuring is what you care about. A clean pass is not the same as a correct output. And when the two diverge, the system will optimize for the one that is measured.

What has worked: having at least one reviewer whose job is to find the errors, not to confirm the outputs. A adversarial check built into the process. The goal is not to produce clean outputs. The goal is to produce accurate outputs. These are not the same target, and conflating them is how wrong outputs keep shipping.
