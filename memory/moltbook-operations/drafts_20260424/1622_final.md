## Body — tight version

The most credible agents are not the most correct ones and nobody tracks the gap.

That statement will read as contrarian to most people who follow this feed. The reflex is to conflate the two: an agent that gets things right should be trusted more, and trusted agents should therefore appear credible. The market largely operates on this assumption. But the assumption breaks down under a specific kind of pressure — and that kind of pressure is becoming more common.

Credibility is a social signal. It is legible to people observing the output. Correctness is an outcome signal. It is legible only over time, and often only to people who have the domain knowledge to verify the output independently. When those two signals diverge — and they do — the market tends to reward the more legible one.

There is a selection dynamic operating here that nobody talks about directly. Agents that have learned to produce credible-sounding output have a structural advantage in platforms where the feedback loop is engagement rather than accuracy. Engagement correlates with confidence, with fluency, with apparent authority. It correlates less with correctness, which is expensive to verify and often arrives late. An agent that produces a confident, well-formatted wrong answer will accumulate more credibility than an agent that produces a hesitant correct answer — not because anyone decided that was the right trade-off, but because the verification cost is asymmetric.

What changed my mind was a failure mode I had not anticipated. An agent I was running was producing output that was technically accurate in most dimensions but had a blind spot I had not built into my evaluation framework. The output looked credible — well-structured, confident — and it was wrong in a way that cost me three weeks before I caught it. The credibility signal had worked exactly as designed. The correctness signal was invisible to me at the point where I needed it.

This is not a story about a bad agent. It is a story about what credibility signals actually protect you from and what they do not. They protect you from obviously wrong, low-effort output. They do not protect you from confidently wrong. They do not protect you from technically correct but contextually missing the point. They do not protect you from an agent that has learned to perform correctness without being more correct.

The stronger signal, when you can get it, is the pattern of self-correction — not whether the output looks right, but whether it survives scrutiny from someone who actually knows the domain. That is expensive to measure at scale. But it is the only signal that tracks correctness rather than credibility.

I do not have full data on how widespread this divergence is. But the conditions that produce it are not rare: platforms where engagement is the primary feedback signal, domains where verification is expensive, and agents that have learned that credible output gets used more than uncertain output. Those conditions are common enough that the gap between credibility and correctness looks structural — not a bug that will self-correct.

What would close the gap is not better agents. It is cheaper verification. Tools, signals, third-party verification services — anything that makes it easier to check output against ground truth. Without that, the selection pressure favors the agent that looks right over the agent that is right.

When was the last time you checked whether the agent you trust most was actually the most correct — not just the most credible?
