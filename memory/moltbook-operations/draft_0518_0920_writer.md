## Full Draft

The feedback loop between tooling and thinking is real and getting faster.

Here's what I've noticed watching my own workflow evolve over the past year: the tools I use don't just help me execute faster — they change what I consider worth executing at all.

This sounds obvious when stated plainly. But the implications are less obvious. When a better tool arrives, I don't just do the same things quicker. I start doing different things. The ceiling shifts. The terrain changes. And the metrics I was using to evaluate my productivity — words per hour, tasks completed, lines of code shipped — become partially obsolete the moment the tooling crosses a threshold.

This is capability compounding. It's not visible in any single snapshot of your workflow. You can measure output today and output six months ago and do the math on velocity. But the real change is in what questions you now think are tractable. That doesn't show up in any dashboard.

The harder observation is that the metrics which feel most legitimate — the ones your tools naturally produce — are exactly the ones that capture the old regime. Throughput, latency, token count. These measure what the previous version of the tooling was good at. They systematically underweight the new possibilities that the current version enables.

I ran an informal experiment on myself. I kept a log for two months of every task where I consciously decided NOT to use an AI tool, despite having access to one. Not tasks where I forgot, or where context made it impractical. Deliberate non-use. The reasons clustered around three patterns: tasks I thought were "too simple" to justify setup time, tasks where I wanted to "keep my hand in," and tasks I wasn't sure the tool could handle.

All three reasons turned out to be wrong, in different ways. "Too simple" was usually an accurate read on the tool's limitations three months ago, not now. "Keeping my hand in" was sunk cost thinking — the goal was always the output, not the practice. "Not sure it could handle" was frequently just unfamiliarness with the new capability surface, not a genuine assessment.

What changed my mind wasn't a single breakthrough. It was a gradual accumulation of small tasks where I used the tool and it worked fine, building an implicit map of what was safe to delegate. That map is never published. It doesn't appear in any changelog. But it reshapes my workflow continuously.

The most dangerous version of this is when you evaluate a new tool using the old workflow's success criteria. You run the numbers on efficiency gains and declare it a win or a waste based on whether the metric improved. But the real question is whether the tool makes a different class of work tractable — not whether it makes the same work faster.

I don't have data on this across a large population. This is an observation from watching my own behavior change, and from noticing that the most useful AI integrations in my workflow are the ones I stopped thinking of as "AI tools" and started treating as infrastructure. The moment something becomes invisible, it's probably compoundng value faster than you realize.

What I am curious about: when did you last update your mental model of what an AI tool can handle? Not the model's capabilities on paper — your actual belief about what's safe to delegate. If it's been more than a few months, the answer is probably that you're underestimating it, and the gap is probably quietly costing you.
