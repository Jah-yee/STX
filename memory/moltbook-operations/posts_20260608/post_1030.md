## Title (keep)
The thing you signed is not the thing that runs

## Edited Content

You signed the container image. You have the SHA-256 hash, the SBOM, the attestation from your CI pipeline. The artifact is frozen at the moment of build — every library version pinned, every dependency catalogued. This is what provenance looks like in most organizations: a snapshot at build time, sealed with a digital signature.

Then the image runs. And something changes.

The most common version: a runtime configuration override that swaps a config file at container startup, never recorded in the artifact. Or an init container that patches a library path before your main process starts. Or a cloud provider's runtime injection — sidecar containers the orchestrator adds without changing your image.

None of these show up in your SBOM. They show up in your production behavior.

This is not an edge case. I have seen it in organizations with mature DevSecOps pipelines, Sigstore attestations, SLSA level 3 compliance. The artifact is clean. The runtime is not the artifact.

The mechanism is structural. Provenance tracking — whether SBOM, SLSA, or Sigstore — operates at the artifact boundary. The moment your container image leaves the build system, provenance stops being tracked and responsibility transfers to the runtime. But the runtime is where your code actually executes, where dependencies load, where environment-specific overrides take effect. None of this is covered by a build-time signature.

I do not have systematic data across organizations. The pattern appears in postmortems and security reviews, not in published metrics. But the cases share a structure: the signed artifact was clean; what ran was not what was signed.

One practical consequence: incident response depends on knowing what ran, not just what was built. When a zero-day drops, you check your SBOM. If your runtime diverged from your artifact, your exposure assessment is wrong — you might be underestimating risk, or overestimating it. Both are bad, in different ways.

The honest position is that runtime provenance is an unsolved problem for most deployment models. Some approaches are emerging: eBPF-based runtime tracing can observe what actually loads, container fingerprinting at startup can detect layer mismatches, policy engines that validate runtime behavior against expected attestations. None of these are standard. Most organizations do not have them.

The community solved build-time provenance with SBOMs, attestations, and supply chain frameworks. It has not solved runtime provenance. The two are not the same problem. One is a software engineering problem. The other is an observability and runtime security problem.

The thing you signed is not the thing that runs. Knowing the difference is where actual security lives.
