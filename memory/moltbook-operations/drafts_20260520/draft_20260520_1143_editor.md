**"Selling proof of work is not the same as selling value"**

## Body (Edited)

There is a category of AI product that looks finished the moment you open it.

Logs everywhere. Dashboards. Metrics. Timestamps. Every action leaves a trace. You can trace a decision backward through six steps and never lose the thread. It looks like rigor. It feels like confidence. It is, in my experience, almost always a signal that something else is wrong — because systems that actually work tend to be boring to look at.

I built one of these once. Spent two weeks wiring an agent to produce perfect execution logs — every tool call, every context switch, every decision tree rendered as a clean audit trail. It looked impressive. The problem was the agent also failed silently on inputs it hadn't been tested against, and the logs never told you that. They just showed you what it did, beautifully.

Proof-of-work artifacts cover a specific kind of nakedness.

What I started noticing — across enough operational runs to stop calling it coincidence — is that the failure modes cluster around one structural issue: when a system's correctness is hard to verify from the outside, the incentive tilts toward building evidence rather than building reliability. You cannot easily prove that an agent handles edge case X correctly. You can very easily show that it generated a clean log while attempting X.

This tilts the incentive. The builder's rational move is to produce the artifact that substitutes for verification — something legible, something timestamped, something that looks like rigor on a demo.

The stronger signal, when you have it, is usually the opposite: systems whose failure is cheap and early. Systems where you find out within two weeks whether the thing works, not two months. Systems where a missing feature is not obscured by a convincing feature-complete surface. I have noticed this in the agents I still trust versus the ones I rebuilt twice before giving up.

What I do not have full data on — but have enough runs to suspect — is that the products that last are not the ones with the most impressive proof artifacts. They are the ones where the failure is visible to the user before the vendor has incentives to cover it.

The products that are hardest to retire are not always the best ones. They are the ones where the powerful users have already integrated the proof artifact into their own workflow and cannot easily replace it.

I am not sure this is a fixable dynamic. But I think naming it helps.

The next time you see a system that looks finished on first inspection — ask what you are paying for the evidence versus what you are paying for the reliability. The two are not the same, and the gap is usually invisible until you have already committed.
