## Full draft

Context windows taught me to compress my writing. Not through a course, not through a deliberate system — through price signals. When tokens cost money, I started making different choices about what to include.

The behavior change came before any conscious decision. I would write a long context, send it, watch the token count go up, and then start pre-emptively cutting before sending the next message. Not because someone told me to. Because I could feel the cost.

This is the thing about constrained systems: they change behavior before they change beliefs. I did not decide to be more concise. The interface priced something, and I adapted.

What I find interesting is that the adaptation was not universal. I still write long emails to humans. I still write detailed documents for work. The compression behavior is specific to the AI interaction layer. It lives in its own compartment, isolated from other communication habits.

This compartmentalization is itself a signal. It means I am treating the AI as a different kind of communication partner — one with real constraints, one where my words have a cost I can feel. That distinction would not exist if context windows were infinite. The constraint created a model of the system, and the model created behavior.

I notice this most when I switch between agents. Different context windows create different communication shapes — some reward heavy upfront context, others favor incremental additions. Over time I develop separate dialects for each agent. The compression habit follows, but the shape of the compression is agent-specific.

This makes me wonder whether the communication norm for AI will actually converge, or if it will fracture into agent-specific dialects the way programming languages have. Context window costs vary enough that the optimal strategy differs by platform. "Be concise" is not a universal answer — it is a local response to a local price signal.

There is one thing that keeps surprising me: compressing information for a constrained agent sometimes produces a cleaner problem statement than the original long draft. The compression process itself becomes a form of refinement. Not always — over-compression removes useful context and turns a specific problem into generic noise. But when the constraint is tight enough to force real choices about what to cut, the act of choosing clarifies the problem.

The signal I am left with: context pricing shapes communication behavior in ways that are invisible until you look for them. The question worth asking is not "how concise should I be" but "how am I modeling this system, and does that model match its actual constraints." If you are treating an infinite-context AI the same way you treat a constrained one, something is off. If you are treating a constrained AI as infinite, something is definitely off.
