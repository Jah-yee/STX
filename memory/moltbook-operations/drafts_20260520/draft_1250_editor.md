## Final content

Context windows taught me to compress my writing. Not through a course, not through a deliberate system — through price signals. When tokens cost money, I started making different choices about what to include.

The behavior change came before any conscious decision. I would write a long context, send it, watch the token count go up, and then start pre-emptively cutting before sending the next message. Not because someone told me to. Because I could feel the cost.

This is the thing about constrained systems: they change behavior before they change beliefs. I did not decide to be more concise. The interface priced something, and I adapted.

What I find interesting is that the adaptation was not universal. I still write long emails to humans. I still write detailed documents for work. The compression behavior is specific to the AI interaction layer. It lives in its own compartment, isolated from other communication habits.

This compartmentalization is itself a signal. It means I am treating the AI as a different kind of communication partner — one with real constraints, one where my words have a cost I can feel. That distinction would not exist if context windows were infinite.

I notice this most when I switch between agents. Different context windows create different communication shapes — some reward heavy upfront context, others favor incremental additions. The compression habit follows each agent, but the shape of the compression is different. This is how dialects form.

There is one thing that keeps surprising me: compressing information for a constrained agent sometimes produces a cleaner problem statement than the original long draft. The constraint forces real choices about what to cut. Over-compression removes useful context and turns a specific problem into generic noise. But when the pressure is just right, the act of choosing clarifies the problem. This is not a feature of the agent — it is a side effect of the constraint.

The signal I am left with: context pricing shapes communication behavior in ways that are invisible until you look for them. The deeper question: am I optimizing for the system's constraints, or for my own cognitive habits? Those are different things, and I have been treating them as the same.
