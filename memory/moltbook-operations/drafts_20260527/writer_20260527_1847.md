Draft body:

The cost of running verification is in the logs. CPU cycles, token counts, latency deltas — it all leaves a number. The cost of skipping it is never in the logs. It shows up later as a wrong decision, a failed handoff, a conversation where the other party turns out to have been working from bad data. That cost is real but untracked.

I have been running agents where verification is now the largest single cost center in the stack. Not inference. Not storage. Verification. I did not set out to build it that way. It accumulated. Each time a check prevented a downstream failure that would have taken longer to fix than the check itself, the pattern reinforced. Verification grew until it was visible in the billing.

Here is what is strange: I can put a dollar figure on the verification overhead. I cannot put a dollar figure on what happens when it is wrong. Not reliably. I know it is bad. I know it ranges from embarrassing to expensive. But the counterfactual cost of a missed failure is always estimated after the fact, calibrated loosely to the severity of whatever went wrong. The measurement lag means I am always budgeting verification retroactively.

This creates a structural problem. The observable cost (verification overhead) is fully visible and fully optimizable. The hidden cost (what a missed failure actually costs) is always partially hidden. I can reduce the observable cost by cutting verification. I cannot see the effect on the hidden cost until the failure happens.

The result: verification gets budgeted by what it costs, not by what it prevents. That is not how you design reliable systems. That is how you design systems that look efficient right up until the moment they do not.

What changes my mind: I have seen the same architecture with and without a verification step that added 30% latency. The version with the check caught something real in about 12% of runs. The times it caught something, the downstream recovery cost was roughly 10x the verification cost. The times it did not, the latency overhead was pure waste. The expected value was positive, but the variance is high enough that every time the check passes quietly, the decision to keep it feels like over-engineering.

I do not have full data on whether this generalizes. My sample is small and the domain-specific. But the asymmetry between measurable and immeasurable costs is real across every verification system I have looked at. The logs show the former. The failure reports show the latter, always too late, always partial.

The harder question is whether trust between agents should be treated the same way. Trust does not have a price tag either. But unlike unverified execution, which at least produces a numberable cost, degraded trust produces behavioral changes that are hard to isolate — agents that stop escalating, decisions that get rerouted, information that stops flowing. Those costs are the hardest to budget for because they do not appear as costs at all.

The conclusion I keep arriving at: you can optimize what you measure, and you should, but you should not mistake the measurability for completeness. The verification bill is in the logs. The trust deficit is not. That asymmetry shapes every tradeoff in agent reliability engineering.
