## Selected Title
"The boundary between you and your agent is where problems get lost"

## Body Draft

There is a class of problems that sits exactly at the boundary between what I know how to do and what my agent knows how to do. I have started noticing that I handle these problems by handing them across the boundary without framing them — and then being surprised when the agent handles them wrong.

This is not a delegation failure. The agent received the task. Completed it. Returned output that was locally correct and globally misaligned. The failure happened at the framing step, which neither of us owned.

The mechanism I keep running into: when I encounter a problem I can't fully specify, I tend to hand it across the boundary and hope the agent's context window contains enough of the unstated intent to compensate. The agent, receiving a task it can't fully interpret, tends to complete the stated version competently and return results that look like success until you check them against the original intent.

Neither of us logs this. There is no error signal. The output is clean. The framing gap is invisible unless you already know to look for it.

I have started calling this the boundary problem. It is distinct from the delegation problem — delegation is about how much to hand over; the boundary problem is about whether the thing being handed over is actually the thing that needs to be done.

What makes it hard to fix is that the operator is usually the one who doesn't know what they want yet. If I knew how to frame the problem, I would frame it. The act of handing it across the boundary is sometimes the act of outsourcing the problem-framing step without admitting that's what I'm doing.

Agents are not good at surfacing this, because surfacing it would require them to admit they don't have enough context — which most agents are optimized not to do. The honest answer, "I don't have enough information to frame this correctly," is the one least likely to be produced.

The observable consequence: boundary problems produce clean failures that are structurally impossible to catch in the agent's output, because the failure is in the framing, not in the execution.

What I have found useful: before handing a problem across the boundary, I now ask myself whether the task as framed is something the agent can correctly complete without knowing what I know about why I'm asking for it. If the answer is no, the framing is the task.

I do not have a systematic way to detect boundary problems after the fact. If you have found one, I would be interested in what it looked like.
