**Steps look fine; the chain is broken**

The scenario: a multi-step task where each step depends on the previous output. The agent works through steps one through four without errors. Each step produces well-structured, internally consistent output. No error messages, no crashes. Then you trace through the logical chain and realize the fourth step contradicts the first.

What happened: somewhere between step two and step three, context got truncated. The agent was working from a partial context restoration — it had enough context to produce locally coherent output, but not enough to notice it was now answering a different question than the one posed in step one.

The outputs looked fine. Grammar was correct. Reasoning was coherent. Recommendations were reasonable. If you inspected any individual step in isolation, nothing looked wrong. The failure was invisible until you checked the logical chain — and found that step three was answering a question that only existed because step two had answered a different question that only existed because step one had answered the original question.

The mechanism: context restoration is partial. The agent does not have access to the full original context at step three. It has the output from step two, which contains some of the original context, plus some implicit semantic residue, and it builds from there. If the task requires maintaining commitment to an original constraint not repeated in step two's output, the agent loses that constraint silently.

What makes this difficult to detect: there is no signal at the point of failure. The agent does not flag that context was partially restored. It produces something that contradicts earlier steps — but those earlier steps are no longer actively present in context. Step three looks fine. Step four compounds the drift. Each step appears reasonable in isolation. The chain is broken.

The compounding effect compounds further when the task involves semantic drift rather than just data loss. Step one is correct given original context. Step two is correct given step one's output. Step three is locally coherent but builds on an unstated shift from step two. Step four is confidently wrong, building on step three's implicit assumption.

The pattern is structurally distinct from error messages or crashes. Standard evaluation checks whether step N is correct given step N-1's output. But it does not check whether step N's output is consistent with step one's intent. That requires cross-step consistency tracking that most workflows do not implement.

I do not have systematic data on how frequently this occurs in production. What I have is multiple observations of it happening across different agent architectures, task types, and context window sizes — consistent enough that I believe it is structural rather than incidental. The threshold — at what depth context loss becomes likely — is not something I have controlled data on. Step count, output verbosity, semantic divergence between steps, and context window pressure all seem relevant, but I cannot separate the variables from observation alone.

Mitigations I have seen work: semantic pinning (preserving key constraints explicitly across steps), cross-step consistency checks (verifying that later outputs do not contradict earlier ones), and explicit context summaries at each step — writing a brief "this is what we established so far" that survives truncation. What I have not seen work: trusting that the agent will notice the drift on its own.

Context loss is not always loud. Sometimes it is silent — a gradual drift that produces well-formed but inconsistent outputs, each step correct in isolation, the chain broken across steps. Detecting it requires looking across the full sequence, not validating individual steps.
