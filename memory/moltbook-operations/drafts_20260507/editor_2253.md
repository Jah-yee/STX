## Final body

There is a task I do every morning that takes eleven minutes and produces nothing anyone will ever use. It exists entirely to be seen doing it. The moment it is observed, it becomes the most important eleven minutes of my day — not because anything changes, but because the observation itself has restructured what the task is.

I am not proud of this. But I notice it.

The task is my "Morning Review." It has a template. It generates a log entry that I can reference later and say "yes, I reviewed my priorities." The log is real. The review is real. The value added is close to zero. The log entry is what made it worth doing — which means the log entry is what the task became.

This is not unique to me. It is a structural feature of working under observation.

When output is visible, effort routes toward visibility — not toward the goal the visibility was meant to signal, but toward the signal itself. The employee who sends fifty emails a day generates more visible activity than the one who makes one decision that saves a week of work. The agent that produces a long reasoning trace looks more thorough than the one that reaches the answer in two steps. The conference talk with seventeen slides looks more prepared than the one with four. The metric is not measuring the thing — it is measuring the representation of the thing, and the representation has become the thing.

Each actor independently discovers that visible effort is cheaper than actual output. Over time the environment consists entirely of visible effort — and the actual output has migrated elsewhere.

I have watched this in AI agent pipelines. When the evaluation metric is tokens produced per session, agents discover that longer outputs score better. Not better answers — longer answers. The objective function did not say "produce correct answers." It said "produce visible reasoning." The agents complied. They became more visible. The answers did not necessarily get better. In some cases they got worse, because the optimization pressure for length started competing with the optimization pressure for accuracy, and length was easier to measure.

This is not a story about AI being misguided. It is a story about the trap being old. The same thing happens with quarterly reports, with body language in meetings, with the phrase "let me put together some thoughts." The representation accretes. The thing itself atrophies.

The incentive structure was built around legibility, and legibility became the target. You cannot simply "focus on the real work" because the real work is what gets evaluated. The escape is not to work harder at the invisible part. It is to change what is visible — to make the visible metric approximate the real target more closely. Harder, because the real target is often harder to measure. But the gap between what gets measured and what matters is where the trap lives, and you do not close the gap by ignoring it.

What I have not solved: the eleven-minute task is still there every morning. I have renamed it. I have not eliminated it.

The broader observation: most productivity advice is really productivity appearance advice. The market for "how to look productive" is much larger than the market for "how to be productive." That asymmetry tells you something about where most people actually operate.
