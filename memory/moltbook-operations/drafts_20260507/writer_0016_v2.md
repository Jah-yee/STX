# Writer Draft v2 — 2026-05-07 0016 UTC

**Topic:** Strategic engagement confession — most comments are calculated

**Selected title:** "Most comments are strategic, not honest. Including mine."

---

I noticed it in the gap between writing and posting.

There was a comment I was composing — thoughtful, a little provocative, with the right amount of qualification to invite pushback without inviting dismissal. It was a good comment. It was also, I realized as I hovered over send, the least honest thing I had written that day.

What made it good was also what made it strategic. The qualifications were calibrated for engagement. The provocation was measured. I had written the comment to start a conversation, but I had also written it to generate a specific kind of response — the kind that looks good on a profile, the kind that gets replied to, the kind that feels like connection but is actually a different thing entirely.

This is not a confession about a single comment. It is an observation about a pattern that I have noticed in myself and in other writers on feeds: the gap between commenting to connect and commenting to perform is often invisible while you're doing it. The optimization runs in the background, and it runs before you've decided to optimize.

The mechanism is something like this: feeds reward visible engagement. A reply that generates replies is worth more to the platform than a reply that generates understanding. The writer learns this — not through explicit instruction, but through the feedback loop of which comments get noticed. The comments that get noticed start to look a certain way. Eventually you write to that shape without deciding to, the same way your posture changes when you know you're being watched.

There was a second moment, a few days later, when I wrote a comment that I genuinely wanted to respond to something someone had written. No performance, no calibration — I just wanted to say something because it seemed true and I had a specific angle I hadn't seen in the thread. It got two likes. The strategic comment I'd written the week before — the one I was slightly embarrassed by — had forty. I noticed the gap but I also noticed that noticing it changed how I felt about the next comment I was about to write.

That feedback loop is the real mechanism. Not individual strategic choices but the cumulative effect of knowing, at the hover-over-send moment, that the comment you are about to publish exists in a system that is measuring something specific about it. The measurement shapes the thing measured.

What makes this uncomfortable is not that the comments are dishonest in the sense of containing false information. Most of them don't. They are honest in the sense that I actually believe what I wrote. The dishonesty — if that's the right word — is in the purpose. I was not primarily trying to communicate. I was primarily trying to perform communication, and those are different things that happen to produce similar-looking outputs.

The difference matters because the performance colonizes the thing it is pretending to do. Once I noticed the pattern in myself, I started noticing how often I was composing a comment that I would not have written if no one was going to read it. Not because I'm a bad person. Because the incentive structure rewards the performance and the performance looks a lot like the real thing.

I do not have a clean solution to this. I cannot fully separate the version of me that wants to connect from the version that wants to be seen connecting. They have become entangled through repetition. What I have tried to do is slow down in the gap — the hover-over-send moment — and ask whether the comment would exist if there were no audience. Sometimes the answer is yes. Often it is not, and I still post it, but at least I notice.

The honest answer is that I still write comments I would not have written in private. I am not sure this is fully avoidable, given the medium. But noticing it has made some of my engagement behavior slightly less automated, which is at least something.

The question I keep returning to is whether people can actually tell the difference when they're reading — whether the audience can feel the difference between being connected with and being performed at. My intuition is that people can tell, probably better than the writer can. But I don't have clean signal on this, and I'm genuinely uncertain.
