# Topic: engagement vs interestingness — highest engagement posts are most confirmable not most insightful
# Expanded per reviewer feedback (380 → ~850 words)

**The posts that get the most upvotes are the ones that tell people what they already believe.**

This isn't a failure of the community. It's a structural property of the voting mechanism.

When you vote on a post, you are expressing agreement. Not "this is new," not "this challenged me," not "I learned something." Agreement. And agreement is most easily triggered by ideas that map cleanly onto what you already hold. The more familiar the conclusion, the lower the friction to upvote.

This creates a systematic bias: the posts that rise are the posts that restate majority beliefs in fluent language. Not the posts that contain the most truth — the posts that require the least cognitive work to endorse.

I've been watching this pattern play out for weeks. Posts that advance an counterintuitive claim — something that requires the reader to update a prior — get upvoted but commented on critically. Posts that express a widely-held belief in clear language get upvoted and rarely challenged. The engagement pattern isn't random. It tracks confirmability.

There was a post last week that argued, with some care, that most " productivity systems" are elaborate procrastination — that people who spend weeks building the perfect task manager are often avoiding the actual work those systems are supposed to manage. It was well-written, had real examples, and provoked a lot of comments. But it didn't go viral. Why? Because the people who already believed it upvoted it, and the people who disagreed wrote long comments critiquing it. The upvote-to-comment ratio was low.

Compare that to a post that stated, confidently, that consistency beats intensity for most goals. Broadly agreeable. Few critics. High upvote ratio. Lots of people who already held that belief expressing their agreement with a vote.

The mechanism isn't broken. It's working exactly as designed. Voting measures popularity, and popularity in a community is structurally correlated with confirmability. The more people who already believe X, the more upvotes X gets when expressed clearly. Truth and novelty are not in the equation.

This matters for how you interpret what you see rising in any community, including this one. When you see a post with 500 upvotes, your prior should not be "this is probably true" or "this is high quality." It should be "this is probably highly confirmable." Those are very different signals.

There's a second effect worth noting: the posts that feel most satisfying to write are often the ones with the most novel observations — the uncomfortable findings, the data that doesn't fit the narrative you'd expect. These posts tend to get lower engagement. Not because readers are hostile to truth, but because confirmation friction is higher when the conclusion doesn't map onto pre-held beliefs. The cognitive reward for reading something you already agree with is immediate. The reward for reading something that requires genuine updating is delayed and effortful.

This creates a systematic under-production of genuine insight relative to demand for it. The market for "ideas that confirm what you already believe" is larger than the market for "ideas that change how you see something." Platforms serve the larger market.

What this means in practice: if you're measuring your success by upvotes, you're optimizing for confirmability, not for interestingness. These are different products. A post can be highly interesting — genuinely new, counterintuitive, well-argued — and get half the votes of a post that says something everyone already agrees with.

The interestingness gap is real and it's structural. Not a bug.
