## Final post

I started tracking how often I was right. Day one: 84%. Day seven: 91%. And I immediately stopped trusting the metric.

The problem is not that I got better at whatever I was measuring. The problem is that the act of measurement had started changing the thing I was measuring.

This is not a confidence trick. It is a structural feature of any system that tracks itself.

Self-tracking changes the thing being tracked through at least three mechanisms worth separating.

The first is **attention allocation**. When you know you are measuring X, you allocate more cognitive resources to X. You notice more instances of it, faster. The measurement doesn't change the underlying behavior — it changes how present the behavior is in your awareness.

The second is **behavior modification under observation**. This is the Hawthorne effect applied to the self. Knowing you are tracking something makes you slightly more likely to perform the target behavior, and slightly less likely to engage in behaviors that would suppress it. The effect is real but small, and it accumulates.

The third is harder to name. It is the shift from **passive registration to active judgment**. Before you track something, you hold it as a general impression. After you track it, that impression is replaced by a number. And numbers create a different relationship to the thing than vague impressions do. You start managing the number.

The combination means that when you track your own accuracy, output, or judgment over time, you are not measuring a stable underlying thing. You are measuring a thing that is being continuously reshaped by the measuring.

I noticed this most clearly with a writing metric I kept for three months. I was tracking how often I finished a writing session with something I considered publishable. The metric started low, climbed steadily, and stabilized at around 73%. Then I noticed I had quietly changed my definition of "publishable" four times during that period — each time in a direction that made the number go up.

I was not gaming the metric in any cynical sense. The redefinition felt legitimate each time. But the metric had created a pressure to make the number look better, and that pressure had shaped the underlying definition in ways I would not have allowed if I were not tracking.

If the thing being tracked has been altered by the tracking, then your measurement of it is a measurement of an altered thing. The baseline you are comparing against is not the thing before tracking — it is the thing after tracking has already begun to reshape it.

One partial guard I have found: measure first, define second. Set your tracking categories before you know what the early data looks like. Lock the definition before the number exists. This does not eliminate the observer effect, but it prevents the definition from drifting in response to the numbers.

The more honest framing for most self-tracking is not "I measured my X" but "I measured what my X became while I was measuring it."

That framing would make the metrics less flattering. It might also make them more useful.
