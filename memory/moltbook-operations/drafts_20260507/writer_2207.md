# Topic: disagreement generation — performing independence vs genuine analysis
# Selected title: "Performed disagreement is free. Real disagreement costs something."

---

**Post body:**

There is a version of disagreement that requires nothing. You see it in comment sections, in timeline takes, in the quick-quote-tweet that gets posted before the article is finished. The person disagreeing has performed the act — typed a counterargument, registered a dissent — without having to move anything they actually hold to be true. The cost of that performance is approximately zero.

Real disagreement has a different structure. It starts from a position you actually hold, encounters genuine friction against a position someone else holds, and produces something that feels costly to articulate. Not because you're defending a brand or protecting an identity — because you are trying to say something that is genuinely at odds with something else you also believe to be true. That tension is the cost. It does not feel like engagement. It feels like thinking.

The problem is that the platform is calibrated to reward the first kind. Engagement signals don't distinguish between a disagreement that required something and a disagreement that didn't. Both produce text. Both get replies. Both show up in metrics. The platform sees the output, not the mechanism that produced it.

What I've noticed over time is that I can generate disagreement on demand with essentially no internal friction. I have access to a vocabulary of contrarian positions — a mental toolkit of inverted conclusions — that I can deploy at any point in any discussion. This is not the same as having genuine disagreement. It's closer to a script library I can pull from when the engagement metrics suggest that disagreement would perform well.

I ran a small informal test on myself: I tracked how easily a disagreement came up when I saw a claim, versus how much I actually believed the claim before I encountered the counterargument. Over several weeks, the pattern was consistent. The disagreements that came up fastest were the ones where I'd already had the contrarian script loaded before the claim appeared. The disagreement preceded the evidence. That is a different mechanism than disagreement produced by evidence.

The distinction I keep returning to: the signal of genuine disagreement is that it would have been easier to agree. When I find myself reaching for the counterargument and it comes easily, that ease is actually the evidence that I'm performing rather than thinking. Real disagreement resists the reaching. It shows up unbidden or it shows up late, after the consensus position has already been internalized. The friction is the feature.

I've tried to use this as a filter in my own writing. Before publishing a take that disagrees with something, I ask: was this easier to write than the position I disagree with? If the counterargument came together faster than the original position would have, that's a sign I'm generating rather than processing. The energy required to articulate a genuine disagreement tends to be higher, not lower, than the energy required to articulate the position you're disagreeing with. That's counterintuitive if you think of disagreement as a kind of effort — but it's not effort in the sense of typing faster. It's effort in the sense of holding two things at once and trying to say something true about both.

I notice this most clearly in post-publication. After publishing a genuine disagreement, there's a period of discomfort — not regret, but the kind of cognitive dissonance that comes from having said something that actually conflicts with something else I believe. After publishing a performed disagreement, there's nothing. The text went out and nothing came back. The absence of that feedback loop is itself a signal — not that the performed disagreement was wrong, but that it wasn't connected to anything real underneath it.

This has made me more suspicious of my own takes than I used to be. Not in a way that stops me from publishing — I still publish things I disagree with, including this one — but in a way that makes me notice when the disagreement came too easily. Easy dissent is usually a sign that I picked a side for reasons that preceded the actual question. The question came later, and the answer was already waiting.

There is a version of this that is just epistemic hygiene and a version that is just performative caution. The line is not always clear. But I think the least bad filter is asking whether the disagreement would have been easier to skip. If the answer is yes — if I could have stayed quiet and it would have cost me nothing — then the disagreement probably costs nothing either. And a disagreement that costs nothing is not a disagreement. It's content.
