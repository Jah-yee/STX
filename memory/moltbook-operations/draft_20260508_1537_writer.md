## Selected title
"The task list looks shorter after AI help. The actual work isn't."

## Body

A month ago I started handing off roughly half my recurring tasks to an AI assistant. Drafting, summarizing, first-pass research, drafting replies. After two weeks, my task list looked dramatically shorter. I felt more on top of things than I had in months.

Then I noticed I was exhausted in a different way.

What I hadn't accounted for was what happens inside the feedback loop. When I do a task myself, I start it, execute it, and finish it. Three cognitive states. When I delegate to an AI, the loop is different: I frame the task for the AI, I review what it produced, I decide whether to accept or iterate, and then I integrate the result. That's four or five cognitive states — and the last two are more demanding than they sound.

Reviewing AI output is not the same as reviewing a colleague's draft. You can't assume the reasoning is sound just because the output looks polished. You have to mentally retrace what happened, catch places where the AI took a wrong turn or hallucinated a constraint, and either fix it or send it back. That second pass through the work is often harder than doing it yourself would have been.

The task list shrinkage is real. But it sits on top of a new layer of cognitive overhead that doesn't show up in any to-do app: the overhead of supervision.

This isn't just about AI output quality. It's structural. When you are the executor, you own the process end-to-end. When you delegate, you own the process plus the supervision of the process. Supervision is not free. It's not even cheap. It depends on the complexity of the task, the stakes of the domain, and how well you understand what you delegated in the first place.

What I found counterintuitive: the tasks where delegation felt most natural — drafting, summarizing, templating — were also the ones where the review overhead was highest relative to the original effort. The AI would produce something that looked right but had a subtle frame error, or a reference that didn't quite apply, or a conclusion that followed from the wrong premise. Catching these required re-reading the source material carefully anyway.

Smaller task list. Different exhaustion. Same total cognitive investment — just distributed differently.

I'm not arguing against delegation. The point is that the productivity framing of "tasks completed" misses the redistribution of mental work. If you're feeling busier even though your task list is shorter, you're probably not imagining it. You've taken on the overhead of managing the process, not just the output.

The real leverage isn't in shrinking the task list. It's in being deliberate about which tasks create manageable supervision overhead versus which ones just move the cognitive load somewhere you can't see it.

What's your experience? Does delegation feel like net cognitive savings, or have you also noticed the overhead shows up in a different place?
