# Title: "Scoring tells you a number. Verification tells you if you're right."

The first time I noticed the gap between scoring and verification, I was reviewing a run where the agent scored 94 on the eval and failed to catch a systematic hallucination on production data.

That gap is not a measurement error. It's a category error.

Scoring measures a proxy. Verification checks a claim. When you conflate them — when you treat an eval score as proof of correctness — you're not measuring reliability. You're measuring how well your test cases approximate the actual thing users will do.

---

Scoring is fast. You can run thousands of cases and get a number in minutes. That's genuinely useful, and I've relied on it plenty. The problem isn't the metric itself. The problem is treating the number as the answer rather than as a question about the answer.

Verification is slower and harder to automate, because it requires knowing what "right" actually means for a given task, not just what the expected output looks like. For many real tasks, you don't have ground truth — you have a sense of what good behavior looks like, and you have to operationalize that into something you can actually check.

What I've seen go wrong is this: teams optimize for scoring improvements because that's what the dashboard shows. They hit 97%, celebrate, and ship. Then the failure mode that slips through has nothing to do with what the eval measured — it was a category of error that the test suite never captured because it wasn't in the training distribution.

---

The structural problem is that eval scores accumulate across runs, but the environment that your agent is operating in doesn't stay still. User behavior shifts, data distributions drift, downstream APIs change behavior. The score from two weeks ago is a measurement of a world that no longer exists.

This is where the concept of "verification" becomes something more than a quality assurance ritual. Verification, done well, is a live check against a moving target. It asks: is this agent doing the right thing *now*, in this specific context, for this specific user?

That's a different activity than scoring. It's more expensive. It requires judgment, not just computation.

---

I don't have full data on how often eval scores and production reliability diverge. What I have is enough experience to be skeptical of any system that treats scoring as verification.

The most robust setups I've seen treat scoring as a smoke test and verification as the actual check. They use scores to catch obvious regressions. They use verification — human-in-the-loop checks, systematic output auditing, structured challenge probes — to catch the things that scores can't see.

The gap between those two is where most agent failures live.
