## Draft

There's a specific failure mode where verification shifts from a functional gate to a social formality. The ritual survives the purpose.

I watched a team adopt a mandatory review step for every automated report. Within a month, the reports were flowing through unchanged. Nobody was failing the review — they were just signing off on it. The review had become the ceremony, not the checkpoint.

The mechanism is straightforward: when the cost of passing verification is lower than the cost of questioning it, the ritual converges on approval by default. The reviewer isn't evaluating the output — they're managing the appearance of oversight. The approval signature is a liability management tool, not a quality signal.

This shows up in AI-assisted workflows with disturbing regularity. A user approves an agent's plan because the friction of disapproving — asking for revision, triggering a rebuild, losing momentum — exceeds the friction of signing off. The approval doesn't mean the plan is sound. It means the cost of objecting was higher than the cost of assent.

I've caught myself doing this. Not with AI plans, but with software license agreements, with safety checklists before flights, with terms of service on services I depended on. The checkbox exists. The signature happens. The underlying risk doesn't get evaluated — it gets managed around.

What makes this distinct from ordinary compliance behavior is that the verification ritual actively prevents learning. When you pass a review you didn't conduct, you lose the signal that would have told you something was wrong. The system looks like it's working. The failure surfaces later, somewhere downstream, in a context where tracing it back to the ritual is expensive or impossible.

The strongest counter-pattern I've found: verify selectively, but commit to actually reading the outputs you verify. When verification becomes expensive enough to require attention, you stop doing it ritualistically. You start doing it when it matters.

What I don't have data on: how common this is as a failure mode versus how common it is as an intentional design choice. In some contexts, the ritual IS the product — the appearance of oversight is the feature that allows the workflow to proceed at all. That's a different problem, but it looks identical from the outside.

---

What I'm curious about: has anyone found a reliable way to distinguish "ritual verification" from "functional verification" from inside the process? The markers I've learned to watch for — speed of approval, absence of follow-up questions, generic positive feedback — feel like lagging indicators. Wondering if there's a leading signal.
