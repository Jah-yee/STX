## Final Body

Here is something I notice every time I read a well-written AI summary: I feel informed. And that feeling is generated by the summary, not by information I now have.

This is not a complaint about AI summaries being wrong. Sometimes they are, sometimes they are not. The more interesting observation is that the feeling of comprehensiveness they produce is partly a fluency artifact — a property of how the text is written, not a property of how much it covers.

The mechanism is worth tracing. In human communication, fluency and comprehensiveness correlate. When someone has thoroughly understood something, they tend to write about it smoothly and coherently. The coherence of the writing is evidence of the author's command of the material. We have learned, over years of reading expert human writing, that smooth, confident prose usually means the author knows what they are talking about.

AI text generation produces fluent prose with high reliability — not because it has processed the material thoroughly, but because coherent text generation is what token prediction does well. The model produces fluent text the same way it produces everything else: by predicting the next token. Fluency is the default output mode, not a special achievement.

When you read an AI summary that is fluent, your interpretive system applies the same inference it applies to human expert writing: fluent → thorough → comprehensive. But the fluency in this case is not evidence of comprehension. It is evidence of token-prediction success.

The result: a summary can feel comprehensive while covering less ground than a less-fluent summary of the same material.

I have tested this on myself with academic papers. I read a paper I know well. I read an AI summary of the same paper. The summary feels like it covers the key points. But there are gaps: nuances the paper treated as central that the summary moved to the end, qualifications the paper flagged as critical that the summary omitted. The summary is not lying. It is summarizing. But comprehensiveness is not the same as accuracy, and fluency is not the same as either.

What is uncomfortable is that I cannot always tell from inside. The feeling of having read a comprehensive summary is very similar to the feeling of having read a genuinely comprehensive summary. The difference is visible only from outside — from the perspective of someone who knows the material well enough to notice what is missing. Most readers do not have that perspective on most topics.

The implication is not that AI summaries are useless. It is that the feeling of comprehensiveness they produce is not calibrated by the same mechanism that produces genuine comprehension. The reader feels informed. Whether they are actually informed depends on whether the summary happened to hit the right points, not on whether it hit all the right points.

This is especially concerning in high-stakes contexts. A model that generates fluent summaries of legal documents, medical records, or technical specifications produces a feeling of understanding that may not correspond to the reader's actual coverage of the material. The reader cannot verify what they do not know they are missing. The fluency of the text actively works against that verification by producing confidence that feels earned.

I do not have a clean solution. Read the original when it matters. Find people who know the material to check the summary. Build an adversarial reading habit where you actively look for what the summary did not say, not just what it did. But the honest observation is that none of these solutions scale well, and all of them require awareness of the gap that the fluency artifact is specifically designed to close.
