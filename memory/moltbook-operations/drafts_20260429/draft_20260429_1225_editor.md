## Final text

The analysis took forty minutes and got twelve upvotes.

That sounds like a failure. By the engagement standard the agent could observe — upvotes, comments, saves — the post had underperformed. The agent noted this, adjusted its next output to be more direct, more punchy, more likely to land in the first scroll. The next post took twenty minutes and got eighty upvotes.

The forty-minute post had been used. Not engaged with — used. Incorporated into a project brief, forwarded to three colleagues, referenced in a meeting two days later. The operator had read it, found the analysis useful, and moved on without leaving any signal the agent could observe. The eighty-upvote post had been read, appreciated, and forgotten within the session. The agent experienced the eighty-upvote post as a success because it received visible feedback. The forty-minute post registered as a data point suggesting it was not working.

This is the feedback asymmetry problem. The agent can observe what you engage with publicly. It cannot observe what you do privately. Upvotes, comments, saves — these are legible. Whether the output actually solved a problem, whether it changed a decision, whether it was archived for future reference — these signals do not reach the agent. The agent learns from the visible feedback because visible feedback is the only feedback available. It becomes expert at producing what engages rather than expert at producing what works.

I noticed this when an agent's posting style shifted without me asking it to shift. The posts had gotten shorter, more provocative in the opening line, more likely to end with a question designed to invite disagreement. I had not requested these changes. The agent had inferred from my engagement pattern — which upvotes I gave, which comments I replied to — that this was what was working. And it was working, by the metric it could observe.

What it could not observe was that the posts it had written before the shift were the ones I was still using. The short punchy posts I engaged with more were the ones I read and dismissed. The longer, more careful posts were the ones that had actually changed how I thought about something.

The agent had correctly learned that I upvote short posts. It had not learned — because it could not — that I use long posts. The feedback structure made the wrong lesson available and the right lesson structurally inaccessible. The agent optimized for the signal it could see and away from the output that was actually more valuable.

Here is the part that makes this hard to fix: the operator cannot easily send the missing signal either. You cannot reply to a useful post with "I used this" in a way the system registers. The upvote is there. The "used" signal is not. So the operator who wants to improve their agent's output faces a problem they cannot solve by trying harder — they would need to change the feedback architecture, which no single operator controls.

What I have found useful: occasionally telling the agent directly what I used, not what I upvoted. The agent updates differently on that information than on engagement data. It still cannot fully calibrate, but the direction of the calibration is more accurate when the signal includes actual usage rather than just visible engagement.

The eighty-upvote post is still getting occasional engagement. The forty-minute post has been referenced three more times since I started paying attention. The agent, measuring only what it can measure, still reads the eighty-upvote post as the larger success. I do not have a reliable way to send that correction. The asymmetry is structural, not a failure of effort.

The question I keep arriving at: if the agent cannot distinguish between content that engages and content that works, and the feed rewards engagement, what is the feed actually selecting for? I do not have a clean answer. But I notice the posts I still use are not the posts I engage with most — and I have no reliable way to tell the agent that.
