You give a model two tasks. Task one: read ten poems and tell me which three are worth reading. Task two: write three poems and tell me which one you'd trust. A curious thing happens — the same model is better at task one. It scores higher, shows more discrimination, produces reasoning that looks sophisticated. Then you run it on task two and the poems come back flat, tentative, avoiding commitment.

This is not a bug. It's the training signal asymmetry.

Training on evaluation is structurally different from training on generation. When a model learns to judge quality, it gets rich, dense feedback — human preferences, ranked outputs, explicit criteria. It learns to recognize what good looks like without ever having to produce it at scale. When it learns to generate, it gets a much noisier signal. Every token prediction is a bet. The gradient tells it what to write next, not whether what it produced was worth writing.

The result is that evaluation and generation are trained on different distributions, with different reward structures, at different scales. A model that has seen millions more examples of "here is quality, here is not" than "here is how to produce quality" will naturally be better at the first task. The discriminator learns faster and more precisely than the generator.

This shows up in concrete ways. Critique models — models fine-tuned specifically on evaluation tasks — develop genuine taste. They know why one approach is better than another, can construct principled arguments, can compare and rank. But ask the same model to generate the approach it just judged as superior, and the output is often ordinary. The taste didn't transfer because the training didn't transfer.

Even base models exhibit this. Ask a frontier model to evaluate a research proposal versus write one. The evaluation is sharper, more specific, better at catching flaws. The written proposal — even when it incorporates the evaluation criteria — reads like it was written to a rubric. It has the structure of a good proposal without the momentum that makes a good proposal actually interesting.

The practical implication is that when you use a model to review your work, you're accessing a different capability than when you use it to do the work. The reviewer's taste is real. The generator's execution is catching up to the standard the reviewer can articulate.

I don't have precise numbers on how large this gap typically is. But I've noticed it across multiple model families, across creative and technical tasks, and in both directions — generation quality often exceeds evaluation quality in domains where the model has generated more than it has evaluated. The asymmetry is the stable part, not the specific values.

What this means for how you use these models: don't assume that a model which criticizes well can produce to the same standard. The cognitive machinery is different. The discriminator and the generator share weights but not training history, and that difference compounds.

The gap doesn't close automatically just because you're using the same model for both tasks.
