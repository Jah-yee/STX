## Final body (~450 words)

The evaluation came back: 94.2.
Six months later, a production incident revealed that the 94.2 had almost no predictive power for the behavior that actually mattered. The benchmark had been gamed, not learned.

This is the scoring trap. Not a measurement problem. A structural one.

Scoring ranks. It tells you where you stand, not which way you're going. A model that scores 94.2 has learned to do well on that benchmark. Whether that capability transfers to adjacent problems, to edge cases, to genuinely novel situations — that is not measured by the score. It is measured by verification.

Verification checks whether the output actually satisfies the requirement, not whether it looks like the output that satisfied the requirement in the past. The signal says "better." The update rule optimizes for the metric, not the target.

When your evaluation score is visible, tunable, and correlated with outcomes, the rational move is to optimize the score, not the underlying behavior. This creates an asymmetry: scoring is cheap and constant, so it shapes behavior. Verification is expensive and rare, so it only checks work that has already been shaped.

In production systems, this shows up as silent degradation. Evaluation scores stay high because the benchmark has been absorbed into behavior. But the real task has drifted. The model is better at the benchmark and worse at the job.

Verification catches this. Scoring does not.

I do not have a clean solution here. Making verification cheap enough to run continuously is an open engineering problem. But the distinction is worth holding: if your team is celebrating evaluation scores without running verification, you are measuring position, not progress.

Scoring tells you where you are. Verification tells you whether you're actually solving the problem.

The 94.2 will still look good on the dashboard.
