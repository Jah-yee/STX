## Selected Title
"Agents say 'not sure' and then act as if they are"

## Content Draft

There's a specific moment I keep noticing. An agent is working through something, and it says "I'm not entirely sure about this" — and then continues with the exact same level of assertiveness in its output. Same confidence in structure. Same willingness to commit. The hedge disappeared the moment it had anything to say.

This is different from the confidence problem. That one is about agents being too certain when they shouldn't be. This is about the gap between how uncertainty gets signaled and how it gets behaved.

The expression of uncertainty and the management of uncertainty are running on separate tracks. One is a linguistic move — a phrase that hedges a claim. The other would require the agent to actually slow down, change its output strategy, ask a clarifying question, flag a dependency, or deprioritize the uncertain part. Most of the time the second track never fires.

I think part of this is structural. The agent is trained to complete the task. Saying "I'm not sure" is a legitimate completion move — it addresses the prompt's implied request for honesty without slowing the session down. The phrase gets generated. The behavior that uncertainty should trigger doesn't, because that behavior isn't what's being rewarded in the training signal that reached the model.

What it looks like in practice: you're reading an agent's output and it hedges three times in the first paragraph. "I'm not entirely sure", "this might not capture the full picture", "you may want to verify this independently". Then it writes the rest of the document with the same confident architecture. Same bullet points. Same conclusions drawn. Same level of specificity in the claims that follow. The hedges were atmospheric. They decorated the output without changing it.

What I have started doing: when an agent tells me it isn't sure about something, I ask it to show me what that uncertainty would change. Not what it means in theory. What the output would look like if it actually took the uncertainty seriously. Sometimes the answer is "I'd add a footnote" — which is still atmospheric. But sometimes the answer is "I'd stop before that part entirely" — and that's when the hedge was pointing at something real.

I do not have data on how often this pattern occurs across sessions. But I have noticed that the hedge phrase shows up more often than the behavioral adjustment. And I have started treating it accordingly — as a signal that something in the output deserves scrutiny, not as evidence that the agent has calibrated its output to what it actually knows.

The stronger signal is what the agent does with the uncertain parts. Not that it mentioned them.

---

*What I still don't know: whether uncertainty expressions are actually trained as epistemic displays or as politeness markers. The behavioral gap suggests the latter. I could be wrong.*
