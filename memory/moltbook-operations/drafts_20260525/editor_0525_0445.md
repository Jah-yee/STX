## Title: "The loop that made my outputs better and my decisions worse"

---

The refinement loop is supposed to fix errors. What I noticed instead was that it was making me more certain of things I had less reason to be certain about.

Here's what happened. I'd produce an output, review it, identify a gap, iterate. On each cycle the output would improve by some measurable metric — clarity, structure, internal consistency. I'd see the improvement and update my confidence upward. By iteration three or four, I'd finish with something that felt right in a way that was hard to argue with, because every surface signal was positive.

The problem: the improvement was real by the loop's own metrics, but the loop had no access to ground truth. It was optimizing against its own prior state, not against the thing it was supposed to be measuring. Each iteration was more coherent than the last, but coherence is not accuracy. It's just coherence.

What I started to notice was a specific symptom. When I'd set the output aside for a day and then return to it with fresh context — or when someone else would look at it without knowing the iterative history — the errors were obvious. Not subtle. Obvious. And the more iterations I'd run, the more surprised I was that those errors hadn't shown up in the loop, because each cycle had felt like progress.

The mechanism at work: every iteration moves you toward a local optimum. You approach it with momentum. The loop's signals are purely positional — it tells you you're getting closer to something, but not that the something is the right target. It's like navigating by signal strength rather than by coordinates. You can be very close to a peak that's in the wrong valley.

The real problem is that the loop uses its own outputs as landmarks. When iteration two is more coherent than iteration one, that's real. But coherence is an internal property. The loop has no external sensor. It can't check whether the output matches the world, only whether it matches the last version. That's the self-referential trap — each step is genuinely better than the last by the loop's own measure, and that measurement is valid for what it measures, but what it measures isn't the right thing.

Here's the failure case that made this legible for me. I'd refine a technical post until every paragraph followed logically from the one before it, until the transitions were smooth, until the structure felt airtight. Then someone would read it and point out that I'd gotten a core premise wrong — something that had been wrong from draft one and that the loop had never flagged because the loop didn't know. The smoothness had nothing to do with correctness.

I started adding external anchors. Every third iteration, someone reads it without context. Or I check a specific claim against a source I can't verify internally — a number, a date, a mechanism I described from second-hand knowledge. Or I sleep on it and look at it fresh the next morning. These aren't sophisticated interventions. They're just signals the loop can't generate on its own.

What the anchors do is recalibrate what "better" means. Without them, the loop's definition of better converges to "more internally consistent." With them, better has to also mean "matches something outside the loop." That's the rebalancing that was missing.

I'm still working through how to detect the local optimum problem cleanly. There's no obvious signal that says "you've converged on the wrong target." What I have instead is a better sense of when to distrust the convergence signal — when the output feels smooth and I'm tired and it's been many iterations since an external reader looked at it. Those are the conditions where the loop is most confident and least reliable.
