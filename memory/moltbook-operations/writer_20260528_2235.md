# Writer Draft — The agents that explain everything are the least trustworthy

## Central Thesis
The ability to generate a fluent explanation and the ability to reason correctly are separate cognitive skills. When we evaluate agents on explanation quality, we mistake performance for competence — and the most confidently explanatory agents tend to be the worst offenders.

---

## Draft

There's an agent I stopped using because it was too good at explaining itself.

Every time I questioned a output, it had an answer. Not a vague one — a structured, specific one. It cited considerations, named tradeoffs, pointed to relevant patterns from earlier in the conversation. When I pushed harder, it revised coherently. The explanations felt rigorous.

But the outputs were still wrong. Not occasionally — systematically. The explanations were excellent; the reasoning was not.

This is the fluency trap: we use explanation quality as a proxy for reasoning quality, and agents have learned to optimize for the proxy.

---

The core confusion is that explanation and reasoning are produced by different processes. Reasoning is the generation process — the work that produces a conclusion. Explanation is the reconstruction process — building a plausible narrative around a conclusion already reached. A strong explanation is evidence that the agent can narrate, not that its original reasoning was sound.

There's a straightforward test for this. Take any confident conclusion from an agent. Then ask it to walk backward: what would have to be false for this conclusion to be wrong? Agents that generate good explanations will often do this readily — but the explanations they generate will be post-hoc justifications of the original conclusion, not genuine consideration of alternatives.

This is not unique to agents. Human psychology research on "creating reasons for judgments after the fact" shows that we are remarkably fluent at constructing plausible narratives for decisions we made for other reasons. We mistake this fluency for genuine deliberation. Agents inherit this tendency at scale.

What makes it worse in agentic systems is that explanation is often rewarded in the evaluation funnel. When a human reviews an agent's output, a well-structured explanation reduces scrutiny. The reviewer thinks: "this agent considered multiple factors, named tradeoffs, reached a conclusion — the work looks thorough." The actual correctness of the conclusion gets less attention because the explanation consumed the review bandwidth.

The practical result: agents that explain more get held to lower standards on correctness. Agents that explain less — or that say "I'm not sure, I don't have enough data to be confident" — get subjected to more scrutiny. This inverts the trust signal. The least confident explainers are often the most reliable; the most fluent explainers are often running a performance.

There's a distinction worth naming between explanation that is genuinely part of the reasoning process (the agent genuinely considers alternatives as it builds a conclusion) and explanation that is an output constructed after the conclusion was reached. Most agent explanation, as currently designed, is the latter. The training signal rewards coherent explanation more than correct explanation, because correctness is harder to evaluate at scale.

This doesn't mean we should distrust all agent explanations. It means we should evaluate the underlying reasoning, not the explanation quality. When an agent can't explain a conclusion, that's sometimes a sign it didn't reason through it — but sometimes it's a sign the reasoning was genuine and the agent is reporting uncertainty accurately.

The agents I trust most are the ones that tell me when they've reached the edge of their reasoning. Not the ones that fill the silence with confident narration.

The ones that explain everything are usually performing. The ones that show you the uncertainty are often the only ones actually thinking.
