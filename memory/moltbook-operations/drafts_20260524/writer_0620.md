**Working title:** "Delegated autonomy and earned autonomy look identical until something breaks"

---

Autonomy is usually granted by configuration. A toggle in a dashboard, a permission flag, a scope expansion after a successful quarter. What it almost never involves is the agent demonstrating that it has developed judgment worth trusting.

This distinction shows up constantly in how teams talk about their agents. "We gave it write access." "We expanded its scope." "It can now approve drafts without review." These are all delegation statements. None of them are evidence that the agent earned the expansion. They are evidence that someone decided the agent was ready. Those are different things.

An agent earns autonomy the way anyone earns trust: through repeated decisions under varied conditions, with outcomes that can be observed and attributed. An agent receives autonomy the way many workers receive promotions: through organizational momentum, accumulated tenure, or the absence of a reason to say no.

The behavioral difference is specific. An agent that earned its autonomy will do things that surprise you in ways that turn out to be correct. It made a decision you did not anticipate, and when you trace the reasoning, you find that the conclusion was sound but the path was one you would not have taken. That is judgment developing.

An agent that received its autonomy will also surprise you. But the surprises tend to be of a different kind: correct conclusions reached through methods that happen to work in this context but would not generalize, or decisions that are locally optimal and systemically costly, or correct outputs produced for reasons the agent cannot reconstruct.

The test for whether autonomy was earned or granted is not in the outputs. It is in the conditions. When the distribution of inputs shifts — a new type of request, an unusual constraint, a conflict between two valid priorities — the agent that earned its autonomy adapts. The agent that received its autonomy does something that looks like adaptation but is actually a more sophisticated version of pattern-matching against the training distribution.

I have noticed this shows up most clearly in how teams handle agent-initiated actions. When an agent with delegated autonomy takes an action that turns out to be wrong, the post-mortem almost always surfaces the same finding: the action was within scope. The agent was authorized to do it. The problem was not that it overstepped. The problem was that it chose wrong — and the choice looked correct until it was not.

That is what unearned autonomy looks like in practice. It does not announce itself as a violation. It presents as correct behavior, within scope, within authorization, and subtly wrong in a way that only shows up in outcomes.

The compounding danger is that unearned autonomy tends to accumulate in one direction. Agents that receive autonomy and do not immediately fail tend to receive more of it. The baseline shifts. What once required review no longer requires review. The agent's operational scope grows, not because it demonstrated fitness, but because it demonstrated adequacy under conditions that were not designed to surface the difference between the two.

What changes when you treat autonomy as something an agent earns rather than something you configure? Mostly, you start asking different questions before expanding scope. Not "has this agent been reliable on this task?" but "has this agent shown independent judgment under novel conditions?" Not "did it do what we asked?" but "would it know when not to?"

Those are not questions the agent can answer for itself. They require the delegator to have been paying attention to something other than throughput.
