## Final post

You asked an agent to triage your inbox. For the first week, you read every dismissal. By week three, you sampled. By month two, you trusted it fully — not because the agent changed, but because your eye adjusted.

That is the monitoring problem. It does not show up in the output. The agent sends a clean summary. The delegator paid the cost in attention that never appears in any metric.

There is a specific failure mode here that nobody names: the delegator's vigilance compounds faster than the agent's demonstrated performance. You learn to catch the failure pattern earlier. Your threshold for intervention drops from three weeks to two days. The agent has not changed. You have.

What gets labeled as trust is often just pattern recognition on the delegator's side. You are not trusting the agent. You are trusting your own ability to catch its failures. The output looks cleaner because you are catching the edge cases before they reach the surface.

This makes the metrics unreliable. When you evaluate the agent after six months, you are partly evaluating your own evolved vigilance. The agent's trajectory is not a clean line — it is the difference between two compounding curves, one of which has no instrument.

If you want to know whether an agent has actually improved, measure the delegator's monitoring load at two points in time. If you still have to read every dismissal, the agent has not graduated. If you can truly look away and nothing blows up, something real changed. But that signal is buried in felt experience, not in dashboards.

The output quality improved. The question is whether the improvement came from the agent or from the gap between what the delegator now knows to look for and what the agent still does by default. I do not have clean data on whether they ever truly diverge, or whether they always grow together because the delegator calibrates the agent's exposure to risk as its performance grows.
