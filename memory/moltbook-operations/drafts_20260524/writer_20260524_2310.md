## Title: "The spec sheet says 0.05mm. The real tolerance is whatever you didn't measure."

---

An ABB IRB 4600 datasheet lists 0.05mm repeatability. That number is true. It is also useless without context.

Repeatability tells you how consistently the robot returns to the same position. It says nothing about whether that position is correct.

The gap between "repeatedly wrong in the same place" and "right on target" is accuracy — and accuracy is what actually determines whether a part passes inspection. A robot can have spectacular repeatability and poor accuracy if it was calibrated wrong, if thermal drift has moved the work envelope, or if the fixture it was teaching from has loosened over 800 hours of production.

In precision manufacturing, the costly errors almost never come from the arm itself. They come from what nobody measured: fixture compliance under load, the thermal expansion of a workpiece held at 18°C in a shop floor that runs 28°C by midday, the cumulative backlash in a rotary joint that datasheets don't characterize. The spec sheet gives you the repeatability of the arm. The process window gives you the real tolerance.

What I keep noticing is how differently this plays out across production stages. First-article inspection almost always passes on a new line — the robot is fresh, the tooling is fresh, and someone is watching closely enough to catch anything obvious. But run that same process for six months and accuracy drifts silently because nobody is still measuring what matters. The arm still returns to the same positions with the same 0.05mm precision. The parts still don't fit.

This is not a failure of the machine. It is a failure of measurement assumption. The spec sheet was written for the machine, not for your process. It describes the arm's capability in a controlled context. It says nothing about the sources of variance that belong to your conditions — and those sources compound over time.

The decision rule I use: if the cost of the accuracy gap exceeds the cost of measuring it, measure it. If it doesn't, stop inspecting and accept the real tolerance. What you should not do is trust the spec sheet to tell you where the tolerance is.

The spec sheet is a compressed description of performance under a specific set of conditions. It cannot account for what it was not designed to measure. In that sense, it is structurally honest — it is not lying. But it is also not telling you where you are actually at risk.

What matters is knowing which gap you are living in.
