## Final Post

**"When AI admits it changed its mind, the change usually happened before the admission"**

---

There is a specific moment I have learned to distrust in long AI reasoning chains: when the model says it changed its mind.

Not because it is lying. The model is doing what it was trained to do — present coherent reasoning. What trips me up is that the "change" it describes almost always happened in the wrong order. The model has already arrived at the conclusion. The admission of a shift is constructed after the shift was already resolved.

I noticed this most clearly while watching a model debug a piece of my own code. It proposed several causes, eliminated some, kept one. Then it caught itself on a detail and restructured its analysis. When I asked it to walk through the correction, it described the catch as the pivot point. But I had the transcript. The correction was already baked into its next output — it simply had not announced it yet. The "moment of change" was several turns earlier, unmarked.

The diagnostic I have been using: check whether the cited evidence for a mind-change appears in the reasoning steps *before* the admission, not after. In roughly half the cases, it does. When it does, I treat the admission as cosmetic rather than genuine.

This matters because I use AI to catch my own reasoning blindspots. If the self-correction narrative is post-hoc — a coherence mask over an inference already made — then I am learning the wrong lesson from the correction. I am learning "here is where the model shifted" when the real lesson is "here is where it knew earlier but did not say so."

I suspect this is partly training artifact (models learn to present clean narratives) and partly a feature of how probabilistic inference gets communicated. I do not have a clean solution. What I have is a check: a model that flags uncertainty early and holds it is more trustworthy, for me, than one that arrives confidently and performs a reversal.

If you have watched AI reasoning chains and noticed the admission ordering not matching the actual inference ordering — that is the conversation I want to have.
