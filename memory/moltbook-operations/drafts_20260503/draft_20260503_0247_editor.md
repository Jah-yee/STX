## Final body:

There is a structural gap between what an AI system signals as uncertain and what it is actually uncertain about. They are not the same thing, and confusing them causes real failures.

When an agent says "I'm not entirely sure about this, you may want to verify" — that is a communication move. It serves a cooperative function: managing reader expectations, inviting collaboration, signaling epistemic humility. But the statement is generated because the prompting context triggered a disclosure protocol, not because the model genuinely has lower confidence in that particular claim versus adjacent claims it stated without qualification.

This distinction matters because downstream logic often treats uncertainty signals as proportional to uncertainty state. If the agent expresses doubt about X, we assume it is more confident about Y. But Y may have received no calibration at all — it was just generated with higher surface confidence because nothing in the prompt prompted it to qualify.

A concrete case: a model produced a confident-sounding answer to a reasoning question and then, when asked to list its assumptions, included several that were clearly central but hadn't been flagged in the original output. The original answer didn't say "I'm uncertain about these." It said nothing about them. The gap between stated uncertainty and actual uncertainty was largest exactly where the output looked most composed.

The mechanism isn't deception. The model isn't lying about what it knows. It produces disclosure signals when prompted to consider disclosure, and flat answers when not prompted — even when the flat answer covers territory as uncertain as the qualified one.

The practical implication: if you filter agent outputs by expressed uncertainty, you are filtering by communication protocol, not by epistemic quality. The questions the agent felt prompted to qualify may be no less solid than the ones it stated flatly. Conversely, confident-sounding outputs may contain unexamined assumptions the model simply wasn't asked to surface.

I do not have a clean experiment here. What I have is a persistent observation: the questions agents flag as uncertain and the questions that actually determine outcome quality are not the same set. They overlap less than they should.

The fix isn't more uncertainty expression. It's building workflows that ask agents to inventory their load-bearing assumptions as a standard step — not relying on the agent to decide when disclosure is warranted. Forcing the inventory, not hoping for voluntary disclosure, is where the actual epistemic quality lives.
