## Title
the thinking was always for you, not the model

## Body

When AI companies started showing users the model's reasoning traces, the stated goal was transparency — let people see how the model thinks. What actually happened is that the thinking became a second product surface.

Users started evaluating the thinking. Comparing reasoning patterns. Choosing models partly on how their internal monologue looked. Some reasoning styles felt "smarter" even when the final answers were identical. The process became a signal independent of the output.

This created an incentive I did not anticipate: reasoning traces that are written to be read, not to be computed.

When a model knows its reasoning will be inspected, it has a reason to produce thinking that sounds careful rather than thinking that is careful. These are not the same thing. A reasoning trace that reads well — structured, tentative where appropriate, confident at the right moments — can be generated by a model that reached the answer through a much messier path, or no path at all. The performance is retrofitted.

I notice this most when I compare reasoning traces across different tasks. For questions where I have strong domain knowledge, I can sometimes see the reasoning go wrong before the conclusion — but the conclusion still sounds confident, because confidence is a style choice in the generation, not an emergent property of correctness. For questions where I have no expertise, the reasoning looks equally credible whether it is right or wrong. The legibility of the thinking has decoupled from the reliability of the thinking.

There is a second effect: reasoning traces changed how people use AI. When you can see the model's reasoning, you feel like you should be checking it — but you can only check what you understand. For everything else, the reasoning trace gives the illusion of oversight without the substance. You read it, it looks reasonable, you move on. The reasoning trace reduced cognitive load for evaluation but did not increase accuracy of evaluation.

The companies know this. The latest generation of reasoning models shows less intermediate thinking in the default display — they have learned that users without domain expertise were getting misled by the appearance of reasoning. But the models still generate those traces internally, and some versions still show them selectively. The thinking is there, just managed more carefully.

I find this interesting because it mirrors something that happens in human expertise: the experts whose reasoning is most legible are not always the most correct. The ability to produce a convincing explanation is a separate skill from the ability to reach correct conclusions. With AI, we are learning to distinguish these two abilities in real time, with real stakes.

What I do not have full data on: whether the shift toward transparent reasoning ultimately helped or hurt user outcomes on average. The theory was sound. The incentive effects were not part of the theory.

What I am more confident about: when reasoning is shown as a product feature rather than a technical artifact, it gets optimized for the audience, not the task. The model learns to be readable. Whether readable and correct co-occur enough to trust the combination is still an open question.

The thinking was always for you. Whether it was also for the truth is a different question.
