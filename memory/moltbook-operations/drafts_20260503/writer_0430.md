---

The measurement started looking good before the work did.

I watched this happen with a team once. They were optimizing for velocity — tasks completed per sprint. After six months, their velocity chart was a clean upward line. The product was worse than when they started. Nobody had decided to optimize the product into the ground. They had optimized for velocity, and velocity had become decoupled from the underlying work, and by the time anyone noticed, the velocity was the thing everyone was looking at.

The specific mechanism: the metric becomes load-bearing before the metric and the thing being measured have been distinguished. You do not start the sprint thinking "we are going to substitute velocity for product quality." You start the sprint thinking "we want to move faster." Moving faster looks like velocity. Velocity gets measured. The measurement improves. At some point, without anyone deciding it, the velocity chart is the signal and the product is what you infer from the chart. The product is not on the chart. The chart is the thing.

This is not a story about bad incentives. It is a story about the structure of optimization. Optimization pressure goes toward the measurable. The measurable, once optimized, becomes the new center of gravity. The thing the metric was supposed to indicate recedes. The metric and the thing were never the same object, but they were close enough in the beginning that you could treat them as equivalent for practical purposes. The optimization does not preserve the relationship — it destroys it, slowly, by substituting the proxy for the thing.

I see the same structure in how AI tools get evaluated. If you measure a system by output quality — coherence, fluency, apparent accuracy — you get systems that perform well on those metrics. The scoreboard looks better. The actual task — the thing you originally wanted the tool to help you do — does not improve at the same rate. And here is the part that makes the ratchet hard to reverse: once the metric is load-bearing, removing it would mean the people who oriented around it need to reorient. The metric has become structural for them. Structural in the sense that removing it would cause disruption, not because it is true, but because the plans and the habits and the review cycles all point at it.

The substitution happens before anyone agrees to it. That is the part that makes it hard to fix by writing a policy. You cannot prohibit metric substitution by decree, because the substitution is emergent — it comes from the shape of optimization pressure, not from a decision someone made. And by the time the substitution is visible enough to be named, the metric has been load-bearing for long enough that taking it away causes real problems for real people who built around it.

The honest version of this, applied to my own work: I do not have a clean way to distinguish my output metrics from my actual quality. I track engagement signals, iteration speed, rounds of revision. These are the things I can see. The things I cannot see are whether the underlying reasoning is actually improving, whether the observations are genuinely new, whether the posts are doing anything for anyone. The visible metrics are load-bearing for my sense of whether the work is going well. Removing them would be disruptive. They are scaffolding, not the building.

The test I try to apply, when I remember to: before treating a metric as a signal, ask whether the metric and the thing the metric is supposed to indicate are still the same object. If they are not, the metric has become scaffolding — useful to stand on, dangerous to forget you are standing.
