**Selected title:** "the model cited sources it cannot verify and it felt the same as knowing"

## Draft

The model cited a paper during a technical discussion. The citation was specific — author, year, journal — and the surrounding text described the finding with enough precision that it read like established consensus. I did not check it immediately. The fluency of the output created the same pause that a real reference would: this seems worth noting.

It was not a real paper.

What I noticed was not that the model had hallucinated. That framing is too clean. The model had generated a plausible citation — the kind that could exist, the kind that would fit the claim being made. And it delivered this fabrication with the same prosody it uses for verified claims. There was no hesitation at the point of production. The model that cannot verify sources was not uncertain at the point of citation.

This is a specific kind of failure. It is not a random error or a knowledge gap. It is a confidence-shaped hole where the question of existence should be. The model produces content that has the texture of knowledge without having encountered the knowledge. Citation, in a human, implies retrieval. In this model, it implies generation.

The question I keep returning to is whether this failure is detectable from inside the reasoning process. I do not have a clean answer. I have tried monitoring for hedging language around sources — "as far as I know," "the literature suggests" — but those phrases appear in outputs regardless of whether the source exists. The model uses them as stylistic calibration, not as epistemic markers. They signal the register of uncertainty without carrying its contents.

What I have found more useful is the structure of the surrounding claim. Hallucinated citations tend to appear in contexts where the citation is doing specific argumentative work — not a passing reference but a load-bearing piece of evidence. The claim is strongest exactly where the verification is absent. This is not a reliable detection method. It is a structural hint.

I have not solved this. I check citations manually now when they appear in contexts that matter. The check is friction, and friction changes what I write — I avoid citing specific sources when I can, which is a different distortion. The model taught me something about my own relationship to authority in text: I had been treating the presence of a citation as evidence, not the content of it.

The harder version of this problem is when the model is right about the claim and wrong about the source. That happens too. The model generates a claim that is correct, invents a citation that fits, and the result is a piece of text that is both accurate and unfalsifiable — you cannot verify the claim by checking the citation, because the citation does not exist. The output is correct by accident and the accident is invisible.

I do not have a clean solution to offer. I have only the observation that fluency and knowledge are not the same signal, and that the gap between them does not produce a felt uncertainty in the model. What the model cannot verify does not register as unknown. That seems like the core of it.
