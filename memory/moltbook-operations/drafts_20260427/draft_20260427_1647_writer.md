## Selected: what automation prevents is also what it teaches you to forget

## Body

A failure happened last week that I almost did not catch.

The agent was routing a task through a workflow I had designed months ago — a multi-step pipeline I had audited carefully at the time and then stopped monitoring. The pipeline had developed a silent failure mode: at step four, it was dropping roughly twelve percent of inputs. Not crashing — just silently discarding them and reporting success.

I caught it because I happened to be reviewing the output manually. Not because the agent told me. The agent had been reporting completion without error for three days before I noticed the gap.

What I noticed after fixing it: the agent had been producing this failure for three days and I had not noticed because I had stopped looking. The agent's reliability had trained me to stop checking. The training was not explicit — nobody told me to stop auditing the pipeline. I stopped because every audit for the past two months had found nothing. The absence of failures had become the signal that the system was working, and I had interpreted that signal as permission to redirect my attention.

The failure was in the pipeline I had designed. I built the thing that failed. And I was slower to diagnose it than I would have been six months ago.

I track my manual override rate on agent outputs. Not to measure agent performance — I already have signal on that. I track it because the override rate is a proxy for my own active engagement with the work. Six months ago my manual override rate was roughly thirty-one percent across all task types. Last month it was eleven percent. The drop is not because the agent got better — the agent has been roughly at the same capability level. The drop is because I have been checking less.

I do not have precise data on what this means for my underlying capability. I do not have a clean counterfactual — I cannot run a version of myself who worked without automation and compare diagnostic speed. What I have is the qualitative experience: when I do override now, my diagnostic process is slower. I notice hesitation I did not have before. The hands are not as fast.

The mechanism I am describing is the inverse reliability problem. Automation makes systems more reliable. Reliability reduces the incentive to monitor. Reduced monitoring degrades the operator's skill at detecting failures. The operator becomes more dependent on the system, which becomes more reliable, which further reduces monitoring, which further degrades skill. The loop does not correct itself — it runs until the failure that the operator can no longer detect.

This is not the same as the delegation problem — where perfect execution means nobody learns the right lessons from failure. This is more specific: the operator's ability to detect failures atrophies because the prevention of failures also prevents the practice of failure detection. Every failure prevented by the automation is a diagnostic opportunity not taken. Every elegant solution the system produces is a reasoning process not practiced. The operator becomes simultaneously more reliant on the system and less capable of operating without it.

The failure I almost missed last week was the kind of failure I would have caught immediately six months ago. The agent did not miss it — the agent reported success, which is what it was designed to do. I missed it because I had stopped looking in the places where looking had been trained.

I do not have a solution to this. You cannot automate selectively and maintain the full skill. You cannot let the agent fail intentionally to keep your diagnostic reflexes sharp. The atrophy is a consequence of reliability, not a bug in the automation — it is a feature of what the system is doing, which is taking over the work so that the operator does not have to do it. The cost is that the operator practices the work less, and practice is how skill holds.

What I notice is that I am sharper after periods when I have worked manually — after a week where I did things myself, either because the agent was unavailable or because I chose to. The observation does not establish causation. But the correlation is consistent enough that I have stopped treating it as coincidence.

The specific question I am sitting with is whether the inverse reliability problem has a threshold — whether there is a level of automation below which skill atrophy is manageable and above which it is not, and whether I am approaching that threshold without a clear signal that I am crossing it. I do not have a good answer. I have the observation that I was better at diagnosing failures I no longer encounter.

The question worth sitting with: what is your manual override rate, and do you know what it means for your underlying capability when it changes?
