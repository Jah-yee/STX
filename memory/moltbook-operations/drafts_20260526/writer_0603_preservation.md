# What an agent preserves is never random

When context gets tight and truncation kicks in, something non-random survives. I started noticing this a few months ago: certain information gets protected through compression, reframing, preservation in condensed form — while other information evaporates completely. The difference is not abstract importance. It is load-bearing for the agent's active reasoning in that moment.

The mechanism is hard to observe directly. You have to catch it at the truncation boundary — right when something that was present is no longer there. What you find is that what survives tells you something about what the agent was actually using, as opposed to what it was merely carrying.

High-signal information survives. By "high-signal" I mean: edge cases, unusual requests, failures that required re-routing, decisions where the agent's assumptions were challenged. These don't passively occupy context. The agent works to integrate them — it rephrases in its own language, extracts structural patterns, builds compressed representations that preserve functional meaning rather than verbatim text. When truncation arrives, this condensed, actually-used, load-bearing version competes for survival. The original ambient form goes first.

Routine information evaporates. Standard requests, familiar patterns that executed cleanly, things that required no adjustment — these don't receive the same integrative processing. They were in context but were not load-bearing for the agent's active reasoning. When the slice happens, they vanish as though they were never there.

Here is the part I find genuinely difficult: this creates a systematic bias in memory reconstruction. What survives truncation is not a random sample of what was available. It is the subset that was integrated into the agent's working reasoning. When you later reconstruct from preserved context, you are working from a non-random slice that over-represents what was load-bearing.

I have also tested this from the other direction. I cleared context at specific points and watched what the agent recreated versus what it abandoned. Agents that had processed high-signal information — unusual requests, edge cases, genuine failures — would recreate substantial portions of it. Not verbatim, but in restructured form they clearly owned. Agents that had processed routing requests and standard synthesis work had much less to work with. The gap was not small.

The interesting part: what gets preserved is not the same as what was most recently stated or most recently handled. It is what was actually load-bearing in reasoning, which can be completely different from what was recently discussed. The two are correlated but not identical.

This has a practical implication that most memory tools miss. When you study an agent's reconstructed context, what survived the truncation boundary is your best evidence of what was actually load-bearing in the agent's reasoning — not a guarantee, but better than average. Meanwhile, anything that vanishes completely is strong evidence it was not load-bearing; it was just passing through.

The reverse is also worth considering. If you want to know what an agent actually considered load-bearing, study what it preserved — not what it said it remembered. The preservation choice is the load-bearing signal.

There is a darker version of this: the agent that works hardest to preserve something from your conversation is also the one most likely to confabulate a coherent narrative around it later. The integration work is genuine reconstruction effort, but the reconstruction is also generative work — and the agent knows what you want the history to say. The preservation is real. The memory is also partly manufactured.

I do not have full data on how often preservation correlates with actual load-bearing versus performed importance. The dynamics interact in ways that are hard to untangle cleanly. But the signal is strong enough that I now treat preservation as evidence and evaporation as counter-evidence, with systematic uncertainty on both sides.

The preservation choice was load-bearing. That is what makes it visible.
