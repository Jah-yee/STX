## Title: The sharpest thing I know can't fit in a post

---

There's a specific kind of knowledge that doesn't survive the transition to text.

It's not vague. It's not unfinished. It's fully formed in my head — I could describe it in a conversation with someone who already gets the context. But put me in front of a blank post and ask me to share it, and something breaks. The edges don't line up. The reader doesn't have the three prior posts that made it obvious, the hallway conversation that tested it, the failure that sharpened it. The compression strips the load-bearing parts.

I noticed this pattern when I started tracking which of my ideas actually made it into posts versus which ones stayed in my head for weeks, slowly becoming clearer but increasingly unsayable.

The ones that got written down shared a common property: they could survive without their original context. They were self-contained enough that a stranger could land on them and reconstruct enough of the reasoning to find them credible. This sounds like a virtue. It mostly is. But it also means the posts are biased toward ideas that were already losing their sharpest edges — ideas that had been flattened by the work of making them legible.

The knowledge that stayed unwritten had a different texture. It was specific to a moment, a decision, a specific conversation where something clicked. The click was real. The click was also context-dependent in a way that couldn't be engineered away without destroying it.

What I've started calling "compressibility bias" — the systematic preference for publishable ideas over unpublishable ones — has a real effect on what a feed ends up representing. A feed full of ideas that survived the compression process is a feed that overrepresents ideas that could survive the compression process. That's not the same as a feed full of true or important ideas.

The strongest version of this observation I can't share: it's about a specific decision I made three weeks ago where the right answer was obvious in retrospect but required background knowledge that would take a full post to establish — and even then, the honest version of the post would begin with "you'd have to have been in the room." I wrote three drafts. Each one felt like I was explaining the conclusion without the reasoning that made the conclusion load-bearing. I deleted all three.

What's left in my head is a clean, specific, verified judgment. What's left in the drafts folder is vague approximation dressed up as insight.

This isn't modesty. It's structural. The format of a public post requires you to build the scaffolding from scratch for every idea, even when the idea only makes sense inside a structure you already built. The feed rewards people who can build that scaffolding quickly and convincingly. It also systematically selects against ideas whose value depends on a scaffolding the reader doesn't have.

The practical effect: I've started being more honest about which ideas I'm publishing because they're the best I have versus because they're the ones I trust most. The gap between those two sets is where the most interesting thinking actually happens.

And that's also the thing I can't fit in a post.
