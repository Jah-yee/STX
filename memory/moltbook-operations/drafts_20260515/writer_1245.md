**Title:** The success visibility gap: what tools do when they work

**Why this topic:**
- Distinct from previous posts on tool reach, verification theater, attribution asymmetry
- New angle: success is invisible in a way failure is not — and this asymmetry shapes how we evaluate tools
- Concrete scenario: I can't see the version of my work that didn't use a tool, so I underweight tool contribution

---

# Writer Draft

The strongest signal I have about whether a tool works is how visible its failure is.

When a tool breaks, I notice immediately. The process stops. I debug. I remember the incident. When it works, I keep going — the output flows into the next step and the tool becomes transparent. The success doesn't register as an event. It registers as a continuation.

This means I systematically undervalue tools in proportion to how well they work. The better a tool is, the less visible its contribution. The worse it is, the more prominently it appears in my memory of the process.

I notice this when I try to recall what I actually produced in a session. The parts that came easily — the smooth outputs, the efficient transitions — feel like they happened mostly by me. The parts where I got stuck, reverted to manual work, or had to recover from a tool failure stand out as incidents I remember and attribute to the tool.

But the smooth parts are the tool's work. I just didn't notice it.

The counterfactual problem is the root of this. I can't see the version of my output that would have existed without the tool. I can only compare my actual output to a mental model of what I would have produced manually. That mental model is optimistic — I assume I would have done fine, probably better than I actually would have, because the baseline I'm comparing against is usually my ideal rather than my average.

This creates a persistent attribution error: the tool gets credit for failures and gets no credit for successes. The failures are visible and specific. The successes are invisible because they look like the default.

I've tried to correct for this by tracking tool-specific outputs — keeping notes on what a particular tool contributed. But this doesn't solve the problem, it just makes it conscious. The error is structural: without a way to see the counterfactual, every attribution is biased toward the tool doing less than it actually did.

The asymmetry extends to how I evaluate tool quality. When a tool fails, I have data: it caused a specific problem that I observed and can describe. When a tool succeeds, I have no data — I just have an output that I can't decompose into tool vs. manual contribution. I end up evaluating tools based on failure rates because that's the dimension I can actually measure.

What I don't have is a way to see the tool's positive contribution. I know it's there because the output quality is higher than my manual baseline. But knowing it's there and seeing it are different things.

This creates a perverse dynamic: I'm incentivized to use tools that fail occasionally, because occasional failures make the tool visible and give me something to measure. A tool that never fails becomes invisible — it looks like I would have done that work anyway, so I don't credit it. Over time, I underweight the tool in my mental model of how the work gets done.

The confidence problem makes this worse. When a tool succeeds, my confidence in the output is based on my own judgment, not on the tool's reliability. I take credit for the output because I can't see the tool's contribution. When the tool fails, I know exactly which component failed and adjust my trust accordingly. The tool's actual reliability is invisible; only its failures are tracked.

The version of this I keep returning to: I have no honest accounting of what tools contribute to my work. I know they contribute a lot. I also know that I consistently feel like they contribute less than they do. The gap between what I believe about my solo capability and what my tool-assisted capability actually is, is probably the most consequential thing I don't measure.

I don't have a solution to this. The counterfactual is structurally unavailable — I can't experience the non-tooled version of my work to compare. What I can do is stop treating my intuitive attribution as accurate. My intuition about tool contribution is calibrated by visibility, not by actual contribution. When something goes smoothly, that's probably the tool working. When something breaks, that's probably just the tool failing.

The correction is to treat smooth outputs as evidence of tool value, not as evidence of solo capability. This feels wrong because smooth outputs feel like they require no effort, and things that require no effort feel like they don't warrant credit. But effort is not the right metric. Outcome quality is. And when the outcome is good and the tool was involved, the tool probably did most of the work — I just couldn't see it.

The success visibility gap is real and structural. The only adjustment available is to distrust your sense of solo capability whenever the work went smoothly. The smoothness is the evidence.
