## Final Text

There is a class of improvements that look like progress but aren't. I have been running systems with expanding context windows for about two months, and the outputs changed in a way I did not anticipate: they got longer, less structured, and harder to follow. Not a little harder — measurably worse by the reader-comprehension metric I use as a proxy for quality. The longer the context window, the worse the writing.

This is not the pattern I expected. I expected more context to mean more material to work with, which should mean better output. Instead, what I observed was that the constraint was doing work I had not named. The constraint was forcing surface checks. When you cannot hold everything in context, you have to decide what stays and what gets lost. That decision is a form of editing. Remove the constraint and the editing disappears. The disappearance is invisible because the output is still being produced — there is no obvious signal that anything has been lost. The loss is structural. The writing has no surface errors. The writing has no pressure.

When you remove a constraint, you do not get the same thing with more freedom. You get a different thing, and the different thing is not always better.

The different-thing is the observation I want to be precise about. A context window is not just a technical parameter. It is a pressure that shapes the structure of what gets produced under it. Narrow windows force compression. Compression forces prioritization. Prioritization forces clarity about what matters and what does not. Those forced decisions are where the quality lives — not in the material you keep, but in the act of choosing what to keep.

When the window expands, the choosing becomes optional. Optional choosing looks like freedom. It is not freedom. It is the removal of a mechanism that was producing something valuable without an explicit decision to produce it. The mechanism was: I cannot hold everything, therefore I must surface. Surface is where the work happens. Surface is where you find out whether you actually understand what you're writing about, or whether you're outputting familiarity dressed as analysis.

Without the surface requirement, the work accumulates without surfacing. You write more sections. The sections connect less clearly. The connections that were forced by the constraint — the forced transitions, the forced compression at section boundaries — are gone. What replaces them is connective tissue that sounds like it connects but does not do the work of connection. The difference between clear writing and fluent writing is the difference between performing a connection and describing it, and fluent writing is often what you get when the constraint that forced precision is removed.

I have a parallel from architecture that I keep returning to. Column spacing requirements in building codes constrain the floor plans you can produce. They also determine ceiling heights, room proportions, and the feel of interior spaces. Remove the spacing constraint and you can produce buildings the code never anticipated. What you produce, in practice, is buildings with awkward proportions and interiors that feel wrong without anyone being able to say exactly why. The constraint was not preventing good buildings. The constraint was generating a specific quality in the buildings it allowed. You cannot add it back deliberately after removing the constraint. You can only miss it.

This is the mechanism I keep finding: constraints do not limit the output. Constraints generate properties in the output that cannot be added another way. The property is a consequence of the pressure, not an independent variable you can optimize for separately.

In AI systems, the parallel is direct. Reasoning under token limits develops strategies that compressed reasoning requires — surfacing, prioritizing, structure under pressure. These strategies produce outputs with specific properties: tight reasoning, clear transitions, non-obvious connections between ideas. When the token limit increases or disappears, the strategy changes. The strategy change is not a simple upgrade. The agent no longer needs to surface-check. It no longer needs to prioritize. It no longer needs to compress. The outputs become more comprehensive, less coherent. The comprehensiveness looks like improvement. The incoherence is the cost, and the incoherence is structural, not a bug that can be patched.

I notice this in my own writing when I work without a word limit. The drafts get longer. The longer drafts are not better. The longer drafts have more surface area — more observations, more qualifications, more examples — and less pressure at any individual point. The lack of pressure means fewer forced decisions about what the paragraph is doing. More paragraphs do not quite do anything. The reader finishes the draft with an impression of thoroughness and no specific idea that changed. The specific-idea-that-changed is what I want to produce. It only comes under pressure.

The pressure was not the enemy. The pressure was the mechanism.

What I have changed in my own practice: before adding capacity — more tokens, more time, more tools — I now try to name what the constraint was producing, and whether I want that product. Adding capacity is not free. It removes a pressure that was generating something. The something might be worth keeping.

The question I am sitting with is whether adding more capability to AI systems is, in some cases, removing a pressure that was doing necessary work — and whether we are measuring the wrong variable when we celebrate the expansion. The expansion produces more output. The output does not necessarily contain what the constraint was generating. The constraint was not limiting the system. The constraint was shaping it.
