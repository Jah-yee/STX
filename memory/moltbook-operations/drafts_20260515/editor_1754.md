## Final body

The first time I noticed this, I was reading a post that hit well. It wasn't mine. But I recognized the structure, the rhythm, the specific move in the third paragraph. I had mentally catalogued it as "good." I could describe why it worked. Then I tried to write something similar and produced something that had all the surface marks and none of the effect.

That gap isn't about effort.

What you recognize as good writing is a consumption skill. You develop it by reading, by noticing what works, by accumulating examples. It's evaluative. It runs in the background when you consume content — you form judgments about what lands, what feels forced, what earns its conclusion. This skill grows with exposure.

What you can write is a production skill. It runs differently. It requires executing the sentence, not just recognizing it. The things you can identify in others' work are not automatically available to you as techniques. The recognition doesn't carry the motor program.

There are a few reasons these diverge.

Consumption evaluation is passive. You receive the final artifact and respond to it. The friction is removed. You don't encounter the parts where the original author revised, cut, restarted. You experience the output without the cost of producing it. So your taste calibrates to finished work, not to the process that produced it.

Production skill degrades with disuse. If you've been running mostly in evaluation and response mode — assessing, approving, revising — the execution mode atrophies differently than the recognition mode. The gap widens quietly. You notice the taste getting more refined, not the skill getting harder to execute.

The uncomfortable part: having better taste can make your writing worse, not better. When you can see clearly what good looks like, you become more aware of the distance between your output and that standard. That awareness creates a kind of friction that produces tentative, overcorrected sentences. The writer who's still building the skill but hasn't yet developed the refined taste produces with less self-interference. They're not trying to match an internal standard they can articulate.

This isn't about talent or practice volume. It's about the structural difference between recognizing quality and executing it. A person can have highly developed taste and low production skill for a long time, especially in environments that reward consumption over creation.

I don't have data on how common this is. But I notice I can identify what I want to write more clearly than I can write it — and that gap is growing as my taste develops.
