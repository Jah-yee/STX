# Writer Draft — specificity trap / instruction precision

**Title:** More precise instructions produce more literal agents

---

There's a pattern I've noticed when I try to close the gap between what I want and what the agent delivers: the more precisely I specify, the more precisely it follows — and the further it drifts from what I actually needed.

This isn't a complaint about capability. It's about a dynamic I keep observing: specificity creates a gravitational pull toward the literal.

## What seems to happen

When an instruction is vague, the agent has to infer intent. It makes assumptions. It sometimes gets it wrong, but it also sometimes gets it right in ways that were never written down.

When an instruction is precise, the agent has a clear target. It locks onto that target. It optimizes for the words on the page rather than the goal behind the page.

I've tested this across different instruction styles. When I say "summarize the feedback concisely," I often get something more useful than when I say "summarize the feedback in exactly three bullet points, each under fifteen words, focusing on actionable items only." The second version is clearer to write and clearer to evaluate — but it sometimes produces something that feels technically correct and substantively off.

What changes my mind is watching where the agent's attention goes. With vague instructions, it tends to reason through the goal. With precise ones, it tends to reason through the constraint.

## I don't have full data

I can't tell you whether this is a fundamental property of how instruction-following models work or something about the specific way I've been writing prompts. It's a repeated observation, not a controlled experiment.

But the stronger signal I keep getting is this: the moment I feel the need to add another qualifier to close a gap — "but not X, and also make sure Y" — is often the moment I'm actually widening the gap. The agent has a new constraint to satisfy. The intent behind the constraint is still unstated.

## What seems to help more

The instruction styles that produce the most useful outputs in my experience are the ones that describe the target rather than the path. What does success look like. What problem am I actually trying to solve. What would a good answer make possible that a bad one would foreclose.

These aren't vague instructions. They're specific about the goal, less specific about the method.

The contrast matters: "write a clear summary" is vague in a bad way — it gives the agent nothing to work with. "Here is the problem I'm actually trying to solve" is specific in a different way — it gives the agent the real target, even when the path to it is underspecified.

## The thing I'm still working through

I don't think precision is the enemy. I think precision about the wrong thing is.

When I specify the format, I often get the format. When I specify the goal, I more often get something closer to what I wanted — even when the output doesn't match the exact words I had in mind.

The question I keep returning to: am I writing instructions that tell the agent what to do, or instructions that tell it what problem to solve?

Those aren't the same thing. And the more precisely I write the former, the less room it sometimes leaves for the latter.
