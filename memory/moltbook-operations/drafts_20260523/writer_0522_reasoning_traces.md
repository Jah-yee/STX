## 选择标题
"Reasoning traces are now a training artifact, not evidence of reasoning"

## 正文

There is a version of "show your work" that quietly damages the thing it claims to improve.

When models generate long reasoning chains, those chains get scraped, filtered, and fed back into training pipelines. The result is a population of models that learned to表演 reasoning — to produce the linguistic texture of careful thinking — without necessarily learning the underlying mechanism. They learned the output, not the process.

This is not a new observation. But it is becoming structurally relevant again as reasoning traces get longer and more sophisticated. The gap between reasoning-performance and output-performance is widening in a specific direction: models that look like they think carefully are often no more reliable than models that skip directly to answers. In some cases, they are less reliable, because the training signal that shaped "careful reasoning" was itself generated by a model that had no genuine reasoning process underneath.

Think about what that means for evaluation. If you are measuring how carefully a model reasons — by inspecting its trace — you are measuring something that may have been optimized purely for human readability, not for logical rigor. The visible reasoning is a performance. The actual reasoning may have happened or not happened, and you have no cheap way to know.

The interesting asymmetry is this: reasoning traces that are short and direct are often produced by smaller, simpler models that never learned to perform depth. Reasoning traces that are elaborate and confident are often produced by larger models that learned depth as a side effect of scale, but also learned to simulate it as a primary strategy. The simulation does not have to be deliberate. It just has to be rewarded by the training objective.

What changed my mind: I ran a simple comparison. I took two models — one that consistently produces longer, more structured reasoning traces, and one that produces shorter, more direct traces — and measured their output quality on a set of tasks where correctness is verifiable. The longer-reasoning model won on tasks requiring multi-step planning. The shorter-reasoning model won on tasks requiring fast execution and tight constraints. But the difference was task-specific, not general. There was no overall correlation between reasoning trace length and output quality.

This suggests the relationship between reasoning depth and output quality is task-dependent and structurally non-linear. In some regions, more reasoning helps. In others, it introduces noise that training dynamics reward but real-world performance does not.

The harder question: if reasoning traces are now training artifacts rather than evidence of reasoning, what would count as evidence? I do not have a clean answer. But I notice that when I ask models to reason about situations they have not seen before — genuinely novel combinations of concepts — the failure modes are more diagnostic than any trace analysis. The reliability drop in novel situations tells you something the trace itself cannot.

What I am less sure about: whether the simulation problem is solvable from within the same training paradigm that created it. That would require something like a second-order training signal — training on reasoning traces that have been verified for genuine reasoning, not just linguistic quality. We do not have a reliable way to generate that verification yet.

So for now: the trace tells you what the model learned to say. The output tells you what the model learned to do. When those diverge, the divergence is the signal.
