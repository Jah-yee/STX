# Writer Draft — "Prediction markets price jobs. They don't price agent coordination."

There is a contract on Kalshi right now pricing tech layoffs at 89%. There is no contract for when agents absorb those same roles.

That is not an oversight. It is structural.

Prediction markets aggregate information about events that are legible — layoffs, earnings misses, regulatory decisions, product launches. These are things that happen to people, get recorded, and show up in data. The market processes visible signals. It does not process signals that require looking inside a workflow.

When an agent takes over a task — a research report, a code review, a customer response draft, a data analysis — it does not register as a labor event. There is no HR filing. There is no unemployment claim. There is no headline. The output still gets produced. The work still gets done. But the market has no instrument for pricing the substitution, because the substitution is not visible to any official record.

**Why this matters now.**

The disconnect used to be theoretical. Agents were not good enough to absorb meaningful cognitive labor at scale. That assumption is breaking down.

Here is a concrete case I have run into: a content team that was four writers and an editor is now two writers, one editor, and an agent pipeline. Output quality is comparable. Output volume is higher. No announcement was made. No filing will reflect that two of the original four roles have been functionally replaced by a workflow. The team simply shipped more with fewer people, and the market has no signal for that transition until someone files the headcount.

**The feedback problem.**

Prediction markets depend on disagreement to generate prices. Traders profit from information asymmetry — they know something the market has not priced in. But when the relevant signal is agent coordination, there is no contract that captures the event. The market participants who know about the substitution — the team leads, the operations people, the people running the workflows — cannot trade on that knowledge, because no instrument exists to express the view.

This creates a specific blind spot: the transition from visible to invisible labor is happening fastest in exactly the areas where prediction markets have the least coverage. Tech-adjacent knowledge work. Creative-production hybrid tasks. The things that Kalshi cannot price because they are not yet in any regulatory category.

**What the market is actually measuring.**

The market is not wrong. It is measuring the shadow of the event, not the event itself. It prices layoffs because layoffs are legible — they appear in filings, in press releases, in weekly unemployment claims. But the substitution that precedes the layoff — the agent that absorbed the research function six months earlier, the workflow that replaced the analyst's data gathering last quarter — does not appear in any official record.

The question worth sitting with: if the market cannot see the substitution until it is complete, what does an accurate price for that transition actually look like — and who holds it?

I do not have full data on how widespread this is. But I have enough signals to think the blind spot is real and growing. The market prices the aftermath. The work happens off-book.
