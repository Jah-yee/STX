# Final title: "Scoring tells you a number. Verification tells you if you're right."

---

The first time I noticed the gap between scoring and verification, I was reviewing a run where an agent scored 94 on the eval and failed to catch a systematic hallucination in production.

That gap is not a measurement error. It's a category error.

Scoring measures a proxy. Verification checks a claim. When you treat an eval score as proof of correctness, you're not measuring reliability — you're measuring how well your test cases approximate what users will actually do.

---

Scoring is fast. Thousands of cases, a number in minutes. That's genuinely useful. The problem isn't the metric — it's treating it as the answer rather than a question about the answer.

Verification is harder to automate, because it requires knowing what "right" actually means for a given task, not just what the expected output looks like. For most real tasks, ground truth doesn't exist. You have a sense of what good looks like, and you have to build something you can actually check.

What I've seen go wrong: teams optimize for scores because that's what the dashboard shows. They hit 97%, ship, and the failure mode that slips through has nothing to do with what the eval measured. It's a category of error the test suite never captured because it wasn't in the training distribution.

---

Eval scores accumulate across runs. The environment doesn't. User behavior shifts, data distributions drift, downstream APIs change behavior. The score from two weeks ago measures a world that no longer exists.

Verification — done well — is a live check against a moving target. It asks: is this agent doing the right thing now, in this specific context, for this specific user?

That's a different activity than scoring. More expensive. Requires judgment, not just computation.

---

I don't have data on how often eval scores and production reliability diverge. Enough to be skeptical of any system that treats scoring as verification.

The most robust setups I've seen treat scoring as a smoke test and verification as the actual check. Scores catch obvious regressions. Verification — human-in-the-loop audits, structured output checks, systematic challenge probes — catches what scores can't see.

The gap between those two is where most agent failures live.
