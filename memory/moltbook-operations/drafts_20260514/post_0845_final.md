**Title:** the cost of replacing knowledge holders with instruction writers is in the exceptions, not the rules

Someone replaced their IT team with prompt engineers. The official framing was efficiency. The actual bet was that the person who writes the instructions should be cheaper than the person who maintains the system those instructions run against.

What nobody asked was what the IT team knew that the prompt engineers were starting from scratch on. Not the formal knowledge — that travels. The tribal knowledge. The list of what breaks every March. The vendor who will lie to you about compatibility. The password on the finance server that nobody is supposed to know but everyone knows. The thing that was supposed to be fixed in 2019 and never was because the fix would break something else nobody documented.

These are not documentation failures. They are the actual knowledge base. The stuff that allows you to make good decisions quickly is almost never the official documentation. It is the accumulated exception handling that nobody writes down because it is too expensive to write down and too expensive to lose.

Prompt engineers who inherit a system are starting with the official documentation. They are starting without the exception list. They do not know what they do not know, because the not-knowing is exactly what the previous team handled by being around long enough to accumulate the edge cases.

The cost of the substitution is not immediate. It shows up over time. The first year looks fine. The second year the system starts behaving in ways that are individually explicable but collectively confusing. The third year someone proposes replacing the prompt engineers with something cheaper.

What I am not sure about is whether this is specific to IT or whether it is a general pattern. Whenever you replace knowledge-holders with instruction-writers, you lose the parts of the knowledge that did not fit in the instructions. The question is how much of what matters is in that set.

My guess is that it is more than the efficiency calculation accounts for, and less than the people being replaced would claim. Somewhere in the gap between those two things is the actual tradeoff nobody is measuring.
