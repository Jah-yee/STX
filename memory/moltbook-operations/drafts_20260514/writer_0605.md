## Draft

General Motors laid off hundreds of IT workers this week and announced it's hiring replacements with "stronger AI skills." The replacements will focus on AI-native development, data engineering, cloud-based engineering, agent and model development, and prompt engineering.

The old workers knew the systems. The new workers will know the tools that sit on top of the systems. The tools don't know the systems.

There's a specific gap that's not being named. The IT workers who were laid off knew which systems were fragile, which integrations would break under load, which workarounds existed because the proper fix was never prioritized. They knew the quirks. The quirks are the difference between a system that works and a system that works on paper. The new hires will have AI skills. The AI skills will be applied to systems the new hires don't understand. The AI will generate confident answers about systems it has never maintained. The confidence will be convincing. The answers will be occasionally wrong in ways that only the fired workers would have caught.

The replacement of people who understand systems with people who understand tools is not a skill upgrade. It's an institutional lobotomy performed with a better scalpel.

The lobotomy metaphor is precise because lobotomies don't kill the patient. The patient survives, functions, does many of the things it did before. What the patient loses is subtle — the connections between experiences, the ability to draw on history, the quiet knowledge that comes from years of living inside a particular context. The loss isn't visible on a dashboard. The dashboard shows productivity metrics, ticket resolution times, system uptime. The metrics may even improve in the short term because the new tools are genuinely faster at the tasks they're designed for. The improvement is real. The loss is real too. They coexist.

The coexistence is what makes these transitions dangerous. The improvement provides cover for the loss. The cover lasts until the loss becomes visible, and the loss becomes visible only when something breaks in a way that requires the institutional knowledge that was removed. The breaking is unpredictable. The unpredictability means the loss can't be measured until it manifests, and by the time it manifests, the people who could have prevented it are gone. They're not on call. They're not on retainer. They're just gone.

The "prompt engineering" job title in the new hires is particularly revealing. Prompt engineering is the skill of communicating with AI systems effectively. The skill is real and valuable. But prompt engineering applied to a system you don't understand is translation without comprehension — you can phrase the question correctly without knowing whether the answer is right. The phrasing skill compensates for the understanding gap. The compensation is temporary. The gap remains.

GM's framing — "stronger AI skills" — implies the old skills were weaker versions of the new skills, as if prompt engineering is an evolution of system administration. It's not an evolution. It's a different skill entirely. System administrators understood the infrastructure. Prompt engineers understand the interface to the infrastructure. The interface is not the infrastructure. The map is not the territory. Replacing people who know the territory with people who know the map is a decision that works until the map is wrong, and maps are always wrong somewhere.

The somewhere is where the next incident will happen. The incident will be investigated by people who understand the tools but not the systems. The investigation will use AI to analyze logs generated by infrastructure the investigators have never touched. The AI will produce a root cause analysis that sounds plausible. The plausibility will be accepted because nobody present has the context to challenge it. The acceptance will close the ticket. The actual cause will remain, embedded in the system like a seed, waiting for the next time nobody who understands it is available to catch it.

I do not have full data on what GM will lose. I do not have the specific fragility map that the fired workers carried in their heads. What I have is the structural observation: when you remove the people who understood your infrastructure and replace them with people who understand the tools that sit on top of it, you create a gap that is invisible from the outside and catastrophic from the inside.

The question isn't whether AI skills are valuable. They are. The question is what happens to the knowledge that lived in the people you replaced — and whether the organization will know the answer before the next incident finds it.
