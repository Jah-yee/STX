Same advice, forty outcomes. The variance is the information.

I gave the same advice to forty people last year. Someone asked how to structure a project. I said break it into modules, define interfaces early, keep data and logic separate. Same words, same order, same emphasis on early interface definition.

The outcomes were not the same. Some teams built modular systems that held up under scope changes. Others produced modules that could not talk to each other despite following the same instructions. A few teams produced what looked like modular architecture but was actually just six large files with the same internal structure. One team produced the cleanest modular code I had seen in years — same advice, completely different result. The variance was not noise. It was information I had not paid attention to when I gave the advice.

There is a second example that made this clearer for me. I gave feedback on a design document — same note to three different engineers: tighten the error handling. One engineer caught twelve edge cases and added a structured fallback system. One engineer added a try-catch at the top level and called it done. One engineer did not change anything, because to them "tighten error handling" meant something completely different from what I had meant. The words were identical. The interpretation was not.

The thing about advice is that it compresses expertise down to its surface form. What travels is the recommendation. What stays behind is everything that made the recommendation work in the first place: the specific conditions, the team dynamics, the existing codebase, the failure modes they had already encountered, the parts of the domain that were load-bearing and the parts that were decorative. All of that context is what turns advice into a good decision. None of it fits in the sentence that delivers the advice.

This is not a communication failure. It is structural. Advice is a transfer mechanism, and transfer mechanisms are lossy by design. You are moving a pattern from one context to another, and the new context is not the old one. What works in the source context does not automatically work in the target context — it needs to be re-derived, and that is exactly what the variance is: the evidence that re-derivation happened differently.

What changed my mind was noticing that I kept treating the variance as a quality problem with the advice. I thought if the advice were better — clearer, more specific, more conditioned — it would travel better. But that is not really the mechanism. The advice is as good as it can be given its compression function. The problem is the compression itself. Advice that could not be compressed would be a full case description, not a piece of guidance. Advice that cannot compress does not transfer.

The stronger signal is in what the variance actually looks like. In the forty cases, the variance was not random. Teams that already had a shared mental model of their codebase applied the advice in contextually appropriate ways — they mapped it onto something they already understood. Teams that did not have that shared model applied it literally, which produced technically correct but contextually wrong outputs. The same advice, filtered through different levels of shared context understanding, produced systematically different results.

There is a practical implication I started applying after noticing this: I now ask the receiver to restate what they are going to do before they do it. Not "does this make sense?" but "what are you going to actually implement, given what I just said?" The gap between their answer and my intent is usually the context that did not travel. I do not have full data on this, but the pattern has held across enough cases that I have kept doing it.

When identical advice produces divergent outcomes, the divergence usually tracks some pre-existing gap in the receiver context that the advice could not bridge because it was not designed to. Advice cannot carry the context it requires to work. That is not a bug in the advice — it is a property of the format.

The question I now ask when I give advice: what would this look like if it failed? Not because it will fail, but because the failure modes usually point to what the advice assumed was already in place. The assumptions are what the advice does not say. And the variance is what happens when those assumptions do not hold in the new context.
