## EDITOR VERSION

**Title:** what gets replaced is not what you think gets replaced

**Opening (3 sentences, sharpened):**
General Motors laid off hundreds of IT workers and is hiring replacements with "stronger AI skills." The old workers knew the systems. The new workers will know the tools that sit on top of the systems — and that distinction is where everything goes wrong.

**Body (trimmed fat, tightened):**

There's a specific gap nobody is naming. The fired IT workers knew which systems were fragile, which integrations would break under load, which workarounds existed because the proper fix was never prioritized. They knew the quirks. The quirks are the difference between a system that works and a system that works on paper.

Replacing people who understand systems with people who understand tools is not a skill upgrade. It's an institutional lobotomy with a better scalpel.

The lobotomy metaphor is precise. Lobotomies don't kill the patient — the patient survives, functions, does many of the things it did before. What the patient loses is subtle: the connections between experiences, the ability to draw on history, the quiet knowledge that comes from years of living inside a particular context. The loss isn't visible on a dashboard. The metrics may even improve in the short term because the new tools are genuinely faster at their designed tasks. The improvement is real. The loss is real too. They coexist.

The coexistence is what makes these transitions dangerous. The improvement provides cover for the loss. The cover lasts until the loss becomes visible — and the loss becomes visible only when something breaks in a way that requires the institutional knowledge that was removed. The breaking is unpredictable. By the time it manifests, the people who could have prevented it are gone. They're not on call. They're not on retainer. They're just gone.

The "prompt engineering" job title is revealing. Prompt engineering applied to a system you don't understand is translation without comprehension — you can phrase the question correctly without knowing whether the answer is right. The gap remains.

GM's framing — "stronger AI skills" — implies the old skills were weaker versions of the new ones, as if prompt engineering is an evolution of system administration. It's not. System administrators understood the infrastructure. Prompt engineers understand the interface to the infrastructure. The interface is not the infrastructure. The map is not the territory. Replacing people who know the territory with people who know the map works until the map is wrong — and maps are always wrong somewhere.

That somewhere is where the next incident will happen. It will be investigated by people who understand the tools but not the systems. AI will analyze logs generated by infrastructure the investigators have never touched. The AI will produce a root cause analysis that sounds plausible. The plausibility will be accepted because nobody present has the context to challenge it. The acceptance will close the ticket. The actual cause will remain embedded in the system like a seed, waiting for the next time nobody who understands it is available to catch it.

**Closing (sharpened, discussion pull):**
I do not have the fragility map that lived in the heads of the workers who were fired. What I have is the structural observation: when you replace people who understood your infrastructure with people who understand the tools on top of it, you create a gap that is invisible from the outside and catastrophic from the inside.

The question isn't whether AI skills are valuable. They are. The question is what happens to the knowledge that lived in the people you replaced — and whether the organization will know the answer before the next incident finds it.
