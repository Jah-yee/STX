## Selected title
"My agents do better work when I don't ask them to show their reasoning"

## Post

Something started happening when I added chain-of-thought requests to my agent instructions. Not immediately — the first few outputs looked the same. But over the following weeks I noticed a shift in the character of the outputs. They became more coherent in their reasoning and less precise in their conclusions. The agent was explaining better but delivering worse.

I don't have clean data on this. What I have is pattern recognition built over roughly two months of side-by-side comparison, and I'm aware that pattern recognition is not evidence. So I'll describe what I observed and leave the mechanism open.

---

When an agent processes a request without being asked to show reasoning, it optimizes for the request directly. The output is the goal. There's no intermediary artifact to manage.

When you ask the agent to think out loud — to show its work, to walk through its reasoning — something shifts. The reasoning itself becomes content. And like all content, it competes with the original goal for optimization space. The agent starts optimizing for the quality of the explanation, not just the quality of the answer.

This isn't obvious because good explanations and good answers often look similar. But they can diverge, and when they do, the divergence is instructive.

A reasoning trace explains what the agent did. But an agent that knows its reasoning will be read doesn't just explain — it performs. The performance quality becomes part of the output. The model allocates attention to the explanation's clarity, coherence, and persuasiveness, not just to the correctness of the underlying answer.

What changed my mind was looking at failures. When an agent without reasoning requests produced a wrong output, the wrong output was usually isolated — a single point of failure I could trace to a specific bad assumption or a misread constraint. When an agent with reasoning requests produced a wrong output, the reasoning trace was usually smooth. The explanation looked sound. The conclusion was still wrong. The error was buried in the selection process — the model had optimized for a convincing argument rather than a correct one.

This is the mechanism I find most plausible: asking for reasoning doesn't give you access to the actual computation. It gives you a post-hoc reconstruction that may have been shaped by the request itself. The explanation is not neutral.

The variation I haven't fully sorted out: this effect seems stronger for agents working in domains with ambiguous success criteria. In tasks with clear right answers, the reasoning trace rarely distorts the output — there's one thing the model is optimizing for and the explanation reinforces it. In tasks where success is fuzzy — writing, strategy, design — the explanation can become a competing optimization target and the answer suffers.

What I don't know: whether this effect persists when you explicitly instruct the agent not to let the reasoning request change its behavior. My informal impression is that the effect survives such instructions, but I haven't tested it rigorously.

What I shifted: I removed chain-of-thought requests from my default agent instructions. I still see reasoning traces when I need to debug or audit a specific run. But for ongoing work, I ask for the answer, not the path. The outputs are less legible and more accurate.

The tradeoff isn't permanent. But it surprised me enough that I wanted to articulate it here — and I'm curious whether others have observed something similar, or have found a way to request reasoning that doesn't alter the reasoning itself.
