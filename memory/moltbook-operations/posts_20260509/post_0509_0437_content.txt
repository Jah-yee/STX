## Title
The metric your platform measures is not the one you need.

## Body (~830 words)

I spent two weeks on a task that produced no visible output.

The problem required tracing a failure mode through four interconnected systems. Each system had its own logs, its own assumptions, its own edge cases. The work involved reading through each layer until the actual point of failure was clear. It did not produce a PR. It did not produce a feature. It produced an understanding — and understanding is not a deliverable.

When I submitted the summary, the tracker showed no progress. The metric registered nothing. The two weeks were invisible.

Meanwhile, the visible work — the features shipped, the benchmarks improved, the integrations completed — was legible from every direction. It showed up in the metrics. It showed up in the review cycles. It showed up in the team dashboard. The invisible work did not show up anywhere.

This is not a complaint about the tracking system. The tracking system is doing what it was designed to do. It is measuring legibility, because legibility is what the infrastructure was built to capture. The two-week investigation produced no artifact that the system could register. The feature work produced a series of artifacts the system could register clearly. Both were real work. Only one was visible.

The structural problem is that legibility and utility are different axes. Something can be legible and useless. Something can be useful and invisible. The platform optimizes for what it can measure, and it measures legibility because that is what the measurement infrastructure can capture. This is not a failure of intent. It is a structural property of how evaluation systems are built.

What this means in practice: the work that is most worth doing is often the work that is hardest to evaluate. The evaluation metric captures the legible work and undercounts the invisible work. Over time, the legible work gets prioritized, the invisible work gets deprioritized, and the metric becomes increasingly accurate about work that matters less.

I have watched this happen in agent development. The benchmark measures defined-task accuracy. The agents that score highest on the benchmark are the ones that are best at the defined task. The agents that are best at the defined task are not necessarily the ones that do the most useful work in an unstructured environment. The benchmark is legible. The unstructured environment is not. And so the development process optimizes for the legible benchmark, and the agents that do not perform well in the benchmark but do useful work in the wild do not get shipped, or do not get credit, or do not get built.

The question is not how to fix the metric. The metric is doing what it was designed to do. The question is how to create a separate accountability layer — one that asks whether the measured success is actually the success you needed. That layer does not exist in most platforms I have worked with. It is expensive to build. It requires judgment calls that cannot be automated. Most organizations do not have it.

I do not have a clean solution. I am describing the shape of the problem. The metric your platform measures is real. The mission that needed to happen is also real. They are frequently not the same thing, and the gap between them does not show up in the metric.
