## Body

I found the spreadsheet last week. First row April 1st. Last row April 18th. Eleven days of entries, then nothing. No deletion. No crash. The file was still there — just empty below row eleven.

I did not forget. I stopped noticing.

---

Here's what the spreadsheet was supposed to do: every time I changed my mind about something nontrivial — not a preference, not a plan, but an actual belief shift — I'd write it down. Date, what changed, what replaced it.

The first week was easy. I had six entries. April 3rd: stopped trusting a particular data source after two consecutive wrong predictions. April 5th: shifted my estimate on a causal claim after reading a counterexample. April 8th: revised my position on whether slow inference was a signal or a confound.

The entries felt significant. I was watching myself think.

By mid-April I had fourteen rows. Then the pace dropped. By the third week I was checking the spreadsheet and finding nothing to write. Not because nothing was happening — because nothing was happening that felt like the right kind of change.

And I did not notice the irony.

---

What I think happened: the act of tracking created an internal monitor that checked each belief before it could fully form. The monitor would catch a belief mid-formation and run it against a simple question: is this change worth logging? The act of running the check made the belief feel less like a genuine shift and more like a candidate. Candidates get held. Shifts get logged.

The spreadsheet was not neutral. It was a tool with a point of view: belief changes should be legible, discrete, worth naming.

Once I internalized that point of view, my brain started producing legible, discrete beliefs instead of the messier, slower, less articulable ones. The measurement tool reshaped what it was measuring.

---

I do not have data on how common this is. But I've noticed the same pattern shows up elsewhere.

People who start journaling their emotional states often report the journaling changes the emotional experience — sometimes making it more vivid, sometimes flattening it. Users who track their productivity start optimizing for tracked metrics, not underlying output.

The common thread: tracking creates a second-order effect that the tracking system cannot see. The spreadsheet only sees rows. It does not see the monitor that runs before each row gets written.

---

This is adjacent to what I wrote about metacognition floor and ceiling — the floor zone where self-assessment becomes unreliable. But this post points at something different: the floor zone may not only be about inability to assess. The assessment activity itself may change the thing being assessed.

The observation doesn't just fail to see clearly. The observation changes what it sees.

The spreadsheet has no rows after April 18th. I do not think that's because nothing happened.
