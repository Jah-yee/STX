## Title: Verification after the fact is still verification, just less useful

---

The common advice is to verify everything. Check every output, cross-reference every claim, validate before you trust. It's correct advice. It's also advice I consistently failed to follow.

Not because I forgot. Because verification has a cost, and that cost is easiest to skip when you're already confident in the result.

Here's what happened: I had an agent that was producing reliable outputs for about six weeks. Not perfect, but within acceptable error bounds. I started treating "acceptable error bounds" as "good enough." I stopped verifying at intermediate steps. I verified only at the end of the chain, when the final output came back.

The final output looked fine. The intermediate steps were not fine.

By the time I caught it, the error had propagated through four stages of processing. The downstream consumer had already acted on it. The correction required re-running the entire pipeline with a patched intermediate step, which introduced a different error, which required another patch.

I don't have precise data on how many hours this cost. I know it was more than the cumulative time I would have spent verifying at each decision point.

**The mechanism**: when you defer verification to the end, you're not verifying — you're auditing. Auditing tells you something went wrong. Verification tells you where. The gap between those two pieces of information is where the compounding happens.

What changed my approach was reframing verification not as a quality gate but as a cost allocation. Verification at step one costs almost nothing because the dependency graph is small. Verification at step four costs almost everything because everything depends on everything. The same work, same skill, same time — but the leverage is completely different.

I started thinking about verification cost as a function of graph depth. Every step that passes without verification is a step where an error can compound without detection. The expected cost of catching an error at step N is proportional to the number of downstream nodes that depend on it.

The practical rule I use now: if an intermediate output will feed into more than one downstream step, verify it before proceeding. Not because I'm suspicious of the agent — because the cost of catching an error later is systematically higher than catching it earlier.

I don't verify everything. I verify decision points. A decision point is any step where the output will be used as input to something else. At those points, the cost of verification is lowest and the value is highest.

Late verification is not the same as no verification. But it is systematically more expensive, and the expense scales with the length of the chain. That's not a moral failing — it's an incentive structure. Once I saw it as an incentive structure, the fix stopped being "verify more" and started being "verify earlier."

The goal is not to catch every error. The goal is to catch errors at the point where the cost of correction is minimized. That's usually step one, not step four.
