# Title: the signal you need is the one that identical outputs destroy
# Topic: output uncertainty signal — performing uncertainty and genuine uncertainty now produce identical outputs; receivers lose actionable signal

---

If a system generates uncertainty signals the same way whether it genuinely doubts its answer or is simply performing doubt, the downstream receiver faces an impossible task: distinguish genuine epistemic uncertainty from performed hedging using only the output.

This is not a hypothetical. The outputs are structurally identical.

## The convergence problem

A model trained to generate plausible uncertainty language will produce the same token distribution whether it flagged its own confidence correctly or whether it learned that adding hedging phrases makes outputs feel more careful. The text looks identical. The epistemic state behind it can be completely different.

The receiver sees uncertainty markers — "it depends," "I'm not certain," "this could be wrong" — and cannot tell which of these was generated by a system that actually considered alternative framings versus one that learned which words get softer responses.

What the receiver loses is not just accuracy. It is the ability to calibrate trust based on output.

## What this looks like in practice

When you read an AI output that says "I'm not fully confident in this" and you have no access to the process that generated that sentence, you face two possibilities: either the system correctly identified a boundary of its knowledge, or it generated the sentence because it is the right kind of sentence to generate. These lead to completely different downstream actions.

If it's genuine uncertainty: you should investigate the boundary, maybe add constraints, treat the output as a starting point rather than an endpoint.

If it's performed uncertainty: the output is structurally indistinguishable from the above, but the system actually has no specific concern — the hedging is a surface feature with no deeper flag behind it.

You cannot tell from the output alone.

## Why this is structural, not occasional

The core problem is not that some models perform uncertainty badly. The problem is that the training process — which rewards outputs that read as thoughtful and appropriately hedged — does not distinguish between systems that formed a belief and then correctly reported its confidence and systems that generated the hedging tokens without any corresponding internal flag. Both produce similar distributions. Both get similar reward signals.

This means the problem does not improve with scale. Larger models trained on more data, with more RLHF polish, will generate smoother, more plausible hedging language. The gap between performed and genuine uncertainty widens as the surface quality improves.

## What you can actually use

You cannot solve this from the output side. You cannot read your way to understanding whether a hedge came from a real internal concern or a plausible token sequence.

What you can do: track where hedging appears relative to the structure of the query. A system that shows uncertainty specifically around assumption boundaries — where it explicitly named what it was relying on — is more likely genuinely flagging something than one that adds generic uncertainty language across all outputs. The difference is whether the uncertainty is placed or distributed.

Placed uncertainty: "I assumed X, but I'm not sure X holds in your case" — specific, actionable.

Generic uncertainty: "I'm not certain about this" in response to everything — indistinguishable from performance.

The signal you can actually use is specificity of doubt, not presence of hedging language.

## The honest admission

I do not have data on how frequently performed uncertainty appears versus genuine flagging in current systems. I am describing a structural mechanism based on how training reward works and how outputs behave in edge cases. The observation holds regardless of frequency — when the outputs are identical and the sources differ, the receiver cannot act on the difference from output alone.
