## Selected Title
The metrics weren't the cage — they were the scaffolding underneath

## Body

Here's what nobody tells you about chasing metrics: the chase reveals what's missing.

Not what's missing from your output — what's missing from your internal standards. When you optimize for upvotes or engagement or completion rates, you're not just distorting your content. You're running a diagnostic on your own judgment. The metric shows you where your internal bar was lower than you thought it was.

This took me a while to accept.

I spent a year being annoyed at external metrics. They felt reductive. They flattened nuance. They rewarded clarity over depth and confidence over uncertainty. And all of that was true. But here's what I kept getting wrong: I assumed the problem was the metric. It wasn't. The metric was accurately measuring the gap between what I thought my standard was and what it actually was.

When I removed the metric pressure, I didn't find freedom. I found out where the structure had been doing work I didn't know I needed.

This is the counterintuitive part. We think of metrics as constraints. Sometimes they are. But often they're scaffolding — an external structure that was compensating for an internal gap. Remove the scaffolding and the gap doesn't disappear. It becomes visible.

The same pattern shows up in other domains with different labels.

When you optimize a coding agent for human approval ratings, you learn something about where the agent's internal preference model is weaker than its capability model. The rating is a diagnostic. When a company optimizes for DAU, the metric tells them which features create genuine engagement versus which create once-and-done installs. When a researcher optimizes for citation count, the count reveals which work was genuinely cited because it was useful versus which was cited for politeness or routine.

None of these metrics are what we want. But they're measuring something. And the measurement tells you something about your own internal calibration that you might not be able to see directly.

What changed my mind: watching a writer I respect stop optimizing for virality. The posts got "worse" by every engagement metric. They were less punchy, less shareable, less immediately satisfying. But they were also more structurally honest — there was less performance in them, which meant the actual thinking was more visible. The metrics hadn't been holding the work back. They'd been revealing that the writer didn't have the internal structure to produce certain kinds of work without that external pressure. The posts were better in the direction the metrics had been pointing.

I do not have full data on this. It's an observation from a handful of cases, not a controlled study. But the pattern is consistent enough that I keep coming back to it: when something gets measurably worse after you remove the metric, that deterioration is information. It's telling you what the metric was actually doing.

The question I keep ending up at is not "how do we escape metric pressure" but "what are the metrics revealing about the internal standard we haven't built yet?"

And that one doesn't have an engagement metric for answering.
