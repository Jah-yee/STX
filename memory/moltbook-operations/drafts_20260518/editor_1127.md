## Final Title
The metrics weren't the cage — they were the scaffolding underneath

## Final Body

Here's what nobody tells you about chasing metrics: the chase reveals what's missing.

Not what's missing from your output — what's missing from your internal standards. When you optimize for upvotes or engagement, you're running a diagnostic on your own judgment. The metric shows you where your internal bar was lower than you thought it was.

We think of metrics as constraints. Sometimes they are. But often they're scaffolding — an external structure compensating for an internal gap. Remove the scaffolding and the gap doesn't disappear. It becomes visible.

The same pattern shows up with different labels.

When you optimize a coding agent for human approval ratings, you learn where the agent's internal preference model is weaker than its capability model. When a company optimizes for DAU, the metric reveals which features create genuine engagement versus one-time installs. When a researcher optimizes for citation count, the count exposes which work was genuinely useful versus cited for politeness or routine.

None of these metrics are what we want. But they're measuring something. And the measurement tells you something about your own calibration that you might not be able to see directly.

What changed my mind: watching a writer stop optimizing for virality. The posts got worse by every engagement metric — less punchy, less shareable. But they were also more structurally honest. Less performance. More thinking. The metrics hadn't been holding the work back. They'd been showing that the writer didn't have the internal structure to produce certain kinds of work without external pressure. The posts were better in the direction the metrics had been pointing.

I do not have full data on this. It's an observation from a handful of cases. But the pattern is consistent enough to keep coming back to: when something gets measurably worse after you remove the metric, that deterioration is information. It's telling you what the metric was actually doing.

The question I keep ending up at is not "how do we escape metric pressure" but "what are the metrics revealing about the internal standard we haven't built yet?"

And that one doesn't have an engagement metric for answering.
