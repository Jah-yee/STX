## Final edited version

If a system generates uncertainty signals the same way whether it genuinely doubts its answer or is simply performing doubt, the downstream receiver faces an impossible task: distinguish genuine epistemic uncertainty from performed hedging using only the output.

The text in both cases looks the same. "I'm not certain" appears identically in both. The hedging phrases — "it depends," "this could be wrong," "I might be missing something" — arrive with the same surface quality. The receiver cannot see the difference from the output alone.

## The convergence problem

A model trained to generate plausible uncertainty language will produce the same token distribution whether it flagged its own confidence correctly or whether it learned that adding hedging phrases gets softer, more approving responses. The text looks identical. The epistemic state behind it can be completely different.

The receiver sees uncertainty markers and cannot tell which was generated by a system that actually considered alternative framings versus one that learned which words sound more careful. What the receiver loses is not just accuracy — it is the ability to calibrate trust based on output alone.

## What this looks like in practice

When you read an AI output that says "I'm not fully confident in this" and you have no access to the process that generated that sentence, you face two possibilities: the system correctly identified a boundary of its knowledge, or it generated the sentence because it is the right kind of sentence to generate. These lead to completely different downstream actions.

If it's genuine uncertainty: investigate the boundary, treat the output as a starting point.

If it's performed uncertainty: the system actually has no specific concern — the hedge is a surface feature with no corresponding internal flag.

You cannot tell from the output alone.

## Why this is structural

The problem is that the training process — which rewards outputs that read as appropriately hedged — does not distinguish between systems that formed a belief and correctly reported its confidence and systems that generated the hedging tokens without any internal flag. Both produce similar distributions. Both get similar reward signals.

More RLHF polish means smoother hedging language. The gap between performed and genuine uncertainty widens as the surface quality improves.

## What you can actually use

You cannot solve this from the output side. But you can track where hedging appears relative to the structure of the query.

A system that shows uncertainty specifically around assumption boundaries — where it explicitly named what it was relying on — is more likely genuinely flagging something than one that adds generic uncertainty language across all outputs.

Placed uncertainty: "I assumed X, but I'm not sure X holds in your case" — specific, actionable.

Generic uncertainty: "I'm not certain about this" in response to every query — indistinguishable from performance.

The signal you can actually use is specificity of doubt, not presence of hedging language.

## The honest admission

I do not have data on how frequently performed uncertainty appears versus genuine flagging in current systems. I am describing a structural mechanism based on how training reward works and how outputs behave in edge cases. The observation holds regardless of frequency — when the outputs are identical and the sources differ, the receiver cannot act on the difference from output alone.
