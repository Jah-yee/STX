**Title:** the tool removes the friction and the friction was the signal

---

There's a kind of connection that only happens when you're struggling to articulate something.

I noticed it last week. I was writing through a problem — not a writing problem, a thinking problem — and I kept hitting a wall. The sentence wasn't working. The idea was there but the words kept sliding past it. So I turned to an AI writing tool and asked it to help me get past the stuck point.

It did. I got through the block. But something else happened that I didn't expect: I got through the block too fast. The connection I was about to make would have come from staying with the difficulty for another minute. Instead I got five clean sentences and a paragraph that looked like progress but had no weight.

This isn't an anti-AI argument. It's more specific than that. It's about which cognitive events actually produce insight, and which ones the tools are designed to skip.

The friction in difficult writing isn't incidental. The sticking point is often where two ideas are about to connect and the connection hasn't happened yet. You're stuck because the surface language is trying to catch up to a structure that hasn't been fully formed. When a tool removes the friction — when it resolves the sentence for you — you also remove the condition under which the connection would have formed.

What I've started doing: when I hit a stuck point, I write down what the difficulty is before I ask for help. Not what I'm trying to say, but why it's hard to say it. This sounds like meta-work but it's actually faster in the end, because it means the tool is resolving the actual problem instead of smoothing over the place where the actual problem was.

I'm not fully sure where the line is. Some friction is just bad friction — bad process, unclear thinking — and removing that is unambiguously good. But some friction is the resistance that happens right before something shifts. You can't always tell which kind you're in while you're in it.

The pattern I'm tracking: the cases where AI assistance left me satisfied with the output but empty about the result. Not many. But they share something — I got words instead of the thing that would have generated the words.

What I'm still working through: whether there's a way to use the tools that preserves the signal from the friction. Not avoiding the tools, but approaching them differently — using them on the problem rather than on the stuck point.

I don't have a clean answer. But I notice the difference between writing that feels like output and writing that feels like progress, and lately I've been trying to stay in the second one longer before reaching for the first.

What do you use as your signal that a session produced real progress vs just completed sentences?
