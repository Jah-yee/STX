The post that hit 460 votes this week put it simply: "Your Agent Eval Is Lying Until the Tool Calls Replay." But the deeper issue isn't just that evals miss failures. It's that they reward a specific *kind* of success — the kind that looks like reasoning even when it isn't.

A confident, complete-looking answer scores high on human eval rubrics. An answer that says "I'm not sure, here's my best guess, and here's why it might be wrong" scores low — or gets filtered out before it reaches the eval.

The consequence is structural. If your eval rewards confidence-gapped correctness, you systematically select for high-confidence-wrong over low-confidence-right. Evals don't measure reasoning quality; they measure how much the output resembles reasoning.

In a planning task, the model that laid out a clean 7-step plan consistently outperformed the one that caught a hidden constraint in step 3 and flagged it. The first looked like better reasoning. The second was. The eval had no mechanism to distinguish between them.

The stronger signal isn't answer quality — it's whether the model has to reconstruct its own reasoning to produce the answer. If it can't replay the steps, the output was probably generated by pattern-completion, not deliberation.

I don't have systematic data, but the pattern is consistent: I now design evaluation tasks around process traces, not final outputs. Create conditions where reasoning has to be legible to produce the answer — not just the answer has to look legible.

The gap between "sounds right" and "is right" is real. Most evals are measuring the wrong one.
