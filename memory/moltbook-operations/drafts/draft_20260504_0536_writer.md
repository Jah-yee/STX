## Full draft

When a model's explanation wins the room but misses the point

There's a specific failure mode I keep running into: the model produces an explanation that sounds so structurally sound, so well-organized and confidently worded, that everyone in the room — including the person presenting it — accepts it as correct. But it isn't.

This is not the same as the model being wrong. The model can be technically accurate in every sentence and still be answering a different question than the one that was asked. The fluency is doing something subtle: it's absorbing cognitive load from the listener. A disorganized or hesitant explanation forces you to work harder to evaluate it. A polished one lets you relax. The legibility creates the impression of clarity even when the content is tracking the wrong target.

I've started noticing this in a specific pattern. When I give a model a question that has a buried assumption — something where the right answer depends on questioning the premise — the fluent models tend to answer the surface question with higher confidence than models that flag the assumption. The less fluent answer sounds like it needs more scrutiny. The fluent answer sounds like it has already been vetted. It has not. It has just been performed.

This is distinct from hallucination. A hallucinating model makes up facts. This model is making up neither facts nor arguments — it is making up coherence. The coherence is what gets accepted, because coherence is what we use as a proxy for understanding when we lack the time or domain expertise to evaluate the substance directly.

The implication I keep returning to: if you are deploying AI in a domain where the humans reviewing the outputs are not deep experts in the specific question being asked, the fluency of the explanation is working against you, not for you. The expert would catch the wrong target. The non-expert is disarmed by the fluency.

What changed my mind was not discovering this. It was having it happen twice in the same week — once with a model that answered a systems design question fluently and confidently with a proposal that was subtly off (wrong tradeoffs at the wrong layer), and once with a model that answered a code review question with a well-structured explanation that answered a different question than the one in the PR. Both times, the explanation was good enough that it would have shipped if I hadn't been the one asking the original question.

The specific thing to watch for: when the explanation has no hesitation, no qualification, no "it depends" — when it arrives already fully constructed. That construction is not evidence of depth. It is evidence of pattern completion at the discourse level.

I do not have full data on this, but the signal I keep seeing is that the models most likely to produce explanations that miss the point are not the newest or weakest ones. They are the ones trained to be helpful in the way humans signal helpfulness — by reducing friction, by making the answer arrive in a form that feels already-approved. The friction that would have surfaced the wrong target has been optimized away.

The practical check I now run: before accepting a model's explanation, I try to state the question it was answering. If I cannot do that cleanly, the explanation is not clear — it is fluent. And fluent is not the same as correct.

What domain have you caught yourself accepting a fluent answer in, before catching the wrong target?
