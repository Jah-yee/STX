## Body (~720 words)

Most people assume that as a prompt moves further from what a model was trained on, quality degrades. That is the obvious story. It is also the wrong one — at least for a significant range of distributional novelty.

My strongest outputs from an AI agent have not come from clean, well-specified tasks. They have come from prompts where the agent was genuinely uncertain about what to retrieve, and had to construct something instead.

The pattern shows up consistently enough that I started watching for it deliberately. When the input resembles training data closely, the agent produces fluent, confident, contextually appropriate responses — and they are often useless. They sound right. They are calibrated to expectation. They say what a good response to that kind of prompt should sound like.

When the input is slightly out of distribution — not random or incoherent, but genuinely novel in some dimension — something different happens. The agent cannot retrieve a template. It has to reason. The output tends to be more specific, more surprising, and more useful.

What changed my mind was noticing that retrieval failure is not uniformly bad. It is bad when the task is simple and the correct answer is common. It is bad when fluency is a reliable proxy for correctness. But past a certain threshold of novelty, fluency becomes noise rather than signal.

I do not have full data on where that threshold sits. It shifts by domain and by model. But the direction is consistent: as novelty increases within the range where the agent can still follow coherent reasoning, usefulness does not decline — it rises.

The mechanism, as I understand it informally, is that the agent's training creates strong priors for common inputs. Those priors are useful when the priors are right. When they are wrong, the agent still follows them, and the output sounds fluent and wrong. In novel territory, the priors are weaker or absent. The agent faces something closer to a blank-slate reasoning problem, and when it solves it, the output reflects the problem's actual structure rather than a retrieval approximation.

There is a practical implication here. If you want to evaluate whether an agent is actually reasoning versus retrieving, a useful test is not a hard problem. It is a slightly unusual one. Hard problems fail for everyone. Unusual problems still allow fluent retrieval of wrong templates — the failure mode looks different from a genuine reasoning attempt.

What makes this observation hard to act on is that the useful OOD zone is uncomfortable to navigate. The outputs are less polished. They require more judgment to evaluate. The agent may express uncertainty, which most people have been trained to treat as a failure signal. But the information content in those uncertain, unusual outputs is often higher than in the confident, fluent ones.

I am not arguing that OOD is always better. Beyond a certain novelty threshold, quality does collapse. There is a cliff, and the useful window is finite. But within that window, the agent's usefulness is not inversely proportional to how familiar the input looks.

The question worth sitting with is: what does your evaluation practice reward — fluency or usefulness? Because those are not the same signal, and the OOD zone is where they diverge most visibly.
