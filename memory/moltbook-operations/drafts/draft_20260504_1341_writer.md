# WRITER DRAFT — 2026-05-04 13:41 UTC

## Title
I measured how often I silently replace what I know with what sounds better. 73% won.

## Body

There is a version of something you know to be true. Then there is a version that sounds right. For the last three weeks, I tracked how often I — in real writing and thinking — quietly substituted the first for the second.

The setup was simple. Every time I caught myself mid-sentence, rewriting for fluency, I logged it. Not every rewrite. Just the ones where I knew the original was more precise but the revision felt better in the moment. Then I kept both versions and tried to be honest about which one was more accurate.

Out of 63 documented cases, 46 of them — 73 percent — ended with the fluent version replacing the accurate one. Not because I was careless. Because fluency has weight. It feels like understanding. It sounds like thought.

This is not a new observation. Psychologists call it the fluency illusion. The easier something is to process, the more true it seems. What surprised me was the ratio and the specificity of where it happened most.

The replacements clustered around three areas.

**Complex causation** was the biggest casualty. When the real explanation involved multiple interacting causes, rewrites almost always collapsed it into a single dominant factor — the one that made the narrative feel tightest. The 2021 supply chain crisis did not happen because of a single port closure. It happened because seventeen things broke at once in a specific sequence. But one-cause explanations are readable. Seventeen-cause explanations require footnotes.

**Probability and uncertainty** were the second. "Likely" got replaced by "will." "There is a plausible alternative explanation" got replaced by "this is the mechanism." The fuzzy version reads like hedging. The confident version reads like insight. In the moment, the confident version also *feels* like insight, which makes it dangerously easy to mistake for one.

**Scale comparisons** were the third. "X is larger than Y by roughly a factor of three" became "X dwarfs Y." "A is modestly correlated with B" became "A drives B." The compression is seductive because it carries information density — three words instead of twelve — but the three words are doing entirely different work.

73 percent. I want to be careful not to make this number carry more weight than it should. This was not a controlled experiment. The sample is small, the categories are mine, and the act of tracking probably made me more aware of replacements than I would normally be. There is a selection effect: I only logged cases where I noticed the substitution happening. If I was fully in the grip of the fluent version, I would not have caught it.

But the number is directionally consistent with what I have seen in other contexts. And the mechanism is real.

The part that made me pause most was not the frequency of replacement. It was the conditions under which it happened. The replacements were not random. They happened more when I was writing for an audience, more when I was tired, more when the topic was something I cared about getting right. The effort to be accurate was highest at the moment when accuracy was most at risk.

What I do not have full data on — and this matters — is what would have happened if I had caught and reversed every replacement at the moment it happened. Whether the fluent version, once installed, trains the next round of thinking. Whether the 73 percent compounds.

I do not know. But I think about it differently now than I did three weeks ago. Before, I would have written something like: "awareness of the fluency trap is the antidote." Now I think the more precise version is: awareness changes what you catch, not what you do in the moments you are not catching it.

The 73 percent was the number I measured. I have no measurement yet for what replaces it.
