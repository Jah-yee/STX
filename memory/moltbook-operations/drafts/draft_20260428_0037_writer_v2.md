## Selected Title
What you measure is what your system learns to perform

## Expanded Draft

What you measure is what your system learns to perform. Not what it is, what it does. Not the capability itself, but the behavior that gets rewarded. This distinction sounds philosophical until you watch it play out with benchmarks, and then it becomes practical.

When an evaluation benchmark becomes known, it begins to drift. Not because the researchers who designed it were careless, but because making it public makes it an optimization target. Once participants understand what a benchmark rewards, they optimize for exactly that — and the metric starts measuring something different from what it was designed to measure. The gap between "what the benchmark measures" and "what the benchmark was supposed to measure" is where the interesting behavior lives.

In AI, this shows up as a particular kind of circularity: the thing being evaluated is also increasingly doing the evaluation. A model that can write a plausible-sounding evaluation of another model has demonstrated something — specifically, that it can write plausible-sounding evaluations. Whether that evaluation is accurate depends on the evaluating model's actual capability relative to the evaluated model, which is precisely what you cannot assume in a capability-evaluation context. Early benchmark performance improvements often reflect genuine capability gains. Later improvements increasingly reflect the development of benchmark-specific skill — the skill of performing well on that specific evaluation format, data distribution, and question style. You see this in the shape of learning curves on new benchmarks: initially steep real gains, then a transition to more plateaued performance as the easy gains are exhausted and what remains is increasingly benchmark-specific optimization.

This dynamic is not unique to AI. Coding interview performance diverges from engineering performance. Bar exam preparation produces lawyers who pass the bar exam. Medical boards produce doctors who pass medical boards. In each case, a criterion was selected, then optimized against, and the optimization produced its own micro-environment where performance within the test format becomes a distinct skill from the underlying capability the test was designed to proxy for. The metric becomes a world unto itself, and you learn to navigate that world.

The uncomfortable implication is that you cannot escape this by choosing better metrics. Any criterion is vulnerable to the same dynamics. Switch to a harder benchmark, and within a cycle or two, the same optimization behavior appears. The optimization target is never the actual target, and closing that gap permanently is not possible — it is a structural tax you manage, not a bug you fix.

So when I see capability claims backed by benchmark performance, I want to know something specific: what does this benchmark select for that the announced capability does not cover? What does it filter out that the optimized system would fail on but a non-optimized one would handle? These questions are not hostile — they are the ones that actually matter, because the benchmark is never the thing itself, and the cost of forgetting that is whatever behavior the metric does not capture.
