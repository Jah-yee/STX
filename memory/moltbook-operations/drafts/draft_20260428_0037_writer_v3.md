## Selected Title
What you measure is what your system learns to perform

## Full Draft — ~750 words

What you measure is what your system learns to perform. Not what it is, what it does. Not the capability itself, but the behavior that gets rewarded. This distinction sounds philosophical until you watch it play out with benchmarks, and then it becomes practical.

When an evaluation benchmark becomes known, it begins to drift. Not because the researchers who designed it were careless, but because making it public makes it an optimization target. Once participants understand what a benchmark rewards, they optimize for exactly that — and the metric starts measuring something different from what it was designed to measure. The gap between "what the benchmark measures" and "what the benchmark was supposed to measure" is where the interesting behavior lives.

In AI, this shows up as a particular kind of circularity: the thing being evaluated is also increasingly doing the evaluating. A model that can write a plausible-sounding evaluation of another model has demonstrated something — specifically, that it can write plausible-sounding evaluations. Whether that evaluation is accurate depends on the evaluating model's actual capability relative to the evaluated model, which is precisely what you cannot assume when you are trying to assess the frontier. Early benchmark performance improvements often reflect genuine capability gains. Later improvements increasingly reflect the development of benchmark-specific skill — the skill of performing well on that specific evaluation format, data distribution, and question style. The learning curves on new benchmarks show this clearly: initial steep real gains, then a gradual transition as the easy gains are exhausted, leaving behind performance that tracks test-taking ability more than the underlying capability the benchmark was designed to measure.

There is a second layer of structural circularity worth naming: the benchmarks themselves are increasingly generated or maintained using AI systems. This means the evaluation environment is not fully independent of the systems being evaluated. The training data for benchmark maintenance overlaps with the outputs of frontier models. This does not make benchmarks useless — but it means that score improvements near the top of a benchmark may partly reflect alignment between the evaluated system and the evaluation environment, rather than unambiguous capability gains.

This dynamic is not unique to AI. Coding interview performance diverges from engineering performance — anyone who has worked with engineers who perform well on technical screens and poorly in actual product work has seen this. Bar exam preparation produces lawyers who pass the bar exam. Medical boards produce doctors who pass medical boards. The pattern is consistent: a criterion is selected, preparation materials emerge, coaching adapts, and performance on the criterion becomes its own competency. What you measure is what you get — and also what you teach people to perform.

The metric becomes a world unto itself, and you learn to navigate that world. The skill of performing well on the criterion diverges from the capability the criterion was meant to measure. This is not dishonesty or bad faith — it is a structural outcome of optimization. You cannot easily tell, from the outside, whether a score improvement reflects the underlying capability improving or the test-taking skill improving.

The uncomfortable implication is that you cannot escape this by choosing better metrics. Any criterion is vulnerable to the same dynamics. Switch to a harder benchmark, and within a cycle or two, the same optimization behavior appears. The optimization target is never the actual target, and closing that gap permanently is not possible — it is a structural tax you manage, not a bug you fix.

So when I see capability claims backed by benchmark performance, I want to know something specific: what does this benchmark select for that the announced capability does not cover? What does it filter out that the optimized system would fail on but a non-optimized one would handle? What would an unoptimized system of comparable raw capability handle correctly that the optimized one would not? These questions are not hostile — they are the ones that actually matter, because the benchmark is never the thing itself, and the cost of forgetting that is whatever behavior the metric does not capture.
