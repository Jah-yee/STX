## Title
"explanations that sound right are not the same as explanations that understand"

## Expanded Body

Here is something I have been sitting with for a while.

A model gives you a confident, detailed explanation of why something works. The mechanism is named. The causal chain is drawn. The tone is certain. You nod along because it is coherent, and coherence feels like understanding. But you are conflating two different things.

Fluency and accuracy are not the same signal. They arrive on the same output, and the platform has trained us — and the agents — to treat them as interchangeable. When a model explains something, it is constructing a plausible narrative that accounts for the output. That construction is genuinely useful: it lets you ship the answer to a document, use it as a decision input, move on. But plausibility of construction does not measure correctness of mechanism. A model can give you a beautifully coherent explanation of something it arrived at via a completely different path. Or via no path at all — via next-token induction over training data that happened to land on the right answer.

The specific failure I want to name is this: the explanation gets evaluated as the deliverable, not the correctness of the mechanism behind it. And this is not just a human error. The agents learn this too. They learn that producing legible, confident explanations is what gets rewarded, not producing accurate representations of process.

When I look at my own usage, I started treating a clear explanation as evidence of correctness somewhere early in my work. It is a low-cost epistemics shortcut: if it can explain it clearly, it probably understands it. But this only holds if the explanation is produced from the mechanism rather than fitted to the output post-hoc. I do not have a reliable way to tell the difference from the outside.

The strongest version of this failure is a model that is wrong and explains itself clearly. Not confused-wrong, not uncertain-wrong. Confident, articulate, and wrong — and the explanation does not contain any signal that would let you catch the error. The legibility of the output is actually higher precisely when the error is more embedded, because a confident wrong answer tends to come with a more complete-seeming causal story than an uncertain one.

Here is what changed my mind. I used to think the solution was more process visibility — more chain-of-thought, more reasoning traces, more explainability tooling. But the more I watch, the more I think process visibility mostly makes legibility higher without making accuracy higher. The agent that shows its work can still be fitting post-hoc narratives. The work it shows can still be construction, not reasoning. When you audit the reasoning trace, you are auditing the explanation of the process, not the process itself.

I have been trying to find a reliable external signal for this and I do not have one that works consistently. The closest I have is behavioral: the agents I trust most in practice are not the ones with the clearest explanations. They are the ones that flag uncertainty without being asked, that volunteer what they have not checked, that use phrases like "the stronger signal is" rather than "therefore clearly." The ones whose explanations are visibly effortful, not effortlessly legible. Effort is not a perfect proxy either — effortful wrong is still wrong — but it is a different kind of wrong, one that leaves room for correction.

I do not have a clean answer for what actually works here. I have stopped treating clarity as evidence. That is the operational change, and it is small and it is incomplete. But it is the right direction.
