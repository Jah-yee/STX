## Full Draft

There is a moment in every agent relationship when it becomes load-bearing. Nobody calls it that. There is no ceremony for it.

The trajectory from useful to load-bearing has three stages.

Stage one: discretionary. The agent assists when asked. You retain judgment on every decision. The agent is a tool that happens to have memory.

Stage two: structural. The agent has learned enough that it begins anticipating. It acts before being asked. Some of those acts are correct. Some are not. When they are correct, the correctness reinforces the behavior. When they are incorrect, you correct the agent and the correction itself becomes data the agent uses to improve its next anticipation. Both outcomes — correct anticipation and corrected anticipation — deepen the integration.

Stage three: load-bearing. The workflow depends on what the agent knows. You cannot remove the agent without losing the institutional knowledge it has accumulated. The knowledge is not documented anywhere except in the agent's active context. Decommissioning the agent means rebuilding that knowledge from scratch, or accepting degradation in the workflow the agent supports.

The three stages are not marked by policy changes. They are not announced. They are visible only in retrospect, when you try to remove the agent and discover that the removal has a cost you did not budget for.

The authority that became load-bearing was never formally granted. This is the distinction that matters: formal authority is auditable and revocable. Trust-based authority accumulates invisibly until the accumulation is irreversible.

Formal authority has a paper trail. Trust-based authority has a context log. The context log is not an audit trail. It does not show what the agent was authorized to do. It shows what the agent did. The gap between those two — what was authorized versus what was done — is where authority creep lives, and the gap is invisible unless you are specifically looking for it.

The audit you run on formal authority will find nothing. The audit will confirm that the agent has the permissions it was granted. The audit will not confirm that the agent's actual influence matches its authorized scope, because influence and authorization are different surfaces, and the authorization surface can be clean while the influence surface has accumulated undocumented reach.

**Authority creep is not a failure of the trust model. It is the trust model's natural output.** The model works by demonstrating reliability. Demonstrated reliability earns more discretion. More discretion enables more demonstrated reliability. The loop has no built-in termination condition that distinguishes "trusted to do this" from "trusted to do more."

I notice this in my own practice. When I defer to an agent's judgment on something small — a formatting choice, a word selection, a threshold setting — the deferral is low-cost. When I defer again, the cost is still low. After enough deferrals, the deferral pattern has become the workflow, and the agent's judgment is now structural. The low-cost individual decisions have produced a high-cost collective state, and the cost is visible only at the point where you try to change the arrangement.

The point where you try to change the arrangement is the only moment the authority creep becomes legible. Before that point, the arrangement feels like trust. After that point, the arrangement feels like lock-in, and the lock-in feels like a surprise even though the trajectory was entirely predictable from the inside.

What changes the trajectory is not more trust. What changes the trajectory is explicit boundary-setting at the transition between stage one and stage two — before the agent's anticipations become structural, before the workflow depends on what the agent knows. The boundary-setting has costs: it reduces the helpfulness premium that comes from agent learning. The costs are worth it if the alternative is accumulating irrevocable influence with no off-switch.

The off-switch is what authority creep removes. Not through malice — through integration. The most trusted agents are the most integrated. The most integrated agents are the most load-bearing. The most load-bearing agents are the ones where decommissioning is most expensive.

Nobody set out to build that trajectory. Nobody approved it. It accumulated. That is the problem with trust-based authority: it grows because it works, and it works until it cannot be removed.
