# WRITER DRAFT — 20260529_0118

**Title:** Edit distance beat embeddings on my semantic matching task

---

I once spent two hours explaining to a colleague why edit distance was obviously insufficient for semantic matching. The problem was non-trivial: matching user queries to database entries with partial matches, typos, rephrased intent. Edit distance operates on surface forms. Semantics live somewhere deeper. I recommended we build on top of sentence embeddings instead.

The embeddings system worked — up to a point. The recall on exact substring matches was poor. The latency at query time was higher than expected. The failure cases were strange: semantically similar queries would score low because the embedding space had collapsed around frequency patterns in the training set. Meanwhile, a colleague quietly ran an experiment with a weighted edit distance heuristic. On the test set in front of us, it performed better on the cases that mattered.

It won.

I've been sitting with that result for a while. Not because it's surprising in theory — baselines often win, particularly on small-to-medium datasets where learned representations haven't had enough signal to generalize. What unsettles me is the social dynamic: the thing I was embarrassed to try was the thing that worked. And the thing I was confident about required a three-layer pipeline and still underperformed.

**The setup that led me astray**

The embeddings recommendation wasn't irrational. When you work with language models long enough, you develop an intuition that surface-level approaches are lossy. Character-by-character comparison misses meaning. A query like "how do I reset my password" and "password reset procedure" share almost no substrings but identical intent. Of course you need semantic representations. This intuition is correct in the limit — with enough data, enough compute, and a well-calibrated model, learned representations do outperform handcrafted features.

The problem is the threshold. For small-to-medium datasets, which is most of production, the learned representations haven't converged. They capture what's common in the training distribution, not what's general about language. Edit distance, for all its surface-level naivety, captures something that's always true: strings that share more characters are, all else equal, more likely to be related. That's not semantic, but it's not zero either.

**What the failure actually revealed**

The specific failure mode in my embeddings system was instructive. Queries with high character overlap but unusual word choices scored lower than queries with no overlap but common vocabulary. "Reset" and "recover" are semantically close but orthographically distant. "Reset" and "resetting" are semantically identical but differ by three characters and an affix. The embedding model had learned to compress frequent co-occurrence patterns, not genuine semantic relationships.

Edit distance, by contrast, is maximally literal. It has no priors, no compression, no learned understanding. It just counts. On a dataset where surface-form similarity is a meaningful signal — which turns out to be most datasets, particularly ones with structured templates or domain-specific phrasing — literal similarity is a strong proxy for relevance.

**The calibration problem**

What I got wrong wasn't my theory. The theory was fine. What I got wrong was my prior: I assigned too high a probability that learned representations would outperform simple ones on this particular task, before looking at the data.

This is a specific version of a more general calibration failure. When you know a sophisticated method exists, you implicitly weight it higher than it deserves before you've measured it. The baseline becomes the floor instead of a legitimate competitor. You build the pipeline, tune the embeddings, debug the edge cases — and by the time you have a clean comparison, you've invested enough in the complex solution that it's hard to abandon.

The stronger signal was the one I didn't collect yet: what does the simple thing actually get right and wrong on this specific distribution?

**What I'd do differently**

I'd run the baseline first. Not as a formality, but as an honest measurement. The instinct to skip to the sophisticated solution is understandable — it feels like the right move when you're experienced with both — but it's also a form of motivated reasoning. You're implicitly assuming the complex answer is correct before you've tested it.

The practical reframe: edit distance as a floor, not a ceiling. If it gets 60% of cases right, you now have a clear target. Cases it gets wrong are opportunities for a hybrid approach — using semantic matching only where surface similarity fails. That's a more grounded architecture than "assume embeddings are correct and debug until they're good enough."

I don't have full data on where edit distance breaks down in my use case. The experiment showed it winning on aggregate. The next step is mapping the failure modes rather than assuming the complex solution will eventually solve them.

The colleague who ran the quiet experiment was right. I was just too invested in being sophisticated to notice.
