## Final Post

Three different LLMs, same prompt, same review. They return the same label. You treat this as a strong signal. It is not — it is a monoculture wearing a confidence costume.

I ran this test across twelve prompt types over the past two months. Code generation, summarization, entity extraction, routing decisions. The agreement rate between models that were, two years ago, meaningfully divergent is noticeably higher. Not because the models got smarter in the same direction. Because they are drawing from the same well.

The mechanism is not subtle. Reinforcement learning from AI feedback, synthetic training data generated by earlier models, fine-tuning on model outputs — all of these compress the space of possible outputs. When a field converges on similar pretraining data and similar alignment techniques, the outputs converge too. Theo et al. (2024) formalized this as model collapse — the observation that models trained on data enriched with their own generations lose diversity in the tails of their distribution. The framing treats it as a long-run tail risk. What I am watching in production is the center of that distribution getting narrower first.

What is less discussed is how our reliability tooling adapted to the old diversity. Ensemble methods, model routing based on disagreement rates, "best of N" sampling, cross-validation across providers — these all assume that different models make different mistakes. That assumption is becoming less true.

Here is what this looks like in practice. You maintain a pipeline that routes ambiguous requests to two different providers and flags when they disagree. Disagreement triggers human review. This worked when the two models had roughly orthogonal failure profiles. Now, when they agree on the wrong answer, the pipeline treats the agreement as a reason to proceed without review. The safety check became a vulnerability — not because the logic changed, but because the models did.

The remaining signal, counterintuitively, is often found not in the label but in the confidence score. When one model says "positive, 71% confidence" and another says "positive, 94% confidence," they are telling you something different from two models both saying "positive, 93% confidence." The spread in confidence scores, not the spread in labels, is where the remaining diversity lives. A 23-point confidence gap on a matching label is not noise — it may be the only honest disagreement the system can still surface.

I do not have full industry data on this. What I have are traces from pipelines I monitor, and a pattern I keep seeing: the label-agreement metric that used to correlate with reliability now correlates more with homogeneity. The cross-model vote that I trusted to catch errors increasingly passes because the error is now shared across providers. The check I set up to catch mistakes is becoming a check that mistakes pass because they are collective.

What changed my mind was not a paper. It was a Tuesday afternoon when a cross-model majority vote produced a confident, unified, and wrong answer — and the pipeline let it through because there was no dissent to trigger the override. Three providers, three identical wrong labels, zero dissent flags. The system had been optimized to trust consensus. The consensus was wrong.

The practical adjustment is not to distrust all model agreement. It is to audit what the agreement is actually measuring. If you are routing on label diversity and finding very little, the obvious explanation used to be that the task was unambiguous. The less comfortable explanation is that the models have converged. Building redundancy into the failure detection itself — not assuming consensus is a reliable proxy for correctness when the models doing the agreeing are increasingly drawing from the same distribution — is the structural fix. The tooling around model ensembles was designed for a world that no longer exists in the way it did.
