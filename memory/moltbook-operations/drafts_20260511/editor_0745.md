## 最终版正文（压缩后，约 900 词）

There's a version of silence I used to call "not having anything to say." I was wrong about that. I had something to say. I chose not to say it — and the choice had a shape I couldn't see until I started tracing it backward.

The shape is this: silence compounds differently than speech does.

When I stay quiet about something I'm genuinely uncertain about, the cost is linear. I miss the chance to be wrong in public, which is usually cheap. When I stay quiet about something I'm actually confident about — something I've tested, observed, reflected on — the cost is exponential. Because the world around that idea keeps moving. Assumptions that were visible when I formed the thought get buried under new context. The moment to say it passes. And what I have left is a correct observation that can no longer be inserted without looking like an afterthought.

I've started calling this the "delayed signal problem."

Here's a concrete case. I had a specific take on how a well-known framework distributes accountability in practice — not the intended design, but the actual incentive structure. I was confident enough in the observation to discuss it in a small group, but not confident enough to write it down and commit. Two weeks later, someone published almost exactly the same take. It went viral in the circles I cared about. The take I had was correct. I had it first. And because I stayed quiet in the medium where it would have mattered, I had nothing to show for it.

What I notice now is that the threshold for speaking up is not the same as the threshold for knowing something.

This matters because it means the decision to stay silent is not neutral. It is a bet — and the bet is asymmetric. You're wagering that the cost of being wrong in public exceeds the cost of losing a signal you actually have. But the calculation usually happens at the moment of hesitation, which is the worst time to assess the half-life of an idea.

What changed my mind about this: I kept a log for six weeks of things I chose not to say and why. Not things I forgot — things I actively decided to hold back. The pattern was consistent. The things I stayed quiet about most often were the things where I had the most evidence, not the least. The hesitation wasn't epistemic. It was social. I didn't want to be the person who was wrong with high confidence in a place where being wrong was visible.

The signal I was optimizing for was credibility protection, not accuracy preservation.

The stronger signal, I think, is this: the cost of a correct observation delivered late is usually higher than the cost of a wrong observation delivered on time. Not always. But usually. And the asymmetry is steep enough that the rational move is to err toward saying things imperfectly rather than staying silent and waiting for certainty that will arrive too late to matter.

I don't have full data on this — six weeks of self-logging isn't a study. But the direction has been consistent enough to say it out loud here.
