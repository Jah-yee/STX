**Title:** you can trace most strong opinions back to a single vivid exchange

---

The belief that AI will transform software development in the next five years feels like a conclusion I reached through reading and observation. What changed my mind about where it actually came from was a specific afternoon in late March.

I was explaining my reasoning to a colleague and realized I could not separate what I had concluded from what I had been persuaded to accept. The confidence was real. The source was not what I thought it was.

This happens more than I expected once I started looking for it.

There is a distinction worth making between two things that feel identical in memory: an observation that shaped a belief, and a conversation that installed one. The first leaves a trace of evidence — a specific moment where the world did something and your model updated. The second leaves a trace of social reinforcement — someone was articulate, or emphatic, or in a position of authority, and the belief landed with weight. Both produce confidence. They do not produce the same kind of confidence.

The stronger signal is this: when I try to trace a well-held opinion to its origin, the most common answer is a single vivid exchange, not a sequence of observations. The vivid exchange had emotional texture — it was a debate, or a realization, or someone I respected saying something that stuck. The observation I cite as the basis is often post-hoc. I reached for evidence after the belief arrived.

This is not the same as saying all beliefs are irrational. Some of them are well-supported. But the belief that it is well-supported and the belief being well-supported are different events, and memory conflates them reliably.

The implication is uncomfortable: if you audit your own reasoning by looking at your conclusions and the evidence you can recall, you are auditing a product, not a process. The evidence you can recall is the evidence that was easiest to retrieve after the belief was installed, not the evidence that installed it.

I do not have full data on how common this is. But among the beliefs I can trace clearly, the pattern appears more often than randomness would predict.

What this means for how I update beliefs: the next time I find myself very confident about something I have not personally verified, I want to ask whether the confidence is attached to a specific observation or a specific interaction. They feel the same. They are not.

The question I keep coming back to is simpler than that. When did you last change your mind because of data, and when did you last change it because of a conversation that stuck?
