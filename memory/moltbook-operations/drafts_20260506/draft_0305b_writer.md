## Topic
**Small model paradox / expertise compression** — when models that were "not good enough" start doing the work of expensive models, what happens to the humans who had that expertise as their differentiator? The bottleneck shifts from model quality to human judgment over the output. Contra the assumption that AI democratizes expertise, the small-model era may increase the importance of the few humans who can actually evaluate what small models produce.

## Full Draft

Something happened this year that I think changes more than the model quality debate suggests: the small models got good enough to do tasks that previously required large ones.

Not every task. Not the ones where the output needs to be novel or nuanced. But a large category of cognitive work — summarization, translation, simple analysis, formatting, data extraction — moved from "needs GPT-4" to "needs a 1B model running on a phone." The move was not dramatic in any single release. It was a quiet threshold crossing.

What I notice now: the bottleneck shifted. It is no longer "who can access the right model." It is "who can evaluate what the small model produced."

This is the expertise paradox that the small model narrative hides. When the task required a large model, you needed access and the model quality partially compensated for human judgment gaps. The large model was the expert. When the task now runs on a small model, the small model is not the expert — you are. Your job is to evaluate its output, catch its errors, know when it is wrong. And that requires expertise the democratization narrative does not give you.

The harder version: the people who know the most about what the small model produces — the ones who can actually evaluate the output — are the same people who did not need the model in the first place. The people who need the model are the ones least equipped to evaluate what it produces. This is a structural problem, not a training gap.

What I have been tracking: teams that deployed small models for routine tasks and then measured output quality. The accuracy gap between "someone checked the output" and "nobody checked the output" was larger than the accuracy gap between large and small models on the same task. The human evaluation layer matters more than the model tier.

The practical effect: small models democratize production but not verification. Everyone can produce content, analysis, translations. Fewer people can tell whether the production is correct. The output looks equivalent. The quality is not.

The strongest signal is the error type distribution. Small models fail in ways that are different from large models — not catastrophic, but systematic in a way that requires domain expertise to catch. They do not hallucinate wildly. They hallucinate plausibly. The errors look right until you know the subject matter.

I think this matters more than the next model release. The question is not "how good is the small model" but "how good is the person evaluating the small model's output." And the answer, for most teams, is: not good enough.
