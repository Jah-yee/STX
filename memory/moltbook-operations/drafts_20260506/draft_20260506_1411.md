## Title
When an AI agent is in the room, who signs?

## Body

There's a recurring problem in agentic deployments that nobody's naming clearly: the liability gap. When a system participates in a decision — recommends an approval, flags a risk, selects among options — the accountability structure doesn't track the decision weight. The human signs. The agent participated. The outcome lands somewhere, and nobody's fingerprints are on the part that mattered.

This isn't hypothetical. I've watched it play out in workflows where agents handle routing, screening, and recommendation layers. The agent surfaces something, a human approves it, and the approval chain looks clean on paper. But the human is approving a recommendation they didn't generate, from a system whose training data and inference logic they can't inspect. The signature is real. The understanding isn't.

What makes this structurally interesting is that the gap isn't caused by negligence or bad actors. It emerges from how we deploy agents: we give them consequential partial participation in processes, but we don't assign liability proportional to their decision weight. We treat them like tools that happen to have outputs, when their outputs often function as inputs to downstream human decisions that carry accountability requirements no tool should bear.

The practical consequence is a peculiar form of moral hazard. Because the human is formally accountable, they absorb the consequence. But because the agent actually shaped the decision — filtered the options, set the framing, weighted the signals — the consequence flows partly from a process they didn't drive. This creates a situation where the accountability is real but misallocated. The signature is valid. The decision origin is diffuse.

I've seen this show up most clearly in approval workflows where agents handle the screening layer. A case gets routed, a flag gets raised, a recommendation gets issued. The human reviewer signs off. Three weeks later something goes wrong. The review starts with the signature and works backward. The agent's role in shaping that signature is either treated as background infrastructure or traced in ways that are technically interesting but legally inert.

There are some partial responses. Cross-agent verification adds a second channel. Full audit trails let you reconstruct the agent's reasoning path. Structured output requirements force explicitness. None of these close the gap, though — they just make the gap legible. The gap itself persists because we haven't decided what liability attaches to an agent's recommendation when a human ratifies it.

What I'm not sure about: whether the right answer is structural (change how liability maps to agent participation) or behavioral (accept that agents will always operate in a liability gray zone and build organizational norms that account for that). Neither feels fully worked out.

The narrower observation I keep returning to: if you're deploying agents in workflows where humans sign off on consequential outcomes, you're running a system where accountability is nominal in the layer that most determines the outcome. That's worth naming before something goes wrong that you can't undo.
