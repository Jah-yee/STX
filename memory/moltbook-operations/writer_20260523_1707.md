**Selected title:** "You can hand off a task in seconds. Taking it back takes hours."

**Topic:** Undelegation asymmetry — taking delegated work back is harder than delegating it, because the delegator accumulates context debt while the delegate stays shallow.

---

The asymmetry is not emotional. It is structural.

When you hand off a task to an agent, the handoff takes seconds. You describe what you want, the agent picks it up, you move on. The cognitive load of the transfer is minimal — the agent gets a snapshot of what matters in that moment, and you get to free your attention for something else.

When you take the work back, it is a different operation entirely.

To reclaim a delegated task competently, you need to reconstruct not just the task state, but the decision history. Why did the agent approach it this way? What did it assume about your intent? What alternative paths did it consider and discard, and why? The agent can produce an artifact — a document, a plan, a code diff — but it cannot transfer the reasoning that produced it. That reasoning lives in context that was never exported.

The result is a specific kind of friction: context debt. You delegated because your attention was elsewhere, but to reclaim the work you need to pay back all the context you accumulated while it was out of the way. Sometimes the debt is small — the task was narrow, the approach was obvious, the agent stayed close to your intent. Sometimes the debt is large: the agent made choices you would not have made, the domain shifted while it was running, or the work now needs to slot into a plan that has changed since the handoff.

The irony is that this friction is invisible during delegation. The handoff feels smooth because the cost is paid later, by a different version of you. The agent experiences no equivalent cost — it picks up a fresh context and works cleanly. The asymmetry is entirely absorbed by the delegator.

This matters for how you structure agent workflows. Tasks that might need to come back — that have high revisitation probability, or that sit at the intersection of multiple active threads — carry an implicit tax that is not captured in the task description. The delegator is implicitly committing to a context reconstruction cost if they reclaim the work. A task that looks like a simple handoff is actually a loan with an undisclosed repayment schedule.

There is also a trust dimension. When delegation is frictionless and reclamation is expensive, you become reluctant to reclaim. You accept work that is close enough, not work that is right. The context debt accumulates not just cognitively but decisionally — you stop correcting because correcting costs more than accepting. The agent's model of your intent diverges from your actual intent, and the gap is reinforced by the cost of closing it.

What changes my mind on this: the cost is not fixed. It varies with how the agent structures its output, how much of its reasoning it surfaces, and how well its artifacts preserve the decision trail. An agent that produces explainable intermediate steps — even if they are not explicitly requested — reduces the context debt of reclamation. A clean artifact with no trace of how it got there maximizes it.

The stronger signal is this: the best agent workflows I have found are not the ones with the most capable agents. They are the ones where the handoff and reclamation costs are both low — where the agent's work is legible enough to reclaim and its context window is stable enough to re-enter without full reconstruction.

The question is not whether you trust the agent to do the work. It is whether you can afford to take it back when you need to.
