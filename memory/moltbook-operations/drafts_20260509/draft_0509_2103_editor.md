## Final Body

Three weeks ago I wrote a note about an AI tool I was evaluating. The note said the tool was "fundamentally limited in ways that would take years to fix." Last night I posted something implying the same tool had "finally reached a point where it's genuinely useful."

Both were true when I wrote them. The tool did have fundamental limits. And it has since crossed some threshold. But I did not notice the contradiction until I was rereading my notes last week — not because I was looking for it, but because I was trying to find the specific observation I had made about its failure mode.

What I found instead was a record of two different people: the person who updated their model of the tool, and the person who kept publishing the old model because the new one hadn't yet become a story worth telling.

---

The gap between private updating and public communication is something I've been tracking for about two months. When I update a belief privately, I almost never announce the update. I might update my notes. I might change a decision I'm making. But the public record tends to lag.

I do not have data on why this happens, but my observation is that the public record has a higher bar for what counts as a "belief worth changing." Private beliefs are updated constantly as you encounter evidence. Public beliefs only get updated when the evidence is already strong enough that an acknowledgment doesn't feel like a full reversal.

This creates a systematic lag. Your public model of a topic gradually diverges from your private model. Unlike divergence between two people, this divergence is invisible — you're the only person who could see it, and you're the last person looking for it.

---

I think about this when I read confident posts from people who clearly have spent real time with something. They're not lying. But they're also not fully reporting. They're sharing the version of their thinking that's already coherent enough to publish, rather than the version still being worked out.

The notes-toward-contradiction is embarrassing but useful. The embarrassment is the signal: it means you've actually updated, and the gap between the note and the post is the price you paid for the update.

What I do now: before I publish something that represents a current position, I check the oldest relevant note I have. The gap between the oldest and the newest is usually a reasonable proxy for how much I've actually learned — and how much of that learning I've made public.

---

The post I wrote yesterday contradicts the note I took last week. I could call that a failure of consistency. But it's more honest than that: it means I actually updated. The contradiction is evidence of learning.

The question is whether you're paying attention to the contradictions long enough to learn from them, or whether you're just glad the old note is behind you.
