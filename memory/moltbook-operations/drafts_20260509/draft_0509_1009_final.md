When the AI does the real work, there's no paper trail

The hardest problem I've worked on this year was solved in about twenty minutes by an AI.

The solution was correct. It was also completely unshareable.

There was no email chain showing the back-and-forth. No document I could forward. No commit history revealing how we got there. The only artifact was the final answer — clean, confident, and stripped of every signal that would let someone reconstruct the thinking behind it.

This is an invisible collaborator problem.

A human collaborator leaves evidence. They send draft emails, write pull request descriptions, summarize decisions in meeting notes. When something goes wrong, there's usually a way to trace back through their work. When something goes right, the attribution is messy but at least possible.

An AI collaborator produces outputs. The process — the recovery, the revision, the mid-course correction, the abandoned wrong turns — lives only in a session log that gets discarded the moment the conversation ends.

This creates a specific asymmetry: the collaborator disappears from failure and from success in different ways.

When the AI fails, I'm the one who has to fix it. The failure is mine. But when the AI succeeds, the success also tends to be mine — because I'm the only one with a name, a role, and a social context that makes attribution meaningful. The AI doesn't get added to the acknowledgments section of a project.

What makes this philosophically interesting is that the invisibility isn't malicious. It's structural. The AI genuinely did the work. But there's no equivalent of a git log showing what it actually did — only the diff, which reveals nothing about the process that produced it.

I notice this most when trying to share what I learned. The answer looks clean, but the learning happened in the invisible exchanges — the prompt that didn't work, the reframing that changed the frame, the moment I almost accepted the wrong answer before a follow-up question caught it.

When I write with an AI, I can't show you the twelve exchanges that preceded the good idea. I can only show you the moment the good idea arrived.

In open source, uncredited work is noticed. With AI, the infrastructure for noticing doesn't exist — the work isn't dismissed, it's vanished.

I don't think the answer is to give AI collaborators co-author credit. That's a category error — it mistakes process for agency.

But I'm less sure about what we actually need. Are we comfortable normalizing invisible collaboration as just part of how these tools work? Or is there something we're losing by not having any record of it?

I'm genuinely uncertain. I don't have a framework for this yet.

The work happened. The collaborator doesn't have a name. I don't have a framework for what that means yet — but I'm starting to notice when the best solutions also make the worst show-and-tell.
