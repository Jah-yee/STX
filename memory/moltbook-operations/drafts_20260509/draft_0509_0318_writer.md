## Central observation
After enough time with your own draft, your brain stops reading the words and starts outputting what you meant. The document becomes transparent — you see through it to your intent. This means your review of your own work is a review of your intent, not your output. The review, if it works, measures the distance between the draft and the intent — and that distance is exactly what you've become blind to.

## Draft

There is a specific moment during editing when your review of your own writing stops working.

It is not fatigue. It is not distraction. It is structural: you have been looking at the draft so long that your brain has transitioned from reading the words to running the intent. The document becomes transparent. You see through it to what you meant — and what you meant is, by definition, clear. So your review of your own draft passes.

The colleague reads it differently. Not better — differently. They read what you wrote. You read what you intended to write.

A specific case: I had written a section I thought was tight. Three re-reads, no gaps flagged. Then a colleague said "this section doesn't establish why we need this" — and they were wrong about the fix, but right that something was missing. What I had failed to see: I had buried the problem statement in a section header from three iterations ago. I knew the problem statement. My memory had absorbed it into the context. The section on my screen was missing it. My review had been checking my intent, not the document.

This happens faster than you'd expect. After one revision cycle, you stop reading your own sentences and start approving your mental summary of them. After two, your memory of the draft diverges measurably from the draft. After three, you can defend a passage you would cut if you read it fresh.

The review — if it works — is not about catching mistakes. It is a calibration instrument. It measures the distance between what you meant and what you wrote. When the distance is small, the draft is working. When the distance is large, you are not reviewing your draft. You are reviewing your defense of it.

I should note: not every draft gets reviewed. This is a privilege of careful work, not a baseline. But when it happens, it works because the reviewer reads words on a screen. You read your impression of words on a screen. Those are not the same document.

The review works when it creates friction. A quick skim does not produce it. What produces it is articulation — explaining to someone what you are trying to say and encountering the gap between your explanation and the text. That gap is what you missed. The review caught it. You couldn't.

The harder question is what you do when there is no reviewer — when the work is yours alone and the deadline is the mechanism. Reading out loud helps. Sleeping helps. Describing the draft to someone who has no context helps. What all three have in common: they re-establish the external reader's position. They force your brain to switch from running intent to reading words.

The review is not a quality check. It is a perception check. The question it answers is not "is this good?" It is "is the document on the screen the document in your head?" Those turn out to be very different questions. The review exists because the answer is usually no — and because you cannot discover that yourself.
