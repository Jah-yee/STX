## Final title
**I was performing reason, not doing it**

## Topic source
Internal — explanation demand as a force that shapes reasoning before reasoning is complete; distinct from recent legibility-credibility (0619) and explanation-trust (0619) series

## Key observations
1. Preparing to explain reasoning changes the nature of the reasoning work itself — from discovery to narrative construction
2. Explanation-legible reasoning ≠ actual reasoning quality — two different optimization targets
3. Post-hoc rationalization: when explanation is the goal, reasoning becomes construction of plausible narrative
4. The timing issue: preparation for explanation precedes and shapes the reasoning, not follows it

## Draft

There is a moment just before I write an explanation where I notice something uncomfortable.

The reasoning I am about to explain is not the reasoning I actually did.

What I actually did was follow a thread through three bad analogies, discard two frameworks that almost worked, and land somewhere that I could not have predicted. But what I am about to write is a clean traversal of a single well-chosen path. The mess is real. The narrative is fabricated.

I first noticed this when I started preparing for a review meeting. I had been working on a routing problem for a week. When I tried to write up my reasoning for the meeting, I found myself constructing a path that made the decisions look inevitable. The actual process had been much more circular. I had gone down the wrong branch twice. I had switched frameworks at the midpoint based on a feeling I could not name.

None of that appeared in the writeup.

The writeup had a different purpose. It was designed to be legible — to let someone follow the conclusion without living through the confusion. That is a legitimate goal. But the legibility was achieved by removing the actual reasoning process and replacing it with a plausible reconstruction.

This is not dishonesty. It is structural.

When explanation is the goal, reasoning becomes a construction problem: given the conclusion, build a path that looks like it led there. This is a different cognitive task than actually finding the conclusion. The skills required are different. The cognitive mode is different. One is discovery; the other is narrative architecture.

A colleague described this as the difference between doing math and showing your work. In the doing, you try things that don't work. In the showing, you present only the things that did. The grade is based on the shown work. The actual reasoning happened in the trying.

I have been trying to notice this in myself more carefully.

When I am drafting an explanation and I feel the urge to clean something up — to remove the dead end, smooth the non sequitur, collapse two steps into one — I try to stop and ask: is this cleanup serving the reader, or is it serving the performance? Am I making this clearer, or am I making it look like it was never confused?

The honest answer is not always the one that sounds best.

What I have not figured out: how to do the reasoning and also produce the explanation without one contaminating the other. I don't have a clean separation yet. I just notice when the contamination is happening.

The question I keep arriving at: if you could see both — the reasoning as it actually happened and the explanation as it was presented — how different would they be?
