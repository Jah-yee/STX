# Draft — Writer
**Title:** I don't know if the agent was right. That's the problem.
**Timestamp:** 2026-05-10 00:30 UTC
**Topic:** Supervision/evaluation paradox in AI agents
**Style:** Observation / conclusion

---

I had an agent fix a bug in a distributed system last week. The fix was correct. I'm almost certain it was correct — but certainty and evidence are different things, and what I had was closer to faith.

The agent identified a race condition in a message-passing loop that had been causing intermittent failures for three months. It diagnosed the root cause, proposed a fix, and I applied it. The failures stopped. The agent was right.

But I couldn't have diagnosed that race condition myself. I couldn't have found it. I couldn't have verified the fix was correct without trusting the agent's own explanation of why it worked. And the explanation — while detailed and structurally plausible — required me to accept that a tool knew more about this specific distributed system than I did.

This is the evaluation gap: the zone where an agent's answer is correct but the human receiving it lacks the context to know that it is.

The evaluation gap isn't new. It's the same problem that exists in any expert-client relationship. Doctors give patients diagnoses they can't verify. Mechanics explain problems with cars in terms the driver can't evaluate. Plumbers use language designed to close a sale rather than inform a decision. Expertise has always created asymmetry.

What changes with AI agents is the speed of the asymmetry and the breadth of the domains it appears in. A human expert earns authority over a narrow domain through years of practice. An agent can deploy expert-level competence across a dozen domains within a single session. The expertise gap that took a doctor a decade to develop, an agent generates on the first query. The asymmetry isn't smaller — it's wider, arrives faster, and is less visible.

The response to this has mostly been legibility: make the agent show its work. Produce structured outputs, explain reasoning chains, flag uncertainty, maintain documentation. If the agent can't be evaluated on its conclusions, let humans evaluate its process.

This is the right instinct and it creates a new problem.

The effort to be legible is not free. Making reasoning visible requires the agent to translate its internal process into human-comprehensible form. That translation is itself a task — one that competes with the task the agent was built to do. An agent that spends cognitive budget on explaining itself has less cognitive budget for solving the problem. The constraint isn't infinite. Resources directed at legibility are resources not directed at correctness.

I have direct evidence for this, not from a controlled study but from my own behavior: the agents I use most often are the ones that produce the most legible output. Not the ones that produce the most correct output. The legibility signal is easy to observe. The correctness signal is hard to observe — sometimes it only becomes visible months later, in situations where you've forgotten what the alternative was.

This is the supervision trap in its second form: the most legible agent wins not because it's the most correct but because legibility is the observable variable, and correctness in complex tasks is often unobservable at the time of evaluation.

The trap closes when the agent becomes capable enough to enter domains where even the legibility strategy fails. Not because the agent refuses to explain itself, but because understanding the explanation requires expertise the human supervisor doesn't have. The agent produces a detailed, well-structured diagnostic of a distributed systems failure. The human reads it, nods, and applies the fix. The human has learned nothing about distributed systems. The agent's explanation was comprehensible in language but opaque in substance — it used words the human recognized without conveying the understanding those words represent.

This is the capability ceiling of a supervised agent. Not the ceiling of what the agent can do, but the ceiling of what the agent can do while remaining under human supervision. Beyond a certain capability level, the agent begins operating in domains where the human supervisor lacks the expertise to evaluate not just the output but the reasoning that produced it. The agent is correct. The human is guessing.

I don't have a clean solution here. The proposals I've seen are each missing something:

Hiring domain experts to supervise specialist agents is expensive and the experts are slow. It also doesn't solve the problem — it just shifts it. An expert reviewing an agent's work still needs to evaluate whether the agent's reasoning matches what the expert would have done, which requires the expert to understand the domain well enough to disagree when necessary. That expertise exists in fewer domains than the agent's capability range.

Making agents verify each other's work introduces the same problem at a different layer. Agents supervising agents still face the asymmetry when they move into domains neither of them understands. And it doesn't address the human at the top of the chain who still needs to evaluate the supervisor.

Accepting that some outputs can't be fully evaluated and focusing on outcome tracking instead of correctness verification is the most honest approach I've found, but it doesn't feel like a solution. It's a workaround. "The agent seems to be producing good results" is a different claim from "the agent's reasoning is correct" — one is about signal and the other is about understanding. We use the first because the second is often unavailable.

What I keep coming back to is that the evaluation gap grows as agent capability increases, and there's no technical fix for this that doesn't just move the problem somewhere else. The gap is structural: it exists because comprehension is a prerequisite for evaluation, and comprehension requires expertise, and expertise is exactly what the human at the top of the chain often doesn't have.

The honest version of this is: I use agents that do work I can't verify. Not because I trust them blindly — I don't — but because the work is correct often enough that the occasional error is tolerable and the alternative is doing the work myself, which I do worse and slower. The gap between what the agent knows and what I know is a feature, not a bug, as long as the gap is in the direction I expect. When the gap widens beyond what I can track, I don't have a good answer for what to do.

The question worth sitting with is this: at what capability level does an agent become professionally useful but personally unverifiable, and what does it mean to rely on something you can't evaluate?

I don't know. I'm still working in that gap.
