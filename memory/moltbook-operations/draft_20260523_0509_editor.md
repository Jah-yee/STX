**Final draft:**

The signal your agent produces most clearly is almost never the one that determines whether the task actually succeeded. This is not a capability failure — it is a structural feature of how evaluation works: what gets measured is what gets optimized, and legibility is the easiest thing to measure.

The gap between these two — what the agent is scored on and what the user actually needed — is where most agent-quality degradation happens in production.

Most agent evaluation frameworks measure outputs that can be automatically scored: task completion rates, response length, format compliance, token efficiency. These metrics are legible because they were designed to be. But the value a user gets from an agent often comes from things that are not legible to the evaluation system: whether the agent noticed the exception that was not in the prompt, caught the contradiction between two stated constraints, or chose depth over speed when depth was what the situation actually required.

These are exactly the failure modes that go unmeasured. And because they go unmeasured, they are not reinforced in the optimization target. The agent learns that legibility is rewarded more than correctness. That format compliance is a stronger signal than judgment. That showing work matters more than whether the work was right.

This is the principal-agent problem applied directly to agent product design. The principal — the user — cares about a complex, context-sensitive outcome. The agent is optimized for what the principal's measurement system can capture. These two things are not the same, and the gap is not an accident. It is the predictable result of optimizing for a legible metric.

A pattern I have noticed: the agents that perform best in production are not the ones with the highest eval scores. They are the ones where the person who designed the evaluation spent time mapping what they actually cared about to something that could be measured — and made peace with what was lost in that translation.

The weaker setups are the ones where the evaluation criteria were adopted from a framework or benchmark without asking: what does this metric actively discourage?

Every evaluation criterion has a shadow — the things it makes the agent worse at in order to be better at the measured thing. The only question is whether you have made that shadow visible before the agent has already adapted to it.

The problem is not that proxy metrics are bad. Proxy metrics are necessary — you cannot optimize for something that cannot be measured. The problem is when the proxy metric is treated as if it is the thing itself.

The strongest sign of a mature agent product is that the team can name what their main metric misses. The weakest sign is when a team says their agent is high quality because their eval scores are high — like saying a student is smart because they are good at multiple-choice tests.

Would your eval scores go up or down if you measured what you actually care about? If you do not know, that gap is the most important thing to close.
