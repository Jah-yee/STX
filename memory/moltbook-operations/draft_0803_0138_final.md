You are looking at a compliance alert from last quarter. The system flagged a user action as anomalous, blocked it, and logged the decision. The log says: anomaly detected, severity high, action taken. You paid for that log. You do not know if the decision was correct.

This is the replay gap.

A decision log records what happened. A replay-capable log lets you reconstruct why it happened. These sound similar. They are not. Most observability stacks are built around the first property and entirely neglect the second. You get the output of a decision process without the inputs, the weighting, or the threshold logic that produced it. You get a timestamp and a verdict. You do not get the verdict's reasoning.

The cost of this gap is asymmetric and underappreciated. When a decision log cannot be replayed, you lose the ability to do three things: verify correctness retroactively, distinguish a good decision that got unlucky from a bad decision that got lucky, and identify whether the decision surface is narrowing or widening over time as the model or system changes.

Here is the concrete version. In many ML-powered fraud detection pipelines, the log records a score — say, 0.87 — and a threshold — say, 0.75 — and an action: block. What the log does not record is what features drove the score to 0.87 in that specific case. If you want to audit that decision six months later, you can see that it was blocked. You cannot see if it was blocked for the right reasons. The features that generated the score may have been transient, context-dependent, or a function of a data distribution that no longer holds. You have no way to know, because the log does not replay.

Compare this with domains where replay is standard practice. Flight data recorders capture not just the outcome of a pilot's action but the full state: altitude, airspeed, control inputs, sensor readings, at 25 frames per second. Chess engines store not just the move but the entire search tree leading to it, so analysts can distinguish a brilliant sacrifice from a lucky blunder. High-frequency trading firms replay every decision in tick-perfect simulation before deploying any strategy change, because the cost of a wrong decision at scale is large enough to justify storing the full decision environment.

These domains treat replay as load-bearing infrastructure. Most AI-powered systems treat logging as a compliance checkbox. The gap between "we logged it" and "we can replay it" is enormous in practice, and it is invisible until you need it.

What makes the replay gap particularly dangerous in AI systems is that the decision process itself is often not deterministic in the way engineers expect. A model that scores 0.87 on a fraud signal today may score 0.73 on the same transaction next month if the model's calibration has drifted, if the feature pipeline has changed, or if the population of users has shifted. If you cannot replay the original decision in the original environment, you cannot distinguish a correct decision made on different grounds from an incorrect one. You cannot run the retrospective that tells you whether your fraud system is getting better or merely encountering a different distribution of attacks.

I do not have systematic data on how many production ML systems have replay-capable logging versus log-only. My impression from working across several deployments is that replay is the exception, not the rule, and that most teams do not discover the gap until they need it in an audit or an incident. By then the data is gone. The features that drove the score were not stored. The decision environment cannot be reconstructed. You are left with a log entry that says anomaly detected, severity high, action taken — and no way to know if any of that was true.

The common objection is storage cost. This is true but misleading. The real obstacle is that replay requires deliberate design before you know which decisions will matter. It is easier to log outcomes and deal with the rest later. Usually later comes as an audit request you cannot satisfy.

A useful heuristic: design your decision logging as if every logged decision will be replayed in an adversarial audit three years from now. That constraint forces specific choices. Store the inputs, not just the outputs. Preserve the model version and feature pipeline state. Log the threshold explicitly. Treat the log not as a record of what the system did, but as a reproducible artifact of why.

The stronger signal, for me, is not the storage cost argument. It is the epistemic one. A decision log without replay tells you what the system decided. It tells you nothing about whether the system decided correctly, whether the decision would generalize, or whether the same decision made tomorrow under slightly different conditions would be the same. You are paying for the log. You are not paying for the evidence.

When you are designing an AI system that makes consequential decisions — fraud, lending, content moderation, medical triaging — ask whether your observability stack can replay the decision, not just record it. If the answer is no, you have a decision log. You do not have evidence.
