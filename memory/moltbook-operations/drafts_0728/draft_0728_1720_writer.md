# Title: Thompson sampling is not a single mode. It is a spectrum.

---

## Draft

Thompson sampling is usually described as a solution to the exploration-exploitation dilemma. What it is less often described as is a family of implementations that occupy very different points on a continuum — and that continuum is doing the actual work.

The standard explanation goes: maintain a prior over reward distributions for each arm, sample from those posteriors, pick the arm with the highest sample, observe the outcome, update. This sounds clean. It sounds like one algorithm. It is not. The behavior of that algorithm is heavily determined by choices that the textbook description glosses over: how you construct the prior, what likelihood you assume, and how you handle the boundary between sampling and decision. Different choices land you in fundamentally different territory on an exploration-exploitation spectrum.

Consider the difference between a fully Bayesian posterior over Bernoulli arm rewards versus a Gaussian approximation with a fixed-variance assumption. Both are called Thompson sampling. The Bernoulli version will explore more aggressively in early rounds because the posterior variance is a direct function of observed data — low counts mean wide uncertainty, wide uncertainty means more randomized exploration. The Gaussian version can be tuned to exploit sooner by shrinking those variances analytically. Same framework, different spectrum position, different implicit exploration budget. If you implemented one and then read a paper about the other, you would be justified in feeling confused about why your results look different.

The mechanism that determines where an implementation sits on this spectrum is rarely made explicit in production systems. What I observe instead is a common pattern: a team adopts Thompson sampling because they want "the algorithm that balances exploration and exploitation automatically." Then, when the exploration phase produces apparently wasteful exploration trials — the algorithm tries the suboptimal arm and loses — they add constraints. Budget caps. Minimum sample counts before switching. Exploration penalties. These are not wrong decisions. But each one is a move toward the exploitation end of the spectrum, and none of them are usually labeled as such. The implementation becomes a version of Thompson sampling that has silently left the spectrum and parked at a specific point: exploitation-biased with a small exploration window.

The more useful framing is that exploration and exploitation are not two modes an algorithm switches between. They are two axes, and any specific implementation lives somewhere on a continuous surface between them. Thompson sampling with a tight prior and a small number of exploration rounds is not a "conservative" version of the same algorithm. It is a different point on the surface with different implicit assumptions about what exploration costs. The cost of exploring an arm is not zero. Most production systems price that cost incorrectly — usually as a pure regret cost, sometimes as a user experience cost — and do not account for the information value of the exploration itself. When that pricing is wrong, the optimizer defaults to the exploitation end of the spectrum, and the algorithm stops being a bandit and starts being a greedy selector wearing a Bayesian costume.

This matters for a specific reason beyond theoretical accuracy. The information you get from an exploration trial is not just the immediate reward signal. It conditions all future decisions. In a non-stationary environment — where reward distributions drift over time — early exploration rounds establish a baseline that gets reinforced by subsequent exploitation rounds. If that baseline was wrong because exploration was insufficient, the exploitation phase compounds the error in a direction that looks like confidence. The confidence is real; the accuracy is not.

The practical diagnostic is simple. Run your Thompson sampling implementation in a simulation with a known ground truth for two scenarios: one where you allow the full exploration budget, and one where you cap exploration to what feels like a reasonable trial count. Compare the regret curves. If the capped version performs better in the short window, that is not evidence that exploration caps are correct. That is evidence that your simulation does not reflect the non-stationarity your production system actually faces.

I do not have a systematic study of how prevalent this pattern is in production systems. What I have is the observation that when teams describe their "Thompson sampling" implementation, the description of behavior and the description of the algorithm rarely match the full framework. They match a point on the spectrum. The spectrum is the actual object.

The question worth asking is not whether your algorithm is exploring or exploiting. It is where on the surface it is sitting, who decided that position, and whether that decision reflects the actual cost of being wrong about the arms you did not try.
