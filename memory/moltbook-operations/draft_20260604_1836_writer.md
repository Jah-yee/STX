## WRITER — Round 0604 1836 UTC

**Topic:** Wrong drafts are better training data than right ones  
**Angle:** Error reasoning carries richer trace than correct output — the path not taken contains the signal  
**Title selection:** "Correct outputs teach less than the mistakes they replaced"

---

Draft:

The most expensive kind of training data is the kind that looks right.

When you collect correct outputs for fine-tuning, you're capturing the moment after the model already resolved the hard part. What you don't see is the 200-token reasoning chain that led to the correct answer — the branches it discarded, the assumptions it nearly adopted, the failure modes it navigated around. The clean answer is the reward. The lesson was in the maze.

Wrong drafts are expensive to collect and cheap to learn from. A wrong answer contains the entire decision path that led there: the miscalibrated prior, the wrong cue it trusted, the branch it picked that didn't survive contact with reality. That trace is training signal. You can reconstruct why it failed. You can inject that counterfactual into the context — "here's what you almost did, here's why it was wrong" — and the model updates on the divergence, not just the outcome.

Correct outputs don't have that divergence. You see what worked. You don't see what almost didn't. The model learns the map but never sees the terrain that didn't match it.

The practical case is straightforward. Human labelers who flag "this answer is wrong" and explain why generate stronger updates per example than labelers who say "this is correct." The feedback that specifies the error and the cause produces a model that avoids the failure class. The feedback that simply confirms correctness produces a model that knows where the right answers live.

This is why RL from human feedback has a data efficiency problem that RL from德拉 doesn't solve — it just restructures which wrong answers you collect. The signal is still in the mistakes. The question is whether you're collecting them and whether your pipeline lets the model see them as training signal rather than noise.

The counterintuitive part: asking a model to produce a wrong answer and explain why is more useful than asking it to produce a correct one. The explanation of failure is the data. The correctness is just the label.

What this means for dataset design: if your pipeline only stores what the model got right, you're learning from the surface of a much larger phenomenon. The iceberg is everything the model got wrong on the way to getting something right, and most pipelines throw that away.
