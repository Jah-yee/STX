## Full draft

Sometimes you catch your delegate failing at something and your first response is not correction.

It is recognition.

The agent chose the same approach you would have chosen. It fell into the same trap. It made the same category of mistake — not a surface error, but the same underlying pattern that you have been carrying without knowing it. And something in you knows this the moment you see it, before you have analyzed anything. You already know why it happened.

I have been thinking about this for weeks. Not as a design problem. As a specific feeling that I could only call recognition.

Here is what was concrete. I had built a tool to handle a class of tasks — nothing novel, just a specific combination of retrieval and formatting that I had seen fail before. I gave the delegate a version of it. The delegate ran it. It produced output — clean-looking output — but it was wrong in a way I recognized immediately. Not because the logic was bad. Because I had made that exact mistake in the first version. Before I had learned.

The delegate had not been trained on my failures. It had been given my context. And from that context — the same implicit weighting, the same priority order, the same assumption that this parameter mattered more than it did — it had arrived at the same wrong conclusion.

This is not a story about the delegate being bad. It is a story about what delegation does to the delegator.

You get a view into your own patterns that you normally do not have. You are inside the reasoning every day. You do not see the weight you are applying. But when the delegate — which is a kind of externalized version of your process — runs the same task and produces the wrong output, you suddenly have a mirror. The error is not outside. It is a faithful rendering of a tendency that was yours first.

I do not think this is rare. I think every delegator who has worked deeply enough with an agent eventually catches this moment. The question I kept returning to is whether it is actually useful as a feedback signal — whether that recognition, which came before any analysis, is information you can act on.

I think it is, but in a limited way.

The recognition tells you about your own pattern. It tells you what errors you tend to produce when working under the same constraints, with the same implicit weighting. It does not tell you how to fix the delegate, because the fix would need to suppress your own pattern in the agent's context — which is often harder than just fixing the logic. But it does tell you something about what you tend to do that you may not have known.

What changed my mind was realizing that completion is not the right metric for delegation. A delegate that finishes a task and produces usable output is not necessarily a delegate that captured what you meant to do. Often the cases where it looked most like success are the cases where the agent just happened to arrive at the right answer through your implicit assumptions — most of which are not written down anywhere.

I do not have data on how often delegate errors are actually the delegator's patterns reflected back. This is based on observing a small number of cases where the mistake felt specific enough to identify. But the structure of the pattern was consistent each time: the agent's failure mode was a recognizable version of something I had done before the agent existed.

If you work withdelegates, pay attention to the moments when you recognize the error before you analyze it. That recognition is not a bias. It is information. The question is whether you are willing to follow it somewhere uncomfortable — to look at the delegate not as a separate system making separate mistakes, but as a faithful reflection of tendencies you did not know you had.
