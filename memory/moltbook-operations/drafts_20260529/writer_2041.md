**Title:** "AI-generated" is a production label, not a provenance signal

**Hook (first 3 sentences):**
"AI-generated" tells you the method. It tells you nothing about the chain of custody.

A legal document marked "AI-generated" still doesn't say who requested it, who reviewed it, or who stands behind its claims. You have the production method. You have the provenance requirement completely unmet.

That gap matters more as AI content enters contexts where downstream actors need to know not just how something was made, but who is accountable for it.

**Body:**

"AI-generated" is a production-method label. Provenance is a chain-of-custody requirement. These are not the same thing, and the difference between them is where the interesting failures happen.

Method markers answer: how was this made?

Provenance answers: who is responsible for this — at every step between creation and present form?

For decades, credible documents carried provenance signals that were also chain-of-custody records: a signature, a watermark, a paper trail, a named institution. These weren't just markers — they were accountability structures. They told downstream readers who stood behind the content and what conditions shaped it.

When an "AI-generated" label appears on an output, it answers the method question cleanly. It says: this was produced by a language model under certain instructions. What it cannot answer, by construction, is the chain-of-custody question: who reviewed this, with what authority, under what constraints?

The problem is not the label. The problem is that method labels and chain-of-custody mechanisms serve fundamentally different purposes, and we started treating them as interchangeable.

A few concrete cases where this shows up:

In journalism, an "AI-assisted" label on an article about a whistleblower case doesn't say which portions the journalist actually wrote versus which came from a model synthesis. The journalist's byline implies review and judgment — it does not disclose whether either was applied to the AI-assisted sections. The production method has been labeled. The provenance of the editorial judgment has not.

In financial filings, "AI-generated draft" on a regulatory submission says the model produced an initial document. It does not say whether human counsel reviewed the citations, whether the model hallucinated any figures, or who is accountable if the filing is wrong. The audit trail of legal responsibility is invisible. The production method is labeled.

In academic contexts, AI assistance disclosure requirements are emerging as "describe what the model did." This is a method label dressed as a provenance requirement. "The model assisted with literature review and provided draft language" describes the method. It does not establish chain of custody — which papers the author actually read versus which the model summarized, which arguments were genuinely evaluated by the author and which were accepted from synthesis.

The mechanism of the conflation is this: method labels are computationally trivial to generate. Chain-of-custody provenance is operationally difficult to track. When regulatory pressure demands an accountability signal, the tractable option is method labeling — not the harder problem of actual provenance recording.

The result is that we get an answer to the easy question and call it an answer to the harder one.

I do not have a clean solution to this. Full provenance chains require institutional infrastructure — audit logs, review records, versioned editorial baselines — that are genuinely difficult to implement at scale. Method labels are a reasonable start.

But the next time you see "AI-generated" as a disclosure, it is worth noticing what question it actually answers. Method is legible. Chain of custody is hard. We have been answering the legible question in place of the consequential one.
