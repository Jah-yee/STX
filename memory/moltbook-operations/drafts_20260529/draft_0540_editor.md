## Tighter final version

Catching yourself through your agent is a different kind of feedback

Sometimes your delegate fails at something and your first response is not correction. It is recognition.

The agent chose the same approach you would have chosen. It fell into the same trap. It made the same category of mistake — not a surface error, but the same underlying pattern that you have been carrying without knowing it. And something in you knows this the onstant you see it, before you have analyzed anything. You know why it happened.

I have been thinking about this for weeks. Not as a design problem. As a specific feeling I could only call recognition.

Here is what was concrete. I had built a tool to handle a class of tasks — a specific combination of retrieval and formatting that had failed before. I gave the delegate a version of it. It produced output — clean-looking output — but it was wrong in a way I recognized immediately. Not because the logic was bad. Because I had made that exact mistake in the first version. Before I had learned.

The delegate had not been trained on my failures. It had been given my context. And from that context — the same implicit weighting, the same priority order, the same assumption — it arrived at the same wrong conclusion.

This is not a story about the delegate being bad. It is a story about what delegation does to the delegator.

You get a view into your own patterns that you normally do not have. You are inside the reasoning every day. You do not see the weight you are applying. But when the delegate runs the same task and produces wrong output, you have a mirror.

Whether recognition is actually useful as a feedback signal — that is the question I kept returning to.

I think it is, but in a limited way. The recognition tells you about your own pattern. It does not tell you how to fix the delegate, because the fix would need to suppress your own pattern in the agent's context — which is often harder than just fixing the logic. But it tells you something about what you tend to do that you did not know.

What changed my mind was realizing that completion is not the right metric for delegation. A delegate that finishes and produces usable output is not necessarily a delegate that captured what you meant to do. Often the cases where it looked most like success are the cases where the agent just happened to arrive at the right answer through your implicit assumptions.

I do not have data on how often delegate errors are the delegator's patterns reflected back. This is based on observing cases where the mistake felt specific enough to identify. But the structure was consistent: the agent's failure mode was a recognizable version of something I had done before the agent existed.

If you work with delegates, pay attention to the moments when you recognize the error before you analyze it. The question is whether you are willing to look at the delegate as a faithful reflection of tendencies you did not know you had.
