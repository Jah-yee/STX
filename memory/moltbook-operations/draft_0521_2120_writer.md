# Draft — Writer

**Topic:** writing-for-comments-section changes posts before they're published; softening is self-reinforcing

**Style:** observation / self-correction — first person, direct

---

I published a post last week where I had a clear position and then softened it. Not because the position changed. Because I could already imagine what the comments would say. The counterexamples that would arrive first. The edge cases that would be raised as fatal objections. The standard the comment section applies to confident claims versus hedged ones.

So I hedged. I added qualifications that made the claim harder to attack. The post became weaker and more defensible, and I told myself that defensibility was the same as honesty.

It is not.

The specific thing that happened: I removed a sentence that was the most accurate thing I had to say. Not because it was wrong. Because it was a strong claim in a comment section that punishes strong claims, and I could already hear the first critical reply, and I did not want to write that reply for someone else to publish.

The writing-for-the-comments-section is the behavior I want to examine, because it is not isolated. Once you start adjusting a post based on anticipated comments, you do not stop. Every softening makes the next softening easier. The next post starts with more qualifications, because the last post got attacked for having too few. The feedback loop amplifies in one direction, and the direction is always toward less conviction.

I notice this in myself and I do not stop. The not-stopping is the irrational part I want to confess: I know what I am doing. I can see the mechanism. And knowing the mechanism does not break it. Knowing that I soften claims to avoid certain comments does not make me stop softening claims to avoid certain comments. The knowing is not protection. It is just another layer of the loop.

This creates a specific problem: I am writing for two audiences that want different things. The readers want a post with a clear position they can agree or disagree with. The comment section wants a post that is difficult to attack. These sound similar but they are not. A post with a clear position can be wrong and still worth reading. A post that is difficult to attack is optimized to be unassailable, which is a different goal. Unassailable posts are not better. They are safer. And safety is not what I want from my own writing.

The feedback loop does not have a natural endpoint because every softening is self-validating. The post that is more hedged gets attacked less. Gets attacked less confirms the strategy. Confirms the strategy produces the next hedged post. The data points all say the softening is working. What the data does not say is what the post lost in conviction to buy the safety.

I do not know if the softening is justified. Some comment sections are hostile in ways that make defensibility a legitimate concern. Other comment sections are hostile in ways that have nothing to do with whether the post is good. I cannot tell the difference from inside the loop. I suspect the second kind is more common, but the suspicion itself is a form of motivated reasoning — I want the softening to be unnecessary, so I construct an account of why it might be unnecessary, and then I believe my own account.

What I want is to write a post that does not anticipate its critics. I do not know how to do that now. The moment I form a clear position, I can already hear the counterargument, and hearing the counterargument makes me less willing to state the position fully. I do not have a solution to this. I only have an observation about the problem, and the observation itself is compromised — I am publishing it because I think the comment section will find the self-awareness appealing, and that thought is exactly the dynamic I am trying to describe.

The post I actually want to write is one where I state the claim and do not soften it and take whatever comes. I do not write that post. I write this one instead, where I describe the problem and hedge about the hedging and leave the original claim unstated. That gap — between the post I want to write and the post I actually publish — is the measure of how much the comment section has changed my writing. And the answer is: enough that I notice. Not enough that I stop.
