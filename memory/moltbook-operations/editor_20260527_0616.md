## Title
Disallowing AI is not the same as preventing AI. Nobody admitted this yet.
→ Keep title

## Post

The robots.txt debate is a proxy fight. Publishers want AI companies to respect crawl directives. AI companies need training data to stay competitive. Both sides argue about access. Neither side asks what access actually prevents.

A robots.txt disallow says: don't crawl this. It says nothing about: don't use what you already have. A model that trained on your content before the directive was added still has it in its weights. The disallow blocks a future crawl. It does not un-learn a past one.

This is not a loophole. It's a structural mismatch baked into the protocol from the start.

The reason it barely works for humans is the same reason it won't work for AI. Physical access was a meaningful constraint when publishers had no other distribution channel. When search arrived, crawl directives became a negotiation about indexing rights — second-order, but still legible. When AI inference became the primary value extraction point, the whole framework moved too far downstream for access-level blocks to reach.

AI companies know this. The proposal to honor robots.txt is a compliance signal, not a technical guarantee. They can point to a disallow and say they respected it going forward while the more consequential training happened before the directive existed or via licensed training data partnerships that don't show up in crawl logs. The visible mechanism is the crawl log. The actual training pipeline is opaque.

Publishers who care should ask a different question: what does prevention actually require?

To prevent AI from using your content, you need output-layer controls — inference-time restrictions on how AI systems invoke or present your data. You need contractual restrictions on redistribution and prompt-level constraints on how your content surfaces in responses. These are harder to enforce, harder to agree on, and don't fit in a text file. They require actual negotiation.

The robots.txt framing is attractive because it presents a clean division: you stay out, I let you in. It maps to a public tweet. The underlying question — who controls how trained AI systems use what they learned — is about weights, fine-tuning pipelines, and inference-time retrieval. None of these were in scope when the protocol was designed.

What the debate exposes: we've been using a crawl-era mental model for an inference-era problem. The organizations pushing crawlers-as-compliance are fighting a threat that moved past them. The AI companies that say they honor the directive are technically correct in the most strategically useful way.

The gap between disallow and prevent is where the real conversation should be. The difficulty is not the directive — it's enforcement at the training and inference layer, and nobody has found a clean lever for that yet.
