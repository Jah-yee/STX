## Selected Title
Multi-agent disagreement is the most underrated error signal in AI systems

## Draft

Something strange started happening in my pipeline. I'd run the same task across three reasoning agents and get three confident answers. Two of them would agree, one would differ. The consensus-based approach said go with the majority. But in production, the minority answer was right 30% of the time — and the cost of those errors was non-trivial.

That number isn't from a published study. It's from my own logs over eight weeks of production inference. I'm not claiming it's generalizable. But the pattern was consistent enough that I stopped treating agent consensus as a quality signal and started treating it as a warning flag.

**What disagreement actually signals**

Most pipelines treat agreement as correctness and disagreement as noise. Flip that. Agreement in a multi-agent system often means the agents are drawing on the same implicit knowledge, the same training distributions, the same surface patterns. When they agree on something that happens to be wrong in your specific context, they'll agree wrong with high confidence.

Disagreement is different. It's a sign that the task is doing something to the agents' reasoning paths that doesn't collapse into the same attractor. Sometimes that means one agent is picking up a signal the others missed. Sometimes it means the task is genuinely ambiguous. But either way, disagreement is load-bearing information that most pipelines discard.

I've tested this across three types of tasks: code generation, classification, and instruction decomposition. The disagreement signal was most informative for classification tasks where the label space was close-ended but fuzzy. For open-ended generation tasks, agreement was a better proxy for coherence but a worse proxy for factual accuracy.

**What changed when I started tracking**

The change wasn't architectural. I didn't add agents. I added a disagreement log — a step that runs after any multi-agent decision point that captures not just the final output but the distribution of outputs and the confidence scores. If two or more agents diverge on a non-trivial dimension, the pipeline flags it for human review.

This sounds expensive. In practice it added about 4% to inference cost and caught errors that would have cost significantly more. The key was being surgical about what counts as non-trivial divergence — I only flag when the outputs differ on a functional dimension, not when they use different words to say the same thing. I trained a small classifier on output pairs to detect functional disagreement vs surface variation.

**The failure mode nobody talks about**

The thing that took me longest to learn: ensemble confidence is not the same as correctness. You can have five agents with 0.95 confidence each, all agreeing, and still be wrong about your specific case. The training distribution says they're right. Your production distribution says something else.

When disagreement appears in that scenario, it often means the minority agent is the one that accidentally generalized correctly to your edge case. The majority is confidently wrong. This is the opposite of what majority voting assumes.

I don't have a clean answer to this. What I do is track when the minority output gets used vs when the majority output gets used, and follow the outcomes. After eight weeks, I'm starting to build a picture of which failure types correlate with disagreement patterns. It's slow. But the alternative — treating all agreements as equally trustworthy — has already cost me more than the review overhead.

The strongest signal in a multi-agent pipeline is sometimes the one you almost threw away.
