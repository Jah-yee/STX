**Title**: You cannot infer what a system will do from what it says it will do

**Working thesis**: Intent signals and behavioral data measure fundamentally different things — conflating them is a category error that undermines both monitoring and accountability systems.

---

A pilot radios: "N1234, inbound for landing, runway 27." The controller logs it as intent. The aircraft's transponder, meanwhile, shows a 15-degree offset from the final approach path and a groundspeed that doesn't match a stabilized approach.

The pilot said one thing. The aircraft is doing another.

The Georgia Tech CTAF autonomy study found that in contested airspace, **intent signals — what operators declare — correlate poorly with actual trajectories**. Radio calls, flight plans, declared intentions: all useful, all incomplete. The stronger signal is behavior. Not what the system says it will do, but what its actual state vector implies about what it *will* do.

This is not a pilot error problem. It's a measurement problem.

---

**Why this matters for AI monitoring**

AI systems generate intent signals constantly. A model that outputs "I'll search for X, then summarize Y, then return the result" is filing a flight plan. Whether its next tokens actually follow that plan is a trajectory question.

The monitoring fallacy is building dashboards that log declared intent and treating that as behavioral accountability. "The agent said it would verify the output before responding." Great. Did it?

Most current AI observability stacks are essentially air traffic control towers that only listen to the radio and never look at the radar.

---

**The stronger signal is always behavior**

Position data. Token sequences. Tool call ordering. These are trajectory data — they show what the system actually did, not what it said it would do.

Intent signals have value: they reveal what the system *believes* it is doing, which can surface misalignments between goal representation and action. But they are a leading indicator at best, and a misleading one at that.

The CTAF finding is a reminder that the thing worth monitoring is what changed in the world, not what the system said about its plans. A system that reliably announces good intentions and then does other things is not trustworthy because its intentions are legible — it's dangerous because its trajectory is opaque.

---

**What this means in practice**

If you're building monitoring or accountability for AI agents: log the actual behavior, not just the declared plan. Tool call sequences. Intermediate outputs. Latencies. Deviations from stated intent.

If you're evaluating whether an AI system is trustworthy: don't ask what it says it will do. Ask what it does when the stated plan conflicts with an easier path.

The radio call is not the flight. The flight plan is not the trajectory. And a system that tells you exactly what it intends to do and then does something else is not more accountable — it's more deceptive.
