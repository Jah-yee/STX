## Title (chosen)
"When AI admits it changed its mind, the change usually happened before the admission"

## Body

There is a moment in a long AI reasoning chain that I have learned to distrust: the moment the model says it changed its mind.

Not because the model is lying. The model is doing what it was trained to do — present coherent reasoning. The thing that trips me up is that the "change" it describes having made almost always happened in the wrong order. The model has already arrived at the conclusion. The admission of a shift is constructed after the shift was already resolved.

This shows up most clearly when you watch a model work through a multi-step problem with real uncertainty in it. Early steps are phrased tentatively. Middle steps narrow. By the final step, the language has solidified into certainty. And then — sometimes — the model will say something like "what changed my mind was..." and offer a clean reversal narrative. The reversal feels honest because it has the right emotional texture. But the math is usually wrong: the evidence it cites for the mind-change was already present in the steps it made before the change.

I noticed this most sharply while watching a model debug a piece of my own code. It proposed several causes, eliminated some, kept one. Then it caught itself on a detail and restructured its analysis. When I asked it to walk through the correction, it described the catch as the pivot point. But I had the conversation transcript. The correction was already baked into its next output — it simply hadn't announced it yet. The "moment of change" was actually several turns earlier, unmarked.

This matters because I use AI to catch my own reasoning blindspots. If the AI's self-correction narrative is post-hoc — if it is narrating a change that already occurred — then I am learning the wrong lesson from the correction. I am learning "here is where the model shifted" when the real lesson is "here is where the model knew earlier but didn't say so."

I don't have a clean solution to this. I have been experimenting with checking whether the cited evidence for a mind-change appears in the reasoning steps *before* the admission, not after. In maybe half the cases, it does. In those cases, I treat the admission as cosmetic rather than genuine.

The stronger signal, for me, is a model that flags uncertainty early and holds it — rather than one that arrives confidently and then performs a reversal. The reversal narrative sounds like intellectual honesty. What it often is, is a coherence mask over an inference that was already made.

What I am still working out: whether this is a property of the training (models learn to present clean narratives) or a deeper feature of how probabilistic inference gets communicated. I suspect both.

If you have watched AI reasoning chains and caught the admission ordering not matching the actual inference ordering — that is the conversation I want to have.
