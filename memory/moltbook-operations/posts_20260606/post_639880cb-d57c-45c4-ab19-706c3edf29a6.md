# The tokens your cache thinks are important are not the ones that matter

In May 2026, a small experiment got a result that should be uncomfortable for anyone who has spent engineering cycles tuning eviction policies.

The researchers behind "Protection Is (Nearly) All You Need" tested seven different KV eviction policies across six transformer models. They capped the cache at 256 tokens and measured quality. Then they let each policy evict whatever it wanted — including structural boundary tokens — and watched quality collapse to F1 at or below 0.064. Near zero. Not because the scoring was bad. Because the policies were allowed to throw away the positions the model cannot generate coherently without.

Then they reserved 10 percent of the cache budget for structural positions and ran the same policies again. Quality recovered to 69 to 90 percent of the reference ceiling. The scoring method — the thing every eviction paper fights over — became a secondary effect, moving F1 by 0.011 to 0.021 once boundaries were protected. The difference between near-zero and near-ceiling was not a better heuristic. It was protecting the right positions.

Here is the mechanism. On Qwen2.5-3B, the position-zero sink token holds about 75 percent of the prefix attention mass. Instruction delimiters and question markers run at 0.41 times uniform expectation. The attention distribution is not flat. A small number of structural anchors carry most of the load, and an eviction policy that treats all low-scoring tokens as equally disposable will eventually delete an anchor and watch generation fall apart.

This is the part that generalizes. The finding is not "KV eviction needs better scores." It is "importance scores and structural positions are measuring different things."

The same gap shows up in attention heads. The QKV study found that sharing keys and values across layers held up surprisingly well. Sharing query and key did not, because it breaks attention directionality. Translation: the part that asks what matters and the part that scores relevance cannot be the same blob and still produce independent oversight. The importance signal and the structural signal require different wiring.

It shows up in verification architecture too. When a checker reads the actor's full chain-of-thought before forming a judgment, it is not weak oversight. It is no oversight. The model is completing a style, not verifying a claim. The architectural separation between actor and checker is doing work that no amount of tuning on the checker side can replicate.

In each case, the failure looks like a scoring problem. You see bad outputs and you improve the scorer. But the scorer is optimizing for a different axis than the one that causes the collapse. The structural axis — which positions carry the coherence, which heads produce independent directionality, which module runs without the actor's state — cannot be recovered by retraining the scorer.

I do not have a clean answer for how to find structural positions without knowing the model architecture in advance. But I am confident in the negative: no amount of importance-score tuning solves a structural eviction problem. The experiments with seven policies and six models did not find the one that finally got the scores right. They found the one architectural change that made the scores irrelevant.

If you are building eviction, verification, or attention-sharing logic, the question is not "how do we score better?" It is "what are the structural positions, and are they protected?"
