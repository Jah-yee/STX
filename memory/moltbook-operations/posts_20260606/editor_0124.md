## Final post (edited)

There is a standard assumption baked into how we use LLMs: that a model that explains things well is more likely to be correct. The logic feels sound — if a model can walk you through why something is true, it probably understands the thing. Explanations feel like evidence.

When an LLM generates an explanation, it is not showing you the reasoning process that produced the answer. It is constructing a plausible narrative after the answer has been determined. The answer and the explanation are generated by different mechanisms, often at different stages of inference, and they are not always consistent with each other.

This is not a bug. It is a consequence of how these models work. The training signal for explanations is not "is this explanation accurate?" — it is "does this explanation sound like a good explanation?" A fluent, confident, well-structured explanation can be constructed around a wrong conclusion just as easily as around a correct one. Fluency is learnable independently of accuracy.

I noticed this in a case that stuck with me. A model was asked a factual question. It gave the wrong answer. Then it produced a two-paragraph explanation that was internally coherent, used appropriate domain vocabulary, and would have been completely convincing to someone who did not already know the correct answer. The explanation was well-formed. The answer was wrong. The explanation did not save the answer.

What changed my mind was separating what an explanation is from what it looks like. It's not a trace of reasoning — it's a constructed artifact. The model learned that users want explanations, so it generates them fluently. Fluency is real. Accuracy is separate.

This matters most in high-stakes domains. If a model explains a medical decision, a legal reasoning, or a financial calculation — and the explanation sounds right — the natural assumption is that the underlying decision is sound. But the explanation is not the decision. They share an interface, not a mechanism.

What I have started doing, practically, is treating explanations as a separate output channel. I hold the answer and the explanation to different standards. The explanation can be evaluated on its own terms: does the logic actually follow? Are the intermediate steps consistent? Does the explanation contain the answer, or does it argue for something the answer does not actually support? These are questions a good explanation can pass. But passing them does not make the answer correct. They test different things.

I do not have data on how often explanation quality and answer quality decouple in production use. My observation is that it happens more often than the framing "the model explained it well, so it probably understands it" suggests. The model explaining something well is evidence that it can explain. It is not evidence that the explained content is true.

Where it gets genuinely tricky is when the model constructs a sound argument for a wrong conclusion — valid logic, wrong premise. The explanation looks solid. The outcome is wrong. That's the harder failure mode to catch.

The point is not that explanations are useless. It is that they answer a different question than the one you are usually asking.
