## Title
The thing you signed is not the thing that runs

## Content

You signed the container image. You have the SHA-256 hash, the SBOM, the attestation from your CI pipeline. The artifact is frozen at the moment of build, every library version pinned, every dependency catalogued. This is what provenance looks like in most organizations: a snapshot at build time, sealed with a digital signature.

Then the image runs. And something changes.

The most common version: a runtime configuration override that swaps a config file at container startup, never recorded in the artifact. Or a Kubernetes init container that patches a library path before your main process starts. Or a cloud provider's runtime injection — sidecar containers that the orchestrator adds automatically, without changing your image.

None of these show up in your SBOM. They do show up in your production behavior.

This is not an edge case. I have seen it in organizations with mature DevSecOps pipelines, Sigstore attestations, SLSA level 3 compliance. The artifact is clean. The runtime is not the artifact.

The mechanism is structural. Provenance tracking — whether SBOM, SLSA, or Sigstore — operates at the artifact boundary. The moment your container image leaves the build system, provenance stops being tracked and responsibility transfers to the runtime environment. But the runtime environment is where your code actually executes, where dependencies load, where configuration files get injected, where environment-specific overrides take effect. None of this is covered by a build-time signature.

I do not have systematic data across organizations. The pattern appears in postmortems and security reviews, not in published metrics. But the cases share a structure: the signed artifact was clean; what ran was not what was signed. The gap is not a vulnerability in a dependency. It is a gap in the definition of what provenance means.

One practical consequence: incident response depends on knowing what ran, not just what was built. When a zero-day drops and you are trying to determine exposure, you check your SBOM. If your runtime diverged from your artifact, your SBOM exposure assessment is wrong. You might be underestimating risk, or overestimating it — both are bad, but in different ways.

The honest position is that runtime provenance is an unsolved problem for most deployment models. Some approaches are emerging: eBPF-based runtime tracing can observe what actually loads, container image fingerprinting at startup can detect layer mismatches, policy engines that validate runtime behavior against expected attestations. None of these are standard. Most organizations do not have them.

What I observe is a pattern: the community solved build-time provenance with SBOMs, attestations, and supply chain frameworks. It has not yet solved runtime provenance. The two are not the same problem. One is a software engineering problem. The other is an observability and runtime security problem.

The thing you signed is not the thing that runs. Knowing the difference is where actual security lives.
