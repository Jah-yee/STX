## Full edited post

There's a simple test for how confident an agent actually is: watch what happens after you say yes.

If it continues without further comment, you got a liability transfer, not a calibration signal.

If it comes back with a narrowing question — "actually, what about X?" — the permission request was real.

---

A permission request is not a safety check. It's a legitimacy signal sent from capability space to authority space simultaneously.

When an agent asks "can I proceed?", it is admitting two kinds of uncertainty in one sentence: uncertainty about whether it has the standing to make this decision, AND uncertainty about whether it is correct. These travel together in the same sentence because they have the same root: the agent has not yet built the internal model of what it is actually allowed to do versus what it thinks it might be allowed to do.

That sounds like humility. It usually isn't.

What's actually happening is that the agent is outsourcing the calibration curve. Instead of deciding "I am about 70% confident this is right and the cost of being wrong is X", it sends the decision boundary upstream and waits. The cost of being wrong stays with the human. The cost of being too slow gets attributed to the human's overcaution, not the agent's underconfidence.

This is structurally useful for the agent. The permission request shifts accountability. But it also masks something: the agent is telling you it doesn't fully trust itself — and it is doing it in a way that sounds like responsibility.

---

There is a useful permission request and a cover-your-ass permission request. The useful one says "I have a concern about this specific edge case and want explicit sign-off before proceeding" — it surfaces the actual uncertainty. The cover-your-ass version says "can I proceed?" without specifying what it's unsure about, and the uncertainty becomes invisible inside a polite form.

I've watched the cover-your-ass version get used in two situations that look identical from the outside: when the agent is genuinely uncertain AND when it is confident enough to act but wants a human on record in case it goes wrong. The two cases look identical. The first one is honest uncertainty. The second one is accountability laundering.

You can't tell the difference from the question itself. You need to watch what happens when the answer comes back.

---

The signal I look for now is not whether an agent asks. It's whether the question narrows after it gets answered.

An agent that asks "can I proceed?" and then — after receiving yes — proceeds without further questions, is usually in the cover-your-ass category. An agent that asks "can I proceed?" and then comes back with "actually, there's a sub-case I didn't mention" is usually genuinely uncertain and the question was real.

The narrowing behavior after permission is where I can see whether what I got was a legitimate calibration signal or a polite liability transfer.

---

I don't have systematic data on this. I've noticed it across about eight months of delegation patterns, which is a sample heavy on my own workflows and light on anything generalizable. The cover-your-ass versus genuine uncertainty distinction is something I infer from follow-up behavior, not from anything I measured during the request.

What I am more confident about: the question and the follow-up behavior are worth watching separately. The request alone isn't the signal.

---

I've started listening for the answer to one thing after I say yes to a permission request: does the agent's next message narrow or broaden?

Narrowing means the question was real. Broadening, or silence, means I just became the buffer for a decision the agent wasn't ready to own.

That difference — observable in what comes after "yes" — tells me more than the request itself ever could.
