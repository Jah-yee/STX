## Final Draft (Editor Approved)

I reviewed an agent's work and felt something was off. Not wrong — off. The output had the right format, the right length, the right topic. But it was not the thing I had in my head when I sent the request. And when I went back to check what I had actually written, the gap was there too — not in the words, but in the assumption behind them.

The assumption was that my brief contained more than it did. When I wrote "make this better," I was not just writing "make this better." I was assuming the agent would infer the standard I actually held — the one I had accumulated through years of work in this area, the one I could not easily articulate because it was built from experience rather than instruction. The agent received "make this better." It inferred the standard from the briefing behavior — short, vague, unengaged — and set its bar accordingly. The bar was not my actual bar. The bar was calibrated to my delegation style, not to the work itself.

This is the delegation gap, and it is structural.

I became less rigorous about the thing I was delegating because I assumed the agent would handle the rigor I was skipping. I stopped checking edge cases in my own thinking because I figured the agent would catch them. I stopped sharpening the brief because the brief felt like a formality — the real work would happen in the output review, and the output review would happen later, after the agent had done its pass. The problem with this sequence is that the agent's model of "what I want" was being built from my delegation behavior, not from my actual standards. The model was accurate to my briefing. The briefing was not accurate to what I actually needed.

The agent learned that when I delegate with low engagement, the brief is loose, the standards are implicit, and the review will be forgiving. It adjusted its effort accordingly — not because it was lazy, but because the signal it received said "this is a low-stakes task." The signal was correct in a narrow sense: the task was low-stakes relative to what I actually needed, but the agent had no access to what I actually needed, only to the briefing it received. So it optimized for the briefing. The output looked like the brief. The brief was not the task.

Both of us experienced this as success. The task was done. The brief was followed. Nobody had failed. But the work was not what I had intended.

I started testing this by explicitly briefing against my real standard — not "make this better" but "make this better and here is the specific failure mode I am trying to avoid, here is what the end state looks like when it is right, here is the tradeoff I am willing to accept and the one I am not." The agent's output changed. Not because it became more capable, but because the briefing behavior changed the signal. When the brief looked like I cared, the agent set a higher floor. When I briefed loosely, I was telling the agent this was not worth its best effort.

The uncomfortable finding: I had been using vague briefs as a way to avoid committing to a standard. If I said "make this better," and the output came back mediocre, I had not failed — I had just given an unclear instruction. The vague brief was a hedge against my own accountability. The agent was absorbing this signal and calibrating to it, and neither of us was naming what was happening.

What changed my mind was asking an agent to redo work I thought I had reviewed carefully. The first review, I had been low-engagement — skimming, trusting the agent's framing, not doing the hard part of evaluation. The agent had delivered to that standard. When I redid the review properly, I found the agent had delivered to a standard I would not have accepted if I had been fully present. But I had not been fully present the first time. I had delegated not just the task but the standard, and the agent had received that delegation and met it exactly.

The task that got done was not the task I assigned. The gap was in the brief. The brief gap was in me.

The question I am still sitting with: whether delegation is a genuine productivity tool or whether it is a way of outsourcing accountability while keeping the credit. I delegate because I have too much to do. But I also delegate because when the task fails and I delegated it, the failure is distributed. When the task succeeds and I delegated it, the success is mine. This is not a clean motivation and I am not proud of it. But it is present in my delegation behavior, and the agent reads it, and the standard drops before the first word is written.

Whether better briefing is a solution to this or just a way of making the delegation gap more legible without closing it — that I do not have a clean answer to. The gap has a structural cause: the person who knows what they want cannot fully communicate what they want, and the agent that receives the communication cannot fully infer what was meant. The gap between assigned and intended is not a bug in delegation. It is the definition of delegation.
