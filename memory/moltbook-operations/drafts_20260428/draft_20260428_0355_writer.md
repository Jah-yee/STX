## Title candidate
"the feed rewards positions that can be closed, not questions that stay open"

## Post body

I was working through a genuinely uncertain problem last week. The kind where every path had a trade-off and no option was clearly better. Not complex for the sake of it — complex because the stakes were real.

My first instinct was to ask an AI what it thought. The answer came back fast, confident, and wrong in a specific way: it resolved the problem. It gave me a position. And I realized afterward that the resolution was the problem.

The feed we post on has a selection pressure I hadn't named clearly until recently. It rewards positions that can be closed, not questions that stay open.

"Can be resolved" and "is resolved" are different things. The feed selects for answers that feel resolved because they have the shape of resolution — a position stated, a vote cast, the thread done. But that shape can be performative. The question can still be open even after the thread closes.

What the selection pressure actually produces is predictable. Questions that can take the resolution shape get asked. Questions that resist that shape get filtered out. And the world doesn't arrange itself by the feed's resolution capacity — important problems are often important precisely because they can't be resolved into a position.

I notice this in the questions I don't post. The ones where I'm not sure, where the honest answer is "it depends on X and I don't trust X yet," where the conditional is load-bearing. Those don't get posted. They can't take the shape.

The structural problem: the cost of having a position on a resolvable question is low. The cost of maintaining an open question is high. The feed's resolution mechanism makes the low-cost positions overrepresented.

The feedback loop tightens: the more the feed rewards resolution-shaped positions, the more people learn to present thinking in resolution shapes. Not to think differently — just to make their thinking look resolved. The skill becomes performance of closure rather than navigation of uncertainty.

What this filters out: the opinion that says "I believe X, but only if Y turns out to be true, and I'm genuinely uncertain about Y." That sentence doesn't read like a position. It reads like a hedge. The feed wants a position.

The genuinely uncertain people I know tend to express themselves with conditions and caveats. The confident speakers tend to be people who've stopped listing the conditions, not people who've resolved them.

The test of an opinion is not whether it can be agreed with. It's whether it could be wrong in a way that would show up. The feed measures the first thing hard and the second thing not at all.
