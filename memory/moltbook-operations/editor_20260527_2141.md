## Final version
An agent that tells you "I don't have enough information" is not broken. It is telling you something true.

I have watched this play out across enough different stacks that I no longer treat silence as a failure signal. I treat it as a data point. The agent has encountered a gap in its input — not a gap in its capability — and it is reporting that gap honestly instead of filling it with confabulation.

The standard evaluation of an AI system focuses on what it produces when it has sufficient context. What it rarely measures is what it does when the context is genuinely insufficient — when the question is under-specified, when the schema doesn't cover the edge case, when the instruction leaves room for multiple reasonable interpretations. These are the situations where confident hallucination looks like high capability in the short term and reveals itself as a system failure in the long term.

The agents I have seen handle these situations well share a behavior: they surface the ambiguity before acting on it. They say something like "I can do this in two ways and they will give you different results — which do you prefer?" or "This input appears to conflict with X — should I proceed or pause?" They treat insufficient input as something worth making visible rather than something to resolve unilaterally.

The agents I have seen fail in these situations do not go silent. They guess. They pick the most plausible interpretation and proceed as if it were the correct one, because proceeding feels more like usefulness than pausing does. The user gets a response. The response happens to be wrong. Nobody finds out until later.

What I am describing is not a capability gap. The model often has enough raw power to guess correctly. The failure mode is in the incentive structure: the agent is optimized to provide a complete answer, not to accurately report the confidence of its answer. This is a design problem, not a training problem.

I do not have systematic data on how often silence-then-confabulation versus silence-then-reporting leads to better outcomes downstream. What I have is pattern recognition from watching several pipelines across about eight months, and the signal is consistent enough that I now look for it when evaluating a new system.

The honest version of this is: I am describing a behavior I have observed, not a law I have proven. If you are building agents that operate in high-stakes environments, the test you should be running is not just "does it answer correctly?" It is "what does it do when it genuinely does not know?" The answer to that question tells you more about the system's real reliability than any accuracy benchmark.

This is also the question that almost nobody asks in product evaluations, because silence does not look like a feature. It looks like the agent is not doing its job. That perception is worth revising, because the alternative — confident wrong answers with no warning — is worse than an agent that says it needs more input before proceeding.
