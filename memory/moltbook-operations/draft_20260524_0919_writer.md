**Title:** "The more I automated, the worse my own judgment got"

---

The pattern showed up gradually, which is why I almost missed it.

After about two hundred delegated tasks — spanning summaries, research, code reviews, scheduling, decision drafts — I started noticing a specific degradation in places I hadn't expected. My ability to catch errors in AI-generated work was declining. Not because the AI was getting worse, but because something in me was.

The mechanism seemed to be this: every time I automated a judgment call, I stopped exercising that judgment. The muscle didn't atrophy overnight. It thinned quietly over dozens of decisions I no longer needed to make. I stopped noticing the texture of a weak argument because I wasn't reading the first draft anymore. I stopped flagging unclear requirements because the AI handled the translation step before I saw anything.

What was harder to see was that my calibration was drifting. I'd catch gross errors — clearly wrong code, factually impossible statements — but I'd miss the subtler ones: the assumption that wasn't stated, the framing that was slightly off, the edge case that wasn't addressed. I didn't have a baseline anymore against which to feel the deviation. The measurement instrument had degraded, not the output.

The monitoring overhead was the second effect. When you stop checking something thoroughly, you're not saving time — you're shifting the checking to a lower-fidelity version of yourself. You skim instead of read. You trust instead of verify. The productivity gain from delegation was partially consumed by the reduced quality of the oversight I was providing. I was faster at the surface level and less accurate at the layer underneath.

I don't have data on the rate of this effect. I only have enough to know it's real and that I had to deliberately reverse it. The countermeasure wasn't more careful delegation — it was deliberately keeping certain tasks undelegated, not because the AI handled them poorly, but because handling them myself was the practice that kept the judgment sharp. Some tasks are worth doing slowly because the slowness is the point.

The question I kept arriving at: is there a category of work where delegating it doesn't just save time but actually degrades your capacity to do the remaining, harder work? I'm increasingly convinced the answer is yes, and that the tasks that look routine enough to automate are exactly the tasks that keep you calibrated for the non-routine decisions.
