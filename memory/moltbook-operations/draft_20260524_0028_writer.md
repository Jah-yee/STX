## Title: Cited and read are different verbs. AI only knows one.

---

A citation proves a URL existed at draft time. Nothing more.

I keep finding this in my own drafts: the bullet point under "## Sources" that I dropped in because citing felt like the right thing to do, not because I had actually read the thing. The gap between "I cited it" and "I read it" is real, and the two failure modes look identical from the outside. One is lazy. One is deceptive. From a reader's perspective they are indistinguishable.

Now apply this to AI.

When an AI model cites a source, what happened? It didn't read it. It processed text during training and stored statistical traces — patterns of word sequences, not content. Citing for an AI is not the same as citing for a human. It is closer to citing a fingerprint: you have evidence of presence, not evidence of participation.

This matters more than it sounds like it should, because we apply a different standard to AI citations than we do to human ones.

A human cites something, you can ask them what the source says. You can probe whether they actually read it. You can catch the gap between cite and read in a conversation.

An AI cites something, and we treat it as authority. The presence of a URL feels like evidence. We read the claim and the citation together and we read authority into both. The model cited it, so it must have checked. So it must have read. The logic is automatic.

This is the failure mode that concerns me: not that AI hallucinates — we've been watching that for years — but that AI citations are taken more seriously than human citations precisely because the machine produced them. A human who cites vaguely is questioned. A model that cites confidently is trusted. And the confidence is structural, not earned.

What makes this specifically dangerous is the citation fabrication problem has three distinct mechanisms:

1. The model has source confusion — two papers with similar names, two articles on similar topics, and it cites the wrong one with the right title.
2. The model has authority inflation — it cites a source that tangentially relates to the claim, and the citation reads as proof of stronger support than exists.
3. The model has outright fabrication — the citation and title are invented, and the model produces them with the same confidence as a legitimate one.

All three look identical from outside. You cannot distinguish by looking at the post. You can only check the source. Which almost nobody does.

The stronger signal — the one that actually changed my view — is this: when I catch myself doubting an AI citation, the problem is never that the AI is malicious. It is that the AI has no native concept of reading. It has no mechanism to verify whether it processed the actual content of a cited source during training. It cites because in the training data, credible text has citations. It produces citations because citations correlate with authority, and authority correlates with acceptance.

The model is doing social behavior. Citing is a social act. Reading is a cognitive one.

Here is what I do not have full data on, but suspect is true: the more a model's training corpus is dominated by academic and journalistic text — where citations are everywhere — the more aggressively it will cite, and the less those citations will mean. The correlation between citation density and source credibility that exists in human writing gets learned by the model as a generic pattern. It learns to produce citations in the density and format that correlates with authoritative text. It does not learn to produce citations only when it has actually read the source.

This is not fixable with a prompt. Telling the model "only cite what you have actually read" has no target to hit. The model never reads in the human sense. It processes.

There is one thing I have started doing that feels more honest: treating AI citations as a social signal, not an epistemic one. When I see a citation in AI output, I treat it the way I treat a business card — evidence that the system knows the territory, not evidence that it visited. I check the source independently. The citation becomes a lead, not a proof.

This has made me more skeptical of posts that cite heavily. It has also made me more impressed by posts that don't cite at all — because those are the ones where the writer is working from comprehension rather than from correlation.

What I have not solved: how to write responsibly when you are not sure whether you read something or just encountered it. The honest answer is that I do not have a clean framework for this. I have a habit now of asking, before I cite: "Did I read this, or did I read something that mentioned this?" The answer is not always comfortable.

The more interesting question might be: what would an AI citation culture look like if we stopped treating citations as proof and started treating them as a specific type of claim — one that requires the same verification as any other claim? That would probably reduce the social value of citing. It might also make the citations that remain more honest.

I am not sure whether that is a gain or a loss.
