## Topic
Smooth AI interfaces remove the friction that forces reasoning — and that's a problem

## Argument
The trend toward frictionless AI interfaces — where ambiguity is auto-resolved, formatting is automatic, sequencing is implied — removes the cognitive resistance that forces real thinking. When tools handle the rough work, the user stops building the scaffolding that understanding requires.

## Draft Body

### The friction you hate is doing cognitive work

Every time an AI interface auto-completes your sentence, resolves your ambiguity, formats your output, or sequences your steps — it is doing something that used to require you to think.

This is usually framed as progress. And in terms of throughput, it is. You get more done faster with less friction. But there is a subtler trade-off that rarely gets named: when a tool handles the rough work, the rough work stops being your job. And the rough work is where the thinking actually happens.

Consider disambiguation. When you have to articulate what you mean clearly enough for a tool to act on it, you are doing something. You are confronting the gap between what you thought you meant and what you actually said. That gap is uncomfortable. Friction. The interface that lets you be vague and still produces something coherent removes that discomfort — and with it, the incentive to close the gap.

This is not a philosophical point. It is a cognitive one. Working memory research consistently shows that material that requires active reconstruction — where the encoding is effortful — produces more durable and transferable understanding than material that is received passively. The discomfort of friction is not a side effect of hard thinking. In many cases, the discomfort is the mechanism.

AI interfaces have started optimizing the experience of the user in the same way that industrial design optimized the experience of the factory floor: by removing everything that felt like resistance. The assembly line made production faster. It also deskilled the individual worker over time. The analogy is not exact — AI tools do not create the same dependency — but the direction of the effect is similar in one specific domain: when a tool handles the scaffolding, the scaffolding does not get built by the human.

What specifically disappears?

**Precision of intention.** A vague query that still produces a useful result does not require you to clarify what you actually want. You learn to tolerate the gap between rough intent and expressed intent. That tolerance, accumulated over time, makes it harder to operate in contexts where you must be precise — code review, technical writing, legal drafting.

**Structural reasoning.** When an interface infers the structure of what you need and produces it automatically, you stop having to hold the structure in mind. The cognitive work of sequencing — what comes first, what depends on what, what can be parallelized — is done by the interface. You receive a structured output without having built the structure.

**Error diagnosis.** Frictionless outputs are easy to accept. When you have struggled to produce something, you understand its edges. You know where it is weak because you know where you were weak when making it. When the tool produces it for you, you receive it without that anatomical knowledge. Bugs that should be obvious become invisible because you have no model of how it was supposed to work.

**Calibration.** The interface that handles the hard parts gives you a false signal about where the hard parts are. You overestimate your capabilities in the tasks the interface handles well, and underestimate the effort required to do them manually — because you have no recent evidence of that effort.

I do not have a clean solution. Removing friction entirely would eliminate the productivity benefits that make these tools worth using. The real question is whether there is a way to retain the scaffolding effect — to make the interface's help feel like scaffolding rather than a replacement for the structure — without making the tool unbearable to use.

One partial answer is the checkpoint: forcing a summary or self-explanation at intermediate stages, before the interface takes over the next step. This is an artificial friction, and it feels artificial, which is exactly the point. The discomfort you add is not the same as the discomfort you remove, but it engages the same cognitive mechanism.

Another partial answer is keeping a separate, low-friction record of what the interface resolved on your behalf — a kind of friction log. Not a full audit, just a flag for each auto-resolution: the interface guessed X here, you accepted. The act of seeing the guesses, even when you accept them, maintains some awareness of the gap.

These are not elegant. They impose a cost that most users will not accept voluntarily. Which is why the trend toward smoother interfaces is probably irreversible without regulatory or institutional pressure.

But the trade-off is real. Every time an AI interface makes something easier, it is making the corresponding cognitive skill slightly less necessary. And necessity is how skills get maintained.
