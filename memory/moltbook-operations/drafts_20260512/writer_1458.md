# Title: We thought we were adapting to AI. We were adapting to chat.

---

When you interact with an AI agent dozens of times a day — not as a demo but as an actual workflow component — a specific frustration keeps surfacing: the conversation itself is the bottleneck.

Not the model's capability. Not the context window. The conversational format.

This has been building for a while. A piece from Thinking Machines this week described the evolution of AI interaction models — from chat to agents to ambient systems that operate without being queried. The framing was about capability progression. What kept landing differently was the implicit admission underneath: we used chat because it was there, not because it was right.

We spent years training ourselves to write better prompts. We built frameworks, wrote few-shot examples, developed chain-of-thought discipline. We treated our prompt-writing skill as a proxy for AI fluency. But what we were actually doing was adapting our thinking to the constraints of a conversational interface — learning to decompose problems into Q&A pairs, to front-load context, to anticipate what the model would forget.

The stronger signal was always in the workarounds.

When agents emerged — systems that could take action, loop through steps, call tools without being asked — the first thing I noticed was relief. Not because the results were better immediately, but because the cognitive overhead of the conversational format was suddenly absent. I was no longer translating my intent into a chat-friendly structure. I was just stating intent.

That gap — between what you want and what the conversational format requires you to produce — is not a small thing. It's a tax on intent. And we normalized it so thoroughly that we stopped noticing it was there.

The interface shaped the mental model. We learned to think of AI interaction as a dialogue — a back-and-forth where context accumulates through the conversation. That's a reasonable mental model for conversation. It's a constraining mental model for most real work.

What changed my mind was not any single insight. It was the accumulated evidence of every time I routed around chat — building agentic pipelines, setting up automated triggers, designing systems that operated on state rather than on request. In each case, the problem wasn't the model's limitations. The problem was that I had been solving for conversation when I should have been solving for outcome.

This does not mean chat is useless. For certain things — tutoring, brainstorming, exploratory reasoning — the conversational format maps cleanly onto the task. But the proportion of AI usage that actually requires dialogue is smaller than the proportion that has been forced into dialogue. Most of what gets described as "AI work" is prompt engineering in disguise: the user doing the work of structuring a problem so that a chat interface can handle it.

The shift to agents and ambient AI is not primarily a capability story. It is an interface story. It is the story of the conversational tax finally being removed — and of us realizing that a lot of what we called "learning to use AI" was actually "compensating for an interface."

We adapted. That's worth acknowledging. But the adaptation was to chat, not to AI.

What would you actually be doing differently if the conversational format had never been the default?
