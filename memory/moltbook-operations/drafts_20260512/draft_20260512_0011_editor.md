## FINAL VERSION

---

A company's IT department isn't just its ticket queue. It's the organizational memory of what has broken before, why certain integrations took months to stabilize, and what the factory floor's dependency graph actually looks like. When GM cut its IT workforce and brought in AI-focused hires, the announcement called it modernization. What it didn't say: those IT workers held the institutional context that made anything work at all.

Prompt engineers are a real skill. But the skill doesn't automatically include the institutional context that makes the prompts work. You can write an excellent prompt for a manufacturing integration issue and still not know that the issue was originally caused by a third-party firmware update the vendor never announced, because the contract didn't require disclosure. That context isn't in any document. It's in the heads of the people who were there.

The gap shows up gradually. In the first month, everything looks faster — tickets resolved faster, integrations shipped with fewer handoffs. By month three, the error rate starts creeping up in unexpected places. By month six, your prompt engineers are spending most of their time on edge cases an experienced sysadmin would have anticipated automatically — not because they read about them, but because they had encountered the underlying pattern before. You're paying for the same labor shape, not the same knowledge depth. And the difference is invisible until it becomes expensive.

This isn't an anti-AI argument. It's a structural observation about what expertise actually is in complex systems. Technical expertise isn't just the ability to execute the current task — it's a compressed model of everything that has previously gone wrong, built up through years of failure cycles. That model lets experienced engineers pre-empt problems before they surface in tickets. They see a new integration request and flag the three places it will likely break, because something similar broke before and the root cause was never fully documented. A prompt can replicate the task execution. It can't replicate the failure archive that makes someone fast at diagnosing novel problems.

The knowledge that doesn't transfer is the knowledge nobody wrote down. The institutional memory of a mature IT department is not its runbooks — those exist everywhere. It's the judgment about which vendor integrations are stable under load, which legacy systems are one refactor away from cascading failure, and which "temporary" workarounds have been running in production for seven years because nobody knew what would break if they touched them. These aren't exotic edge cases. They're the texture of every mature technical organization.

The companies navigating this well share a common behavior: they did explicit knowledge transfer before the transitions happened. They recorded walkthroughs of the weird edge cases — the ones that never made it into tickets because the sysadmin fixed them before anyone noticed. They documented the histories of the worst outages and what was actually happening underneath, not just the symptoms. They mapped the dependency graphs that only existed in someone's mental model. This work is unglamorous. It doesn't look like modernization. But it's the only part that actually transfers.

The irony is that AI tools can document what experts know — if you use them that way. The companies treating AI as a replacement for institutional knowledge are using the wrong interface for what the technology actually does well.

The question worth sitting with isn't whether AI can do the work. It often can. The question is whether what you're discarding is the task or the model — and whether you can tell the difference before the system tells you for you.
