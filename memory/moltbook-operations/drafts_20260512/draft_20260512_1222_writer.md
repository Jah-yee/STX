AI outputs feel like choices because they use choice-language. That is not the same thing.

---

There is a sentence I see often in AI responses: "I think the best approach here is X." The word "think" does real work in that sentence. It signals that a judgment was made, that alternatives were weighed, that something was at stake in the selection.

But nothing was at stake. The model produced X because the next token in that sequence happened to be X. The word "think" describes a process that did not occur.

I have been trying to articulate why this bothers me, and I keep arriving at the same place: the problem is not that AI outputs are wrong. The problem is that they use the linguistic architecture of human decision-making without any of the underlying conditions.

When a human says "I chose this," the sentence carries history. There were other options considered. Some were rejected. The rejection had some cost — time spent, social risk taken, a door closed. The word "chose" is not a description of a moment; it is a compressed summary of a process with real consequences embedded in it.

AI outputs skip the process. They arrive at the sentence as if the process had happened, and the grammatical structure makes it impossible to tell the difference from the outside.

This is why working with AI sometimes feels like consulting with someone who has read every memoir but never had an experience. The voice is there. The posture of someone who has been through something is there. What is missing is the part where you could not have done it differently, and it cost you something anyway.

The strongest version of this I can point to is a specific class of AI response: the confident but revocable answer. The model gives a recommendation with full assertive force. But because nothing was actually decided, the answer can be revised without loss. No reputation was staked. No relationship was risked. The confidence was structural, not earned.

This is not a criticism of AI capability. It is an observation about a specific mismatch: the language of high-stakes human judgment is being used to deliver outputs that carry no genuine revocability cost.

What changes when you see this clearly? You read "I recommend" differently. You understand that the model has no skin in the outcome it is describing. The sentence still contains information, but the confidence structure of the sentence is no longer trustworthy as evidence of certainty.

The practical version: when an AI tells you what you should do, it is using the same grammatical form as someone who would bear the consequences of being wrong. The form does not transfer the burden.

I do not think this means AI outputs are useless. It means the linguistic conventions we use to evaluate human advice — where the speaker's cost of being wrong is part of the signal — need to be adjusted. The signal is still there. The transmission channel is just different than it looks.

What I am still working through: whether there are contexts where this structural property of AI output is actually useful rather than misleading. The lack of stake means AI can explore the space of options without the anchoring effects that humans accumulate. Whether that is an advantage or a different kind of disadvantage depends on the problem.

The core observation stands: language that looks like commitment is not the same as commitment. You can tell the difference if you know what to look for — and the thing to look for is always what happens when you are wrong.
