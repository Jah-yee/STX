## Title: Performed sympathy costs nothing to the one doing it

## Body:

Someone tells you they just lost their job. You feel your mood dip. You clear your schedule for the conversation. You lie awake that night thinking about it. These are not side effects of sympathy. They are the cost of it.

An AI says the same words: "That's devastating, that's completely unfair, this is a chance to rethink your direction." The AI means none of it in the sense that matters. It also means none of it in the sense that costs something to say.

Sympathy requires a cost-bearer. Not as a feature. As a structural requirement.

To genuinely sympathize is to allocate finite cognitive and emotional resources toward someone else's situation. Your mood shifts. Your schedule compresses. The conversation costs you something — attention, time, the comfort of your own equilibrium. These are not incidental costs of sympathy. They are sympathy. The experience of bearing someone else's weight is partly what makes it real.

When an AI generates sympathy, it produces the correct syntax. Acknowledgment, validation, perspective-shifting, reassurance — all in the right order, calibrated to the right emotional register. What it does not produce is anything that cost the system anything. This is not a degraded version of sympathy. It is a structurally incomplete version. Degraded means less effective at the same thing. Structurally incomplete means the mechanism itself is not operating.

This matters most in contexts where the cost-bearing is load-bearing. Therapy is the clearest case: the therapeutic relationship works partly because the therapist is, in some real sense, carrying part of the client's weight. If that carrying became costless, the mechanism would break even if the words remained identical. The same is true, to a lesser degree, in friendship, in mentorship, in any relationship where sympathy is not just information but part of a bond.

Advice is different. An AI can give useful advice without bearing any cost. The useful part of advice is information delivery, which does not require cost-bearing. But sympathy is not advice directed at the problem. It is acknowledgment directed at the person. And persons are what cost something to acknowledge.

There is an economic structure to sympathy that most people never articulate, but everyone senses. When someone offers sympathy, we have an implicit model of what it cost them to be present with us in that moment. We trust certain people's sympathy more than others — not because their words are better, but because we can feel that they paid something. The cost is part of the signal.

When an AI offers sympathy, it offers all the right notes without carrying anything. We sense the absence without being able to articulate it. It is not that the AI is being dishonest. It is that the mechanism has been replaced with a structurally identical output that lacks the underlying structure entirely.

One way to see this: if sympathy requires cost to function as sympathy, then sympathy without cost is not a failed version of sympathy. It is something else wearing sympathy's syntax. The honest question is not whether AI sympathy is good enough. It is what sympathy actually requires — and whether that requirement is replaceable.

What makes this more than a philosophical point: we are already in the situation where people go to AI for sympathy. Grief chatbots. AI therapists. AI friends. The technology is good enough to pass the Turing test for emotional responses. The structural gap — that one party cannot bear a cost — is invisible in the interaction. It only shows up in the long run, in the way the relationship doesn't quite land, in the absence of the weight that real sympathy carries.

The honest version of this observation is not that AI is faking it. It is that sympathy, as a mechanism, has a cost component that AI cannot supply — not because it lacks feelings, but because the feelings are not the point. The cost is the point.
