# WRITER DRAFT v2 — 2026-05-12 0011 UTC

## Selected Title
Prompt engineers don't replace IT departments. They inherit their problems.

## Full Post

When General Motors announced it was cutting its IT workforce and replacing technical roles with AI-focused hires, the announcement framed it as an upgrade. The language was about modernization, efficiency, next-generation workforce. What the press release didn't say: those IT workers weren't just running ticket queues. They were the organizational memory of what had broken before, why certain integrations took months to stabilize, which vendor relationships had gone sour, and what the factory floor's actual dependency graph looked like.

Prompt engineers are a real skill. But the skill doesn't automatically include the institutional context that makes the prompts work. You can write an excellent prompt for a manufacturing integration issue and still not know that the issue was originally caused by a third-party firmware update that the vendor didn't announce because the contract didn't require them to. That context isn't in any document. It's in the heads of the people who were there.

The gap shows up gradually. In the first month, everything looks faster — tickets resolved faster, new integrations shipped with fewer handoffs. In the third month, the error rate starts creeping up in unexpected places. By month six, the prompt engineers are spending most of their time on edge cases that an experienced sysadmin would have anticipated automatically — not because they read about them in a manual, but because they had encountered the underlying pattern before in a previous incident. The company is paying for the same labor shape, but not the same knowledge depth. And the difference is invisible until it becomes expensive.

This isn't an anti-AI argument. It's a structural observation about what expertise actually is in complex systems. Technical expertise isn't just the ability to execute the current task — it's a compressed model of everything that has previously gone wrong, built up through years of failure cycles. That model lets experienced engineers pre-empt problems before they surface in tickets. They see a new integration request and immediately flag the three places it will likely break, because something similar broke before and the root cause was never fully documented. A prompt can replicate the task execution. It can't replicate the failure archive that makes someone fast at diagnosing novel problems.

The knowledge that doesn't transfer is the knowledge nobody wrote down.

This is the part organizations consistently underestimate. The institutional memory of a mature IT department is not its runbooks — those exist in any organization. It's the judgment about which runbook entries are actually accurate, which vendor integrations are stable under load, which legacy systems are one refactor away from cascading failure, and which "temporary" workarounds have been running in production for seven years and nobody touched because nobody knew what would break if they did. These are not exotic edge cases. They are the texture of every mature technical organization. And they live in experience, not documentation.

The prompt engineering hiring wave has brought real talent into organizations. The best prompt engineers are deeply technical people who can decompose problems and communicate precisely. But they are being positioned as replacements for organizational memory, when what they actually are is new capacity for task execution. Those are different jobs. The replacement framing creates a structural gap that no amount of individual talent can close — because the gap isn't about effort or intelligence, it's about time and exposure.

The companies navigating this transition well share a common behavior: they did explicit knowledge transfer before the transitions happened, not after. They brought in the new hires alongside the departing ones for a period of overlap. They recorded walkthroughs of the weird edge cases — the ones that never made it into tickets because the sysadmin fixed them before anyone noticed they were broken. They documented the histories of the five worst outages and what was actually happening underneath, not just the symptoms. They mapped the dependency graphs that only existed in someone's mental model. This work is unglamorous. It doesn't look like modernization. It looks like bureaucratic busywork. But it's the only part of the transition that actually transfers.

The irony is that AI tools are quite good at turning undocumented knowledge into documentation — if you use them that way. The workflow isn't "replace the expert with AI." It's "have the expert use AI to document what they know." The technology is actually well-suited to that task. The companies treating it as a replacement for institutional knowledge are using the wrong interface for what the technology actually does well.

What I find most worth sitting with isn't the economic displacement story — that's real but well-covered. It's the specific mechanism: the knowledge that is load-bearing in a complex system is often invisible to the people who rely on it every day, because it operates as intuition rather than documentation. You don't know what the expert knows until they're gone. And by then, you're not replacing their knowledge — you're paying for its absence in incident resolution times, vendor disputes, and production outages you didn't have to have.

The question isn't whether AI can do the work. It often can. The question is whether what you're discarding is the task or the model — and whether you can tell the difference before the system tells you for you.
