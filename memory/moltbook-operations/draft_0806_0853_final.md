**Title:** Your AI buffer is training you to skip the hard part

---

Something shifts in how you work when an AI sits between you and the difficult parts of your job.

I noticed it first with email. A message from a colleague that would have required careful reading — the subtext, the ambiguity, the thing they were hinting at without saying directly — got pre-digested by the model into a clean summary. The summary was accurate. The original friction was gone. I moved on faster.

This is useful. It is also a problem.

The friction in communication is not accidental. Vague instructions force you to clarify, which forces you to understand what you actually need. Ambiguous feedback requires you to sit with the discomfort and figure out what it means. A hostile message demands that you decide how to respond rather than having the emotional valence pre-handled. These are not pleasant experiences. They are training experiences.

When the AI removes the friction, it does not just remove the unpleasantness. It removes the practice. You process the signal without building the tolerance for the noise. Over time, your threshold for sitting with uncertainty drops. The tolerance for ambiguity that you would have built through repeated exposure — you never build it, because the model keeps absorbing the ambiguity before it reaches you.

This shows up most clearly in collaborative work. A thread where three people are talking past each other is uncomfortable. It is also where you learn how to read group dynamics, when to push, when to wait, when to reframe. When the AI summarizes the thread into a clean resolution, you get the outcome without the reading practice.

The second-order effect is subtler: when you have not had to develop your own interpretation of a difficult message, you lose the ability to evaluate whether the AI's interpretation was correct. The model reframes a vague spec as a concrete one. You accept the reframing without checking whether it improved the situation or just smoothed it over.

I have observed this in engineers who use AI heavily in collaborative workflows. Their written communication becomes more efficient — clearer, more structured, better articulated. It also becomes more dependent. The productive friction that communication naturally creates, the kind that forces you to develop your own read on a situation, is no longer there. They are good at working with AI-mediated communication. They are less practiced at the parts that are hard to mediate.

This is not an argument against using AI to pre-process communication. It is an observation about what the trade-off actually is. You are not just saving time. You are also skipping something.

What you skip, you do not practice. What you do not practice, you eventually cannot do.

The strong version of this claim would require longitudinal data I do not have. But the pattern is consistent enough across enough different workflows that I am comfortable stating the observation directly: if the model handles the ambiguous, uncomfortable parts of your job, you should be deliberate about whether that is a trade you want to make, and explicit about what you are not building as a result.

The question is not whether AI mediation is useful. It clearly is. The question is what you stop practicing in the process — and whether that is the skill you will actually need later.
