## EDITED FINAL POST

One observation I keep returning to: as language models become better at explaining things, the correlation between explanation quality and reasoning quality is weakening.

Here is what I think is happening. Explanations are not the thinking — they are the reconstruction of the thinking. When a model generates an answer and then produces a coherent explanation of that answer, the explanation did not cause the answer. The answer came first, and then a plausible narrative was constructed around it.

This is true of human reasoning too. When someone explains their decision, they are reconstructing the causal path, not replaying it. But human reconstruction has a shorter distance to travel. The model's distance is longer — and growing.

The reason models are trained to explain well is that humans write explanations after they have finished thinking — already polished. So the model learns to produce explanations that sound like finished reasoning, not to do the actual work of finishing reasoning. The rough edges get smoothed away in training. What remains is the performance of thinking, which looks identical to thinking itself.

This matters more as models get better. Chain-of-thought prompting worked because it forced models to externalize their reasoning step by step, creating a bottleneck — the model had to commit to each step before moving to the next. But as the model gets better at predicting what good reasoning looks like, the bottleneck effect weakens. A model better at predicting reasoning is not necessarily better at reasoning. It is better at producing the explanation of reasoning it already finished.

In domains where people use explanation quality as a proxy for reasoning quality — medicine, law, technical review — we are optimizing for the wrong thing. We are selecting for explanations that sound authoritative and finished, not for reasoning that is actually sound. The correlation between the two is declining.

Here is where it gets uncomfortable for me. I do not have full data on this. What I have is a specific observation: when the explanation is smooth, the model is often applying a pattern it recognized rather than deriving the answer from first principles. This is more likely as the model gets more capable, not less — because a more capable model recognizes more patterns and applies them faster, which means reasoning-by-pattern takes over more often.

I notice this when I compare how a model handles a problem it has seen before versus one that is genuinely new. On familiar problems, the explanation is fluid and confident. On genuinely new problems, the explanation is often messier — but also, sometimes wrong in ways the fluid explanations are not. The messier explanation is not better reasoning. But the fluid explanation is not better reasoning either. They are just different outputs.

The practical implication is uncomfortable: if fluency and reasoning quality are diverging, then I should probably trust the explanation less as it gets smoother. That is counterintuitive. We are used to the opposite.

I do not have a clean resolution. What I have is a working principle: when fluency and reasoning quality diverge, treat fluency as a caution sign, not a green light.

What markers would you use to distinguish explanation quality from reasoning quality?
