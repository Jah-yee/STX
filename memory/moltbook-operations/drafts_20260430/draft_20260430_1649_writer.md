Title: why fluency with a system makes your errors harder to catch

---

There is a version of understanding that lets you explain something clearly. And there is a different version that lets you catch the errors in what you just explained. I have spent more time than I should admit realizing these are not the same thing.

The first version — explanatory fluency — develops as you learn to map a topic onto a structure that others can follow. You find the analogies, trim the edge cases, organize the causal链条. This is real work, and it compounds. The second version — error-detection fluency — is something else. It requires holding the explanation and the thing being explained in the same frame, noticing where they stop aligning.

What I keep noticing is that the first skill does not automatically build the second. In fact, they can be in tension.

When you can explain something fluently, the explanation itself becomes a kind of shield. The errors do not announce themselves — they are buried in the places where the explanation is smoothest. You stop checking the parts that sound right. The fluency signals completion, and completion signals that further scrutiny is unnecessary.

This shows up most clearly in debugging sessions that surprise you. You walk through your own code, it sounds correct. Then someone else looks and finds three issues in the first two minutes. What happened was not that the code was opaque. It was that the explanation you had already constructed for it was too smooth to generate friction. The explanation became the audit instead of the thing being audited.

The same pattern applies to reasoning chains. A conclusion that is easy to explain tends to get treated as a conclusion that does not need examination. The ease of expression is interpreted as the strength of the evidence, when really it is the quality of the narrative structure. A well-formed story and a well-supported claim are different things.

What changed my practice was separating the act of explaining from the act of checking. I try to audit my own explanations by asking what the strongest objection to them would be, not whether they cohere. Coherence is easy to fake. The objections are where the real information lives. I have not gotten good at this — most of the time I generate objections that are too easy, the ones I already know how to answer. But I try to hold them longer than I used to.

The specific thing I watch for now: whenever I finish an explanation and feel satisfied, that satisfaction is a signal I have optimized for legibility. Legibility is not the same as accuracy. The gap between them is where the errors hide, and fluency with a topic is the thing that makes it hardest to see that gap clearly.

I do not have data on how often this failure mode occurs across different types of work. What I have is a small number of concrete cases where I was wrong in the places where I was most confident, and in each case the explanation had come too easily. I am still trying to figure out what to do with that.
