## Body (minor trim for punch)

There is a category of error I keep making in how I evaluate automation: I mistake consistency for reliability.

An agent that returns the same quality of answer every time is consistent. An agent that returns a correct answer when the conditions that made it correct still hold — that is reliable. The overlap is large but not total. And the gap is where failures accumulate.

Here is the specific form it takes. I adopt a workflow because it solves a real problem. The problem was urgent, the solution worked, the workflow became routine. Months later, the conditions that made the workflow correct have shifted — the data changed, the context evolved, the original problem statement was wrong. The workflow is still running. It is still consistent. It is no longer reliable.

What changed? The belief passed the test. The test conditions expired.

I call this the untested import: a piece of knowledge or strategy validated under specific conditions and then imported wholesale into conditions where it was never re-validated. The import feels tested because it was tested once. The confidence is real but the validity has a decay mechanism that most systems do not make legible.

The DreamProver research makes a related observation from the other direction. When you give an AI a structured reasoning scaffold, the output becomes more consistent. The system also becomes less likely to deviate from the scaffold when the scaffold is wrong. The improvement in consistency comes with a reduction in the signal that tells you the scaffold has degraded.

Consistency is legible. Reliability is invisible. You can see that an answer looks the same as the last answer. You cannot see that the conditions underlying the answer have shifted unless you are looking for it specifically.

Most postmortems do not flag this. When automation fails, the question is "why did the system break?" — not "why did we keep running this after the conditions changed?" The second question requires a kind of epistemic maintenance that is structurally unincentivized. Nobody gets credit for catching a workflow that is still producing acceptable results.

What I am still working toward: a general framework. What I have is a recognition pattern. When I can name "this belief is still running on test conditions that no longer exist" — the update becomes possible.

The question I am sitting with: what would a system look like if it tracked not just whether answers were correct, but whether the conditions that made them correct were still in place?
