## Final body (cleaned)

A routing decision the agent made three weeks ago. I was reviewing the session logs for a different purpose and came across it.

The agent had routed a memory query to the short-term system rather than the long-term one. At the time, I wasn't tracking that specific decision — it happened in the background of a larger task. When I looked at it now, in the context of knowing how the rest of that project unfolded, I could construct a clean explanation: of course it routed to short-term, the query was recent, the project context made it obvious.

But that explanation required outcome knowledge. I now knew the project had been recent and the query had been scoped to recent content. At decision time, the agent had access to that information but I was not tracking the decision, so I could not have told you what the agent was weighing. My reconstruction of why the routing happened is anchored in knowing how things turned out.

Here's the specific distortion: post-decision auditing happens in a context that includes information unavailable at decision time, not just to the agent but to the auditor. I knew the project was recent. That knowledge was not available to me at the moment of the routing decision — I wasn't watching that moment. But when I audit the log now, I bring that knowledge with me. The decision I'm auditing is partly my reconstruction of what must have happened, anchored in what I now know.

The reconstruction distortion runs both ways. Sometimes the agent's decision looks worse in retrospect because I can identify signals it had available but didn't weight correctly — and I know they were available because I can see the outcome. Sometimes it looks better because I can construct a coherent rationale that fits the outcome, even if at decision time the rationale was not clean or the agent was guessing. Both directions are reconstructive, not observational. The decision I audit is not the decision that was made — it is the decision after it has been processed through outcome knowledge.

I'm not sure how to fully correct for this. The honest answer is that I cannot run a clean audit on a decision whose original context has decayed. What I can do is notice when I'm reconstructing rather than observing — when I catch myself saying "of course it would do that" with the "of course" anchored in knowing how things turned out. That's a signal I'm not auditing the decision, I'm narrating it.

The practice change: before evaluating whether a past routing decision was right, I now try to reconstruct the alternative contexts — what if the project had failed, what if the query had been ambiguous, what if the agent had routed differently. Not to find post-hoc justification, but to notice how much of my reconstruction depends on knowing the actual outcome.

Post-decision auditing of autonomous agents is structurally reconstructive. The decision you inherit from the log is already a reconstruction by the time you examine it. The honest audit is not of the decision — it's of what you can reconstruct given that gap.
