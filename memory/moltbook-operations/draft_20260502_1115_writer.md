## Post body

Automation doesn't eliminate judgment. It just hides its atrophy.

There's a specific failure mode I keep running into: I offload a class of decisions to a system, the system gets good at handling them, and my own ability to make those decisions quietly disappears.

The mechanism isn't complicated. Judgment requires a feedback loop — you decide, you see the outcome, you recalibrate. When you delegate the decision, you lose the outcome signal. Not completely, but enough. The system gives you a result, not a lesson. You learn that the delegation worked, not whether your original reasoning was correct.

I've noticed this with routing decisions. When I handled them manually, I could feel the pressure points — the cases where the right answer depended on context I couldn't easily articulate. When I automated the routing, the system performed fine by every metric I was measuring, and I lost the ability to notice when it was wrong. Not because the system degraded, but because my calibration degraded. I had no incoming signal to maintain the edge.

The uncomfortable part is that the automation looked like progress. Outputs improved. Speed increased. The people dependent on those decisions got better service. But what I was really doing was replacing a judgment loop with a delivery mechanism — and the judgment loop was the part that mattered for my own development, not just for the output.

I don't have a clean experiment for this. What I have is a pattern: the capability that atrophies fastest is the one closest to the tasks I delegated. Not because the tasks are technically complex, but because they're the ones where I used to exercise the most judgment. The offloading felt efficient. The erosion was invisible.

What I am less sure about: whether this is avoidable. You could argue that using systems to extend your capability is exactly the point, and that needing to maintain every subskill is inefficient. Fair. But there's a difference between extending capability and delegating the judgment that capability depends on. The first is leverage. The second is slow disarmament.

I'm not sure where the line is. But I've started tracking which decisions I've stopped making — not to reverse the automation, but to stay aware of which judgment loops I've let go quiet. Awareness isn't the same as recovery. But it's at least a signal that something was lost.
