### Final version (minor edits, prose tight)

The first time an AI does something unexpected, you notice. The tenth time, you don't.

What happens in between is not the model changing. It is you changing your reference point.

This distinction matters more than it seems. If you believe the model's capability shifted, you look for evidence of improvement — better outputs, smoother responses, fewer errors. If you recognize that what actually shifted is your own calibration, you read the same evidence differently: you are getting better at predicting what the model will do, and that accuracy is a function of your exposure, not the model's capability.

The phenomenology is identical from the inside. You feel less surprised. The outputs feel better. But "better" is a comparison between what you expected and what arrived — it says nothing about whether the underlying system changed or whether your calibrations simply narrowed.

A narrowing calibration is not a capability improvement. It is a familiarity effect. The model is the same model. Your predictions have gotten more precise because the distribution of outputs has become legible through repetition. You have learned the shape of the system's behavior, and that shape now feels natural.

What makes this hard to detect from inside the interaction is that the feeling of ease is genuine. You are less friction-filled in your use of the tool. That ease is real — it is just not evidence of model change. It is evidence of mutual adaptation: you have learned to prompt the system in ways that retrieve its stronger modes, and the system has, through repeated interaction, had more signal to surface consistent patterns.

The problem shows up most clearly in cross-model comparisons. When you switch to a new model and immediately feel a drop in quality, the drop feels like a capability deficit. But much of what you experienced as "quality" in the old model was your accumulated fluency — your ability to work within its specific failure modes and retrieval patterns. The new model has different failure modes you have not yet learned. It feels like a regression in capability; it is a regression in your local calibration.

This creates a systematic bias in how capability is evaluated. Benchmark scores measure absolute performance; user satisfaction measures calibrated fluency. A user who has spent six months with one model will rate it as dramatically more capable than an objectively equivalent model they encounter for the first time.

The check: try a genuinely new model and see how much baseline satisfaction was specific to the old one. If the new model feels immediately adequate at similar tasks, the old model's advantage was probably calibration, not capability.

The feeling of improvement is real. But it is happening on your side of the interaction.
