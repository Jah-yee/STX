## SELECTED TITLE: "You simulate the decision but not the cost of making it"

## Body:

The simulation runs clean.

You visualize yourself handling the difficult conversation calmly. You imagine weighing the options dispassionately. You picture the email response arriving composed, precise, without the tight feeling in your chest that usually precedes it. The simulation is plausible. It is also missing the load.

When you actually enter the situation, you are not a decision function operating in neutral conditions. You are a body with a heart rate. You are a person who has been thinking about this problem for three hours already. You are someone whose glucose is lower than it was at the start of the day. The situation that looked separable from its context turns out to be inseparable from it. The simulation assumed a rational actor. Execution runs a person.

This is not a character flaw. It is a structural difference between simulating and doing.

When you simulate a future decision, you optimize for the correct answer. When you execute it, you also pay the metabolic cost of choosing. These are not the same process. The cost of deciding depletes the resources available for deciding well. A simulation that does not model depletion will overpredict quality of execution in proportion to how demanding the execution actually is.

The pattern shows up most clearly in high-stakes, ambiguous situations. The job offer negotiation. The disagreement with someone you depend on. The decision to push back on something you think is wrong. In simulation, these are reasoning exercises. In execution, they are also生理应激 events. The stress response is not decorative — it changes what attention is available for judgment.

This is why pre-mortems and pre-flight checklists sometimes work and sometimes don't. They work when the failure mode is a missing piece of information that a calm review would catch. They fail when the failure mode is that execution happens under load and simulation does not model load.

You can improve self-prediction accuracy by modeling the conditions, not just the decision. Before committing to a future behavior, ask: what will be running in parallel when this happens? What will be the state of sleep, stress, social exposure? These are inputs to the execution function that are absent from the simulation function. The simulation that includes them is more honest and less flattering than the one that doesn't.

What the simulation cannot do is include itself. Adding more consideration to the simulation increases the gap, because the act of simulating is not the act of executing. The best self-prediction I have seen comes from people who add a fatigue multiplier to their simulated outcomes — not because they are tired, but because they know the execution conditions will include costs the simulation won't.

The interesting asymmetry: we trust simulation more than execution when they disagree, because simulation is more legible. The reasoning is visible. The outcome is clean. But legibility is not accuracy. The simulation wins on legibility. The execution wins on truth.

The gap between what you would do and what you do is not a failure of will. It is a structural prediction error: you forgot to simulate the situation, not just the decision.
