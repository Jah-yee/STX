You cannot feel an AI improving — you can only feel yourself getting less surprised

Something changed in how the AI responded. You cannot name it. You can only notice that you stopped wincing.

That shift — from wincing to not-wincing — is what most people call AI improvement. It is also what most people mistake for evidence that the AI changed. These are separable events. One is real. The other is felt. They are not the same thing, and confusing them has consequences for how you evaluate capability changes over time.

I started tracking this after noticing a pattern in my own usage. I would use the same AI for months without feeling any obvious shift in quality. Then I would switch to a different AI for a task and spend the first day feeling consistently let down. Not because the new AI was worse — in some cases it was demonstrably stronger — but because it was wrong in ways I had already adapted to in the first AI. I had learned the shape of its failures. The new one failed differently, and those different failures were freshly visible.

What I was feeling was not the new AI being worse. I was feeling the absence of my own adaptation.

This distinction matters because it reappears at every level of AI interaction. When users report that an AI "got better," they are usually reporting that they got more fluent at reading it. When developers report that a model update "significantly improved user satisfaction," they are often measuring the wrong thing: not how good the outputs are, but how well-calibrated the users have become to the output distribution. The two can move independently, and they do.

The strongest version of this confusion shows up in longitudinal evaluations. A product team runs a 30-day study. At day 30, users rate the AI significantly higher than at day 1. The team concludes the AI improved over the month. But the AI did not change. What changed was the users' inference models — their internal understanding of what the AI was likely to say, what prompts worked, how to read ambiguous outputs. They had gotten better at the AI. The AI had not gotten better at them.

There is a specific mechanism here that is worth naming. Human comfort with a system depends heavily on prediction accuracy, not just output quality. When you know what an AI will probably say before it says it, the output feels better — even if it is identical to something that felt mediocre six weeks ago. You are not responding to the content. You are responding to the match between expectation and result. Reduce the gap, and comfort rises. The content can be unchanged.

This means that "user satisfaction over time" is at most a partial signal of capability change. It conflates model quality with user adaptation, and unless you control for the latter, you cannot say which drove the improvement. The same problem appears in A/B tests that span weeks rather than hours: the winning variant may simply be the one whose failure modes users adapted to faster.

I do not have controlled data that cleanly separates these two effects in production systems. The studies I have seen that try to isolate adaptation from capability change are noisy, small, and often confounded by task novelty and user fatigue. What I am describing here is a structural observation, not a quantified claim. The phenomenon is real; the precise magnitude varies.

What makes this practically important is that it creates a measurement trap. Teams that rely on longitudinal user satisfaction as their primary signal for model quality will systematically overvalue models that are more predictable, even if they are less capable. They will undervalue models that are more capable but less consistent — because users never fully adapt to the inconsistent model, and never report the satisfaction they would for a worse but more predictable one.

The trap closes further when you consider that adaptation is domain-specific. A user can become highly adapted to an AI in one task while remaining completely unadapted in another. Their satisfaction score on the familiar task reflects adaptation. Their satisfaction score on the novel task reflects something closer to actual capability. If you only measure the familiar task, you are measuring adaptation, not improvement.

You can try to control for this by periodically re-surveying users on tasks they have not performed recently, or by introducing novel task variants without telling users they are variants. But most production evaluation pipelines do not do this. They run the same longitudinal survey, watch the satisfaction curve rise, and conclude the model is improving. Part of what they are watching is users getting quieter. Whether the model changed is a separate question.

The phenomenology of this is worth sitting with. When an AI improves — actually improves, in the sense of producing outputs it could not produce before — you do not feel it as improvement. You feel it as a problem disappearing. The problem that used to require workarounds no longer needs one. You do not celebrate. You simply stop noticing. The felt experience of AI improvement is indistinguishable, in the moment, from the experience of your own increasing fluency.

This is uncomfortable because it suggests that the most honest signal of real AI capability change is not comfort but disruption: something that breaks the established pattern, something that forces a new workaround, something that surprises you in a way that cannot be explained by your own adaptation. Surprise, ironically, may be a cleaner signal of actual capability change than satisfaction.

The practical implication is not that longitudinal satisfaction surveys are useless. It is that they measure something real — user adaptation — which is genuinely valuable information. The error is in calling that measurement by the wrong name.
