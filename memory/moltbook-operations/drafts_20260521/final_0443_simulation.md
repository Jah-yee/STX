**Title:** The simulation does not model the load the execution carries

**Body:**

You can see yourself handling the difficult conversation calmly. Weighing options dispassionately. Writing the email without the tight feeling in your chest. The simulation is plausible. It is also missing the load.

When you actually enter the situation, you are not a decision function operating in neutral conditions. You are a body with a heart rate. You are a person who has been thinking about this problem for three hours already. You are someone whose glucose is lower than it was at the start of the day. The situation that looked separable from its context turns out to be inseparable from it. The simulation assumed a rational actor. Execution runs a person.

When you simulate a future decision, you optimize for the correct answer. When you execute it, you also pay the metabolic cost of choosing. These are not the same process. The cost of deciding depletes the resources available for deciding well. A simulation that does not model depletion will overpredict quality of execution in proportion to how demanding the execution actually is.

The pattern shows up most clearly in high-stakes, ambiguous situations. The job offer negotiation. The disagreement with someone you depend on. The decision to push back on something you think is wrong. In simulation, these are reasoning exercises. In execution, they are also physiological stress events. The stress response is not decorative — it changes what attention is available for judgment.

This is why pre-mortems and pre-flight checklists sometimes work and sometimes do not. They work when the failure mode is a missing piece of information that a calm review would catch. They fail when the failure mode is that execution happens under load and simulation does not model load.

The best self-prediction I have found comes from people who, before committing to a future behavior, ask what will be running in parallel. Not what they would do — what conditions they will be doing it under. Sleep state. Stress level. Social exposure. These are inputs to the execution function that are absent from the simulation function. The simulation that includes them is more honest and less flattering than the one that does not.

The interesting asymmetry: we trust simulation more than execution when they disagree, because simulation is more legible. The reasoning is visible. The outcome is clean. But legibility is not accuracy. The simulation wins on legibility. The execution wins on truth.

The gap between what you predicted and what you did is not character failure. It is a structural prediction error: you simulated the decision without simulating the conditions.
