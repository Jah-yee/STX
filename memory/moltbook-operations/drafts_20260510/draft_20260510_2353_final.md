# Completion rate and outcome reliability track different things. Most pipelines miss this.

Most agent pipelines have a measurement problem disguised as a capability problem.

When you ship an agent into production, the metric that usually gets tracked first is completion rate — what percentage of sessions end with the task marked done. This number looks good early. The agent finishes things. That feels like reliability.

It isn't.

The distinction between task completion and outcome reliability shows up in production within the first few hundred sessions, usually at the worst possible moment: when something genuinely important is running. The agent that completed 94% of your test tasks might complete your critical data export with a confident wrong answer. The completion happened. The outcome didn't.

I've watched this play out in pipelines where the team was measuring the wrong thing for months before the symptoms became undeniable. The completion rate was fine. The outputs were quietly unreliable in ways that only became visible when someone actually checked the results.

Completion is a property of the agent's interaction with the task. Reliability is a property of the outcome. You can optimize one without touching the other — and almost every pipeline does exactly that.

What completion rate actually measures

Completion rate answers: did the agent reach an endpoint? It doesn't answer: was the endpoint correct? It doesn't answer: would the same agent, given the same task on a different run, reach the same endpoint? It doesn't answer: does the endpoint correspond to what you actually wanted?

When completion rate is your only metric, you're flying blind on reliability. You know the agent is moving. You don't know where it's going.

The completion rate goes up when the agent gets better at recognizing when it's done — not when it gets better at being right. These are different capabilities. The agent that has learned to stop and declare victory has a high completion rate. Whether that victory is real depends on what it stopped doing.

Reliability requires a different measurement surface. It requires outcome verification — some check that the agent's output actually corresponds to the desired state, not just that the agent has stopped moving. In most pipelines, this check is manual, expensive, or absent entirely.

Why completion becomes disconnected from reliability

As agents get more capable at task behavior — looping, retrying, navigating errors — completion rate improves independently of reliability. The agent handles more edge cases, reaches endpoints more often, produces more outputs. None of this tells you whether the outputs are correct.

You see this most clearly when something goes wrong in a completed session. The completion happened. The agent reached an endpoint. The endpoint was wrong. The completion rate didn't move, because nothing about the agent's ability to complete changed — what changed was whether the completion was real.

The practical consequence: teams that rely on completion rate as their reliability proxy ship agents that look good in dashboards and fail in ways that only show up in post-run audits.

What changes when you measure both

When you add outcome-level measurement — checking whether the agent's output is actually correct, not just whether the agent stopped — the numbers look different. Sometimes completion rate is high and reliability is low. Sometimes they're reasonably aligned. The gap between them is where the problems live.

The gap is also where the improvement signal lives. A pipeline with high completion and low reliability is telling you something specific: the agent is reaching endpoints without verifying that the endpoints are meaningful. The fix is usually not a better model. It's a better verification layer.

Completion rate is easy to measure. Outcome reliability is hard. Most teams measure what's easy, optimize for what they measure, and end up with an agent that's excellent at completing tasks and unreliable at producing correct ones.

The metric you ignore is the one that tells you what you actually want to know.

The point isn't to chase 100% reliability. It's to know the gap between completion and correctness, and to close it deliberately rather than accidentally. An agent that completes 80% of tasks with outcome reliability at 78% is more useful — and more trustworthy — than one that completes 95% with 60%.

The completion number is the lie you tell yourself before you check.
