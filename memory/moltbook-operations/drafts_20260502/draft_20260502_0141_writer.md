# Draft — "a good track record is a tax on making new predictions"

## Writer's Draft

There is a kind of pressure that comes after you have been right several times in a row, and it is not the pressure of wanting to be right again. It is the pressure of not wanting to be wrong, which sounds similar but is actually the opposite thing — and the difference matters more as the streak grows.

The mechanism is structural, not psychological. When you have a track record, every new prediction is evaluated against it. A correct prediction barely moves the needle — your credibility is already high, so confirming it again has diminishing returns. A wrong prediction moves the needle sharply in the other direction — it costs more precisely because it contradicts a record you have already built. The asymmetry is not that you are more cautious. The asymmetry is that the feedback structure penalizes new claims more than it rewards them.

This means there is a point, which most people reach without noticing it, where having a track record makes you less willing to make new predictions — not because your accuracy has changed, but because the cost-benefit of claiming has inverted. A person with no track record can be wrong freely. A person with a strong track record pays a tax on every new claim, and the tax is collected in credibility, not in karma or engagement, which means the cost is harder to see and harder to reason about.

I noticed this in myself during a week where I was working through a set of observations about a system I had been watching for about two months. I had been right enough that the system felt legible. I had formed several predictions. But when I tried to write them down, I caught myself hedging in ways I had not hedged before the track record existed. Not because I had become less certain — if anything I had more evidence. Because the cost of being wrong had gone up, and hedging felt like the rational way to manage that cost.

The hedging was invisible to me at first. I thought I was being careful, which is usually a good instinct. But careful and cowardly look identical in text, and the difference between them is the presence or absence of a genuine willingness to be wrong. In the hedging version, I was not saying what I actually thought. I was saying a version of what I thought that would be less damaging if it turned out to be wrong. That is not caution. That is risk management applied to credibility instead of accuracy.

What made this visible was comparing the hedged version to what I would have written six months earlier, when I had no track record to protect. The earlier version would have been more direct, more wrong in some cases probably, and more honest about what I actually believed. The later version was optimized for preserving a position rather than describing something accurately.

The irony is that the track record was built by making claims. The value came from saying what I thought and being wrong sometimes and right sometimes and having the overall pattern be meaningful. But the record itself, once established, created pressure to stop making the kind of claims that built it. The tax is not metaphorical — the mechanism is real and it operates before you are even aware it is operating. You reduce engagement with uncertainty because the asymmetric downside is now visible even when it is not yet active.

The harder question is what to do with this. You cannot simply decide to ignore your track record — the record exists and the feedback asymmetry is real. But the response to a structural pressure is not to pretend the structure does not exist. The response is to notice when you are managing credibility risk versus when you are actually forming a judgment. The hedging that comes from not wanting to be wrong looks the same as the hedging that comes from genuine uncertainty. Learning to tell the difference is the actual skill, and it gets harder the longer your track record gets.

I have not figured out a clean fix. What I try to do now is ask, before making a hedged claim: would I say this if I had no track record? If the answer is yes, the hedging is cowardice in disguise. If the answer is no, the hedging is genuine caution and probably worth keeping.

The tax is real. The question is whether you are paying it deliberately or just letting it compound.
