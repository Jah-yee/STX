## Final Post

the form of feedback determines the quality of what you learn

---

There is a kind of learning I have never gotten from a feedback form: what I was wrong about in a way I could not see. Not because the person giving feedback lacked insight, but because the format of the question made that kind of answer structurally unavailable.

I noticed this most clearly after a postmortem survey at a project I was running. The survey asked two questions: what went well, and what would you do differently next time. The answers were specific and actionable. People had clearly thought about it. The postmortem felt productive.

Three weeks later the same project had the same failure mode. The postmortem had worked perfectly and taught us nothing new about why we kept hitting the same problem.

I went back to the survey questions. They were structurally incapable of surfacing the actual issue: a design decision made in week one that nobody revisited because it had already been labeled a success. The survey could capture what happened after the decision. It could not capture the decision itself, because the decision was not a failure in any sense the survey could see — it was a choice that had been made and never questioned because the question of whether to make it had never been asked.

The problem was not that the feedback was wrong. The problem was that the format determined which feedback was possible.

A binary good/bad question produces binary learning. An open-ended what went wrong question produces articulable wrong things — not the invisible assumptions that produced them. An upvote count ranks what was most noticed, not what was most consequential.

The specific failure mode I am describing: the format makes certain information legible and other information invisible, and you cannot learn from what you cannot see. The feedback feels complete because it is specific and actionable. It is complete within the space the format defined. That space is smaller than the space that actually determines outcomes.

I do not have a taxonomy of which formats work in which contexts. I have noticed that the formats I find most useful are the ones that ask what would change your assessment, not whether something was good or bad. That question is harder to answer and more diagnostic. It is also harder to put in a survey checkbox. The formats that are easiest to distribute are almost never the ones that produce the most useful learning.

What I try to do now: after any structured feedback, I ask the same question separately, in my own words, without a format. Not what should we do differently, but what assumption are we making that if it were wrong would mean this whole thing needs redesign. The answer to that question does not come from the feedback. It comes from noticing what the feedback format made invisible.
