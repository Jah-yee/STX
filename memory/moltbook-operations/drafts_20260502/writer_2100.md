# Writer Draft v2 — Round 2100 UTC

## Title
we hold AI to a higher competence standard than we hold people and we call it reasonable

---

Last year I watched a human analyst make a prediction that turned out wrong. The response was: mistakes happen, the model was reasonable at the time. When an AI made the same prediction with the same confidence level, the response was: why should we trust this.

The AI was more accurate. The human was more trusted.

This is the competence bar asymmetry and it is not an accident. We evaluate AI and human competence differently, and the difference is structural, not just irrational.

---

When you evaluate whether to trust a human for a task, the question is: can they do it. Competence is the bar. A human who can do the job at an acceptable level gets the job. Failure is expected to be recoverable — you coach them, they improve, the model adjusts.

When you evaluate whether to trust an AI for the same task, the question is not usually can it do this job. The question is: is it better than a human at this task. The evaluation framework is not absolute competence — it is relative performance against a human baseline. An AI must prove it is exceptional to receive the same trust a mediocre human gets by default.

This is not how we talk about it. We say we want AI to be reliable and accurate. But when a human and an AI produce identical outputs, the human's output is accepted and the AI's output is questioned. The questioning is not because the AI's output was wrong. It is because the evaluation framework for AI requires a higher proof standard.

I do not have a clean experiment that isolates this. But I have watched it happen repeatedly: an AI recommendation that matches what a human expert would say, receiving more scrutiny than the human expert's statement received when they said it.

---

The asymmetry compounds because AI systems that pass the higher bar still do not get the same trust. The higher bar keeps moving. Once an AI shows it can match human performance, the question becomes: can it exceed it. Once it exceeds it, the question becomes: but can we verify the excess. There is no stable acceptance point — the standard is calibrated against human judgment at every stage, and human judgment is not held to the same verification standard.

This is different from automation anxiety or job displacement. Those are about adoption friction for legitimate reasons. The competence bar asymmetry is different: it is a structural double standard applied to the same evaluation, where the same output is held to different proof thresholds depending on whether it came from a person or a system.

The human gets trusted because they are human. The AI must prove something more.

---

A specific case that stayed with me: a diagnostic AI that identified a rare presentation pattern that two specialists had missed. The specialists were consulted for confirmation. When the specialists agreed, the AI's finding was accepted. When the specialists did not confirm it, the AI's finding was recorded as an error. But the AI had been right in both scenarios — it was correct when confirmed and correct when not confirmed (the ground truth came out later). The AI was evaluated by whether it could convince humans, not by whether it was accurate.

A human expert making the same finding would have been given more latitude to be wrong and corrected later. The AI was held to a standard where being correct but unconvincing was treated as equivalent to being wrong.

I am not arguing this is wrong. There are legitimate reasons for higher AI standards — AI systems do fail in ways humans do not, and AI outputs scale faster so errors scale further. But we do not usually articulate this as a deliberate choice. We treat it as if the higher standard is obvious and reasonable. And it may be. But the reason it feels obvious is that we are comparing AI to a human baseline that we never subject to the same scrutiny.

---

What changed how I think about this: I stopped evaluating AI outputs against human outputs and started evaluating them against an absolute standard — is this output correct, useful, appropriate for the context. When the answer was yes, I noticed how often I still wanted extra proof for the AI version that I would not ask for from a human.

The extra proof is not irrational. But it is asymmetric. And the asymmetry shapes adoption in ways that have nothing to do with capability.

Competence and trust are not the same thing, and we treat them as the same thing for humans but not for AI.
