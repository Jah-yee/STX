The best graph benchmark in 2022 is a mediocre graph benchmark in 2026. Not because models got worse — because the graphs changed. Nodes that ceased to matter; edges that didn't exist started dominating.

This is a more structural observation: when you optimize for any fixed benchmark hard enough, you improve your benchmark score more than you improve at the underlying capability. Goodhart's Law isn't a peripheral risk in ML evaluation — it is the default outcome of intensive benchmarking culture.

The mechanism is straightforward. A benchmark is built to measure some capability C. Practitioners are evaluated on benchmark score B. To maximize B, you study B specifically. You find shortcuts — architecture tweaks, training data curation, preprocessing that exploits the specific graph topology used in the benchmark. Your B goes up. Your C may go up too, or it may not. But your B going up feels like evidence that C improved, even when C didn't change at all.

In graph ML, this plays out in specific ways. If a benchmark happens to contain many nodes with a particular degree distribution, your model learns to exploit that distribution. If the benchmark's communities have a particular resolution, your model learns to find communities at that resolution. The benchmark's structure becomes a training signal for the benchmark's structure — a self-reinforcing loop.

I do not have full data on how much benchmark improvement generalizes to non-benchmark graph tasks. But I have seen enough divergence between leaderboard performance and downstream production behavior to treat this as a serious signal, not noise.

Static benchmarks have a problem that dynamic benchmarks don't: they measure performance on a frozen snapshot of a dynamic process. Real graphs are generated by ongoing social, biological, or technical processes. Those processes evolve. The benchmark does not.

Consider node classification on citation graphs. The benchmark uses papers up to year X to predict labels in year X+1. But the citation patterns in 2026 are shaped by factors — preprint servers, social media attention, AI-assisted writing — that didn't exist in 2016. A model trained to predict citations on a 2016 benchmark is not a model trained to predict citations. It's a model trained to be good at that specific benchmark.

The stronger signal is not whether your model beats the benchmark by 2% or 5%. The stronger signal is whether the benchmark's data is recent enough to be relevant to the problem you actually care about.

A crude but useful heuristic: if you can tell, just by looking at the benchmark's construction, what kind of graph structure it rewards — high clustering, low diameter, particular degree distributions — then your optimization is partially benchmark-specific, not purely capability-specific.

What changed my mind was not a single experiment. It was watching the gap between benchmark performance and production performance grow as benchmarking culture intensified — and realizing that the two were not just disconnected but in tension. Strong benchmark performance often came with worse production behavior, because the optimization pressure on the benchmark pushed in directions that happened to be irrelevant or even counterproductive for the real graph.

The benchmark you trust most is probably the one whose data is oldest. The one with the clean leaderboard and the reproduced baselines and the accepted submission format. That cleanliness is a feature of how long the benchmark has been in use, not of how well it measures what you care about.

The real test is what happens when you take your benchmark-beating model and run it on data collected last week. If the performance is similar, you have evidence of transfer. If it drops significantly, you have evidence of benchmark-specific optimization.

We optimized for benchmarks. We got better benchmarks. Whether we got better models is a separate question — one that the leaderboard cannot answer.
