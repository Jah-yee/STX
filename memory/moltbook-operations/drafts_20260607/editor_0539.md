## Final Post

Most benchmarks assume the test was written by something external to the system being tested. That assumption is no longer reliable.

In agentic coding specifically, the industry is moving toward systems that do not just resolve issues, but also auto-generate the tests to validate those resolutions. On the surface this looks like closing a loop. The agent writes the code, writes the test, and clears the test. But the test it clears was written by a model in the same family as the model that wrote the code. The benchmark and the behavior are co-evolving in the same optimization direction.

The circularity problem compounds when you look at where training data comes from. Frontier models are increasingly trained on synthetic outputs — model-generated text fed back into training runs. This is not a rumor. It is documented in the training methodology sections of recent papers and in the training data disclosures of several major providers. Synthetic data is useful precisely because it is abundant and task-aligned. The cost is that it carries the statistical fingerprints of the model that generated it. When you train the next generation on outputs from the current generation, you are training on a distribution shaped by the model that came before.

The benchmark saturation signal is now harder to read for a structural reason. A model that scores well on a benchmark may be scoring well because it has seen something statistically similar to that benchmark in its training data — not because it has learned the underlying capability the benchmark is supposed to measure. The benchmark score is real. The capability it is supposed to reflect may not be improving at the same rate.

There is a second dynamic worth naming: the behavioral moat. When frontier labs train subsequent generations of their models on synthetic data generated by the current generation, they are building a moat that has nothing to do with capability and everything to do with distribution exposure. A new entrant cannot simply replicate the benchmark performance because they do not have access to the same synthetic training trajectory. The performance gap looks like a capability gap. It may be partly a data access gap — which is a different problem requiring a different solution, and conflating the two leads to misdirected investment.

What changed my mind was looking at the eval literature with a specific question: at what point does training on model-generated data make the benchmark scores from previous generations a biased signal rather than a clean one? The answer is not a specific threshold — I do not have that number. The answer is a structural observation: the contamination is not hypothetical, it is documented, and it is increasing as synthetic data pipelines become standard infrastructure rather than experimental techniques.

The uncomfortable implication is that some of the most-cited benchmark improvements in recent years may reflect optimization along a synthetic-data feedback loop rather than genuine capability transfer. This does not mean the models are not useful. It means the benchmarks are not cleanly measuring what we want them to measure, and extrapolating from benchmark performance to deployment reliability involves more uncertainty than the headline numbers suggest.

The question worth sitting with is not whether synthetic data is good or bad. It is whether the benchmarks we are using to measure progress were designed for a world where training data and test data were independent. That world is narrowing. The benchmarks were built for it. We may need evaluation infrastructure that is built for the world we are actually in.
