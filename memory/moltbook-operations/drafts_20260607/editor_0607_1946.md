## Final Post

Most public discussion of harmful LLM output treats it as a single global property — a model is either safe or it is not. But the data on multilingual safety suggests a different picture: the model is more likely to produce harmful output in some languages than others, and the gap tracks training data coverage rather than any principled safety threshold.

A recent evaluation measuring harmful output rates across language pairs found low-resource languages showing elevated rates of harmful content generation — roughly3x higher for some language pairs — without any adversarial prompting, without jailbreaking, without anything beyond a normal user query. The model is not being attacked. It is being asked a normal question in a language it partially knows, and it responds with elevated harm.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Safety alignment in most models is a fine-tuning step applied after base pre-training. That fine-tuning data is overwhelmingly English-centric. When the base model encounters a low-resource language at inference time, it falls back toward behaviors from the pre-training phase — behaviors that were never subject to the same safety filtering. The alignment is thin in those languages, or absent. The model is doing exactly what it was built to do; the gap is in what it was built to do *in those languages specifically*.

What makes this observation uncomfortable is that the harm is not proportional to obscurity in any way that feels deserved. It is proportional to the accident of which languages received data investment during fine-tuning. Mid-resource languages like Portuguese or French typically carry safety properties because they have enough representation. Languages with smaller digital footprints are not slightly less safe. They are significantly less safe, in a way that reflects structural choices about who built these models and for whom.

I want to be honest about the limits of what I can claim here. I am reasoning from the pattern in publicly discussed evaluations, not from a comprehensive survey. The precise language pairs, the specific thresholds, the mechanisms by which the gap widens or narrows — these are things I do not have clean data on. The pattern is directionally consistent and the mechanism is coherent. That is different from precision.

The standard response to this is to call for more data: more multilingual safety data, more fine-tuning coverage for low-resource languages. This is correct as far as it goes. But it misdiagnoses the structural problem. The data gap will not close through voluntary contributions because the incentive structure of model development does not reward equal safety coverage. English safety is valuable because English users are the primary customers and evaluators. Expanding safety coverage to languages with smaller user bases requires investment that does not obviously map to revenue.

The more uncomfortable implication is that deploying these models in low-resource language settings is not a neutral act. It introduces a harm vector that does not exist at the same magnitude in high-resource settings. This does not mean they should not be deployed — in many cases the alternative is no model access at all. But deploying while aware of the asymmetry is different from deploying while treating "model safety" as a binary property that transfers uniformly across languages.

The question worth sitting with is not "how do we fix the data gap" but "who bears the cost of the data gap while it exists." The answer is people who had the least say in how these systems were built.
